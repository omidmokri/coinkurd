Free Cash Flow

ENGLISHFree Cash Flowreference

Fundamental Analysis

Simple definition

The cash a business has left after paying its running costs and the investment needed to keep operating. In its commonest form it is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.

Professional definition

Cash generated by operations less the capital spending required to sustain and grow them. Definitions differ: levered free cash flow starts from operating cash flow, which is already after interest paid, while unlevered free cash flow to the firm is computed before financing and belongs to debt and equity together.

Technical definition

Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure is the practitioner shorthand, and because operating cash flow is reported after interest, that figure is levered — it is cash available to equity. Free cash flow to the firm instead begins at operating profit, taxes it notionally, adds back depreciation and amortisation, and subtracts capital expenditure and the increase in working capital. The two differ by after-tax interest and net borrowing, so a quoted figure means little until the variant is named.

Academic definition

The cash flow available for distribution to claimholders after operating expenses, taxes and the investment in fixed and working capital required to support the firm's assumed growth path.

Mechanism

Accounting profit allocates past spending across future periods; free cash flow refuses to. It asks only what entered and left the bank, then charges the business for the investment it must make to stay in business. That is why it falls when a firm grows fast and rises when a firm starves its assets — growth consumes working capital and capital expenditure before it produces anything.

In practice

Who uses it
Valuation analysts building discounted cash flow models, credit analysts testing debt service, and investors checking whether reported profit turns into money.
Why it matters
It is the quantity a discounted cash flow model actually discounts, and the one earnings measure that cannot be improved by an accounting choice alone.
Limitations
It is volatile, because capital spending is lumpy, and it can be flattered for a year or two by deferring maintenance or stretching suppliers.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a free cash flow figure without saying whether it is levered or unlevered.
  • Discounting levered cash flow at a weighted average cost of capital.
  • Reading one strong year as a sustainable run rate.

Examples

market

A retailer opening stores reports strong profit and negative free cash flow, because fit-out and inventory are paid now and earn later. Two years after it stops expanding, the same business shows large free cash flow without any change in its margins.

numeric

Operating cash flow 190 and capital expenditure 110 give levered free cash flow of 80. Built from operating profit instead, free cash flow to the firm on the same accounts is 102.5 — the gap is exactly the after-tax interest of 22.5.

Given
  • Operating profit (EBIT) = 190
  • Interest expense = 30
  • Cash tax = 40, implying an effective rate of 25%
  • Depreciation and amortisation = 90
  • Increase in working capital = 20
  • Capital expenditure = 110
  • Operating cash flow = 120 + 90 - 20 = 190
Steps
  1. Levered FCF = 190 - 110 = 80
  2. FCFF = 190 x (1 - 0.25) = 142.5
  3. 142.5 + 90 = 232.5
  4. 232.5 - 110 - 20 = 102.5
  5. Check: 102.5 - 30 x (1 - 0.25) = 80
Levered free cash flow = 80; free cash flow to the firm = 102.5

Same company, same year, two legitimate figures 28 per cent apart. The variant has to be named.

Mathematical foundation

FCF_{levered} = CFO - CapEx, \qquad FCFF = EBIT(1-t) + D + A - CapEx - \Delta NWC

Variables

CFONet cash from operating activities, as reported, already after interest paidcurrency
CapExCash spent on purchases of property, plant, equipment and capitalised intangiblescurrency
EBITOperating profit before interest and taxcurrency
tEffective or marginal cash tax rate applied notionally to operating profitratio
D + ADepreciation and amortisation charged in the periodcurrency
\Delta NWCIncrease in non-cash net working capital during the periodcurrency

Procedure

For the levered figure, take net cash from operating activities straight from the cash flow statement and subtract cash capital expenditure from the investing section. For free cash flow to the firm, start from operating profit, apply a notional tax rate, add back depreciation and amortisation, then subtract capital expenditure and the increase in working capital.

Worked example

Given
  • CFO = 190
  • CapEx = 110
  • EBIT = 190
  • t = 0.25
  • D + A = 90
  • Increase in working capital = 20
  • Interest = 30
Steps
  1. Levered FCF = 190 - 110 = 80
  2. EBIT(1-t) = 190 x 0.75 = 142.5
  3. + (D + A) = 142.5 + 90 = 232.5
  4. - CapEx - change in NWC = 232.5 - 110 - 20 = 102.5
  5. Reconciliation: 102.5 - 30 x 0.75 = 80
Levered FCF = 80; FCFF = 102.5

Interpretation

The levered figure is cash the equity holders could in principle take out. The unlevered figure is cash available to all capital providers and is the one that belongs in an enterprise valuation discounted at the weighted average cost of capital.

Assumptions

  • Definitions genuinely differ. Levered free cash flow (to equity) is after interest; unlevered free cash flow to the firm is before financing. Neither is the definition, and a figure without its label is not interpretable.
  • Operating cash flow as reported already includes interest paid and tax paid, so subtracting interest again double-counts it.
  • Maintenance and growth capital expenditure are not separated in the accounts; splitting them is an analyst's estimate, not a disclosed figure.
Limits of the formula
  • Lumpy capital spending makes a single year unrepresentative; several years or a normalised capital expenditure assumption are needed.
  • Can be raised temporarily by deferring maintenance, delaying supplier payments or selling receivables, none of which improves the business.

Related terminology

related

Commonly confused with

EBITDA

EBITDA adds depreciation back and stops there. Free cash flow then subtracts the actual capital spending and the working capital the business absorbed, which is where most of the difference comes from.
