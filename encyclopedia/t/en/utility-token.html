Simple definition

A token whose stated purpose is to be used for something inside a service — paying fees, buying access, redeeming a function — rather than to represent a share of a business.

Professional definition

A token class defined by intended function: it gives access to goods, services or capacity provided by the issuer or its network. Under MiCA a utility token is a crypto-asset intended only to provide digital access to a good or service, available only from the issuer.

Technical definition

The label is a regulatory and marketing category, not a technical property: on-chain, a utility token and a token representing investment rights can be byte-identical ERC-20 contracts. Classification turns on economic substance — what buyers were promised, whether returns depend on the efforts of a central team, whether a secondary market was fostered — and the resulting treatment differs by jurisdiction. Calling a token a utility token in its documentation does not change how a regulator will characterise it.

Academic definition

A category of crypto-asset distinguished by consumptive or access-granting function rather than by claims on income or governance, whose legal classification is determined by substance under the applicable national or regional framework.

Mechanism

The classification works backwards from substance to label, which is why the category is unstable at the edges. If buyers were led to expect a return generated by a central team's work, the economic facts look like an investment whatever the wording says; if the token only buys capacity that already exists, they do not. Features that attract speculators also pull a token towards investment treatment.

In practice

Who uses it
Issuers drafting offering documents, exchanges deciding listings, compliance teams in each jurisdiction, and buyers reading what a token actually entitles them to.
Why it matters
The class a token falls into determines disclosure duties, marketing limits, who may sell it and to whom, so it changes the asset's whole legal surface.
Limitations
This entry describes classification frameworks, not any particular asset's status. Classification is jurisdiction-specific, decided by authorities and courts, and it changes.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the issuer's own label as a legal classification.
  • Assuming a class assigned in one jurisdiction applies everywhere.
  • Reading a utility label as evidence the token has demand.

Examples

comparison

A prepaid credit that buys storage on a network already running looks like consumption. A pre-sold claim on a network that does not yet exist, marketed on the team's roadmap, looks like funding a venture. The same contract code can carry either story.

regulatory

MiCA treats a utility token as a crypto-asset intended only to give digital access to a good or service supplied by its issuer, and sets disclosure duties for offers to the public in the EU. Other jurisdictions apply their own tests to the same token.

These are not two technical designs but two legal characterisations, and the same token can be read differently in different countries. Nothing in a contract's code settles which label applies.
