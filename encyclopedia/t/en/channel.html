Foundation

Simple definition

Two parallel lines that contain price: one along the lows, one along the highs. Price travels between them.

Professional definition

A pair of parallel boundaries fitted to a trending or sideways series, usually a trendline plus a line parallel to it through the opposing extremes. It gives a slope and a width, so one drawing supplies both a trend reference and the band in which price has recently stayed.

Technical definition

A channel is two numbers, a slope and a width, both fitted by hand or by regression to a chosen window. Nothing enforces parallelism, so the second line is a convention imposed on the data, and changing the window changes both numbers. A channel is descriptive: it reports where price has traded relative to a fitted line, and says nothing about whether the boundaries will hold.

Academic definition

A pair of parallel boundaries fitted to a price series, defined by a slope and a constant separation, describing recent containment of price about a linear trend.

Mechanism

Price that advances at a roughly steady rate traces a band whose width reflects the swing amplitude around that rate. The two lines are not forces; they summarise where previous swings turned. They hold while both the rate of advance and the swing size persist, and both change when volatility changes — which is why channels widen and fail together.

In practice

Who uses it
Trend traders framing entries and exits, and anyone who wants a visual reference for how far price has stretched from its trend.
Why it matters
One drawing gives trend direction, recent swing amplitude and two reference levels, which is enough to plan an entry and an invalidation.
Limitations
The fit depends on the window chosen, the parallel line is imposed rather than measured, and the channel describes the past without constraining the next swing.

Common mistakes

  • Extending a channel far beyond the window it was fitted to.
  • Redrawing a boundary every time price exceeds it.
  • Reading channel width as a volatility measure without computing one.

Examples

chart

A rising market turns at successively higher lows. A line through those lows, copied upward to touch the highs, frames the move: direction from the slope, stretch from the distance to the upper line.

numeric

The lower line runs through 100 at bar zero and 112 at bar twelve, so it rises one point per bar. Copied eight points higher it tracks the highs. At bar twenty the channel runs from 120 to 128.

Given
  • lower line through (bar 0, 100) and (bar 12, 112)
  • highest high sits 8 points above the lower line
  • projection to bar 20
Steps
  1. slope = (112 - 100) / 12 = 1.0 per bar
  2. lower line at bar 20 = 100 + 20 x 1.0 = 120
  3. upper line = 120 + 8 = 128
  4. width = 8 points, which is 6.7% of 120
At bar 20 the channel runs from 120 to 128, a width of 8 points.

The projection is arithmetic on a fitted line. Whether price respects it at bar 20 is not something the fit can answer.

Commonly confused with

Keltner Channel

A drawn channel is geometry fitted to chosen pivots. A Keltner channel is computed: its centre is a moving average and its width is a multiple of average true range.
