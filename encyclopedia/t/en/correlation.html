Simple definition

A number between -1 and +1 saying how closely two things have moved together. Zero means no straight-line relationship, not that they are unrelated.

Professional definition

The covariance of two return series divided by the product of their standard deviations, giving a scale-free measure of linear association bounded between -1 and +1. It is an estimate over a chosen window and changes as that window moves.

Technical definition

Pearson's coefficient measures only the linear component of dependence. A perfectly deterministic non-linear relationship can give a correlation near zero, and the statistic is sensitive to outliers and to sampling frequency, so daily and weekly correlations of the same pair differ. It is also unstable: correlations among risk assets rise towards one in a sell-off, exactly when a portfolio is relying on them being low.

Academic definition

The normalised cross-moment of two random variables, equal to their covariance divided by the product of their standard deviations, bounded on [-1, +1] and measuring linear association only.

Mechanism

Correlation rises in a crisis because a common factor starts to dominate. When funding tightens and investors sell whatever can be sold, idiosyncratic drivers are swamped by one shared driver, liquidity. Diversification is therefore weakest precisely when it is most needed, and a portfolio built on a calm-period correlation matrix is more concentrated than it looks.

In practice

Who uses it
Portfolio builders, risk managers aggregating exposure, pairs and basis traders, and anyone claiming a set of positions is diversified.
Why it matters
It is the input that decides whether two positions are one bet or two, and therefore how much risk a portfolio actually carries.
Limitations
Linear, window-dependent, unstable, and silent on causation. A high reading can come from a shared third driver and can vanish or invert without notice.

Common mistakes

  • Reading correlation as causation.
  • Spreading capital across assets that share one factor and calling it diversification.
  • Sizing risk through a crisis with a calm-period correlation matrix.

Examples

market

Through a calm quarter two holdings show a correlation near 0.3 and the book looks diversified. In a sharp liquidation both fall together and the realised correlation over those days is above 0.9. The diversification was a property of the quiet period, not of the assets.

numeric

Two daily return series have a covariance of 0.00036. Their standard deviations are 3.0% and 2.0%, whose product is 0.00060. The correlation is 0.60, so a linear fit explains 36% of one series' variance.

Given
  • covariance of the two daily return series = 0.00036
  • standard deviation of A = 0.030
  • standard deviation of B = 0.020
  • same window, same sampling frequency, timestamps aligned
Steps
  1. product of standard deviations = 0.030 x 0.020 = 0.00060
  2. r = 0.00036 / 0.00060 = 0.60
  3. r squared = 0.36
r = 0.60, r squared = 0.36

An r of 0.60 means a linear fit accounts for 36% of the variance of one series. It says nothing about which series drives the other, nor whether the relationship will hold next month.

Mathematical foundation

r_{XY} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{s_X s_Y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\ \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}

Procedure

Align the two series on timestamps so that every pair is contemporaneous. Compute each series' mean and its deviations. Multiply the paired deviations and sum them to get the covariance numerator, then divide by the product of the two root-sum-of-squares terms, which normalises the result onto the interval from minus one to plus one.

Worked example

Given
  • Cov(X,Y) = 0.00036
  • s_X = 0.030
  • s_Y = 0.020
Steps
  1. s_X x s_Y = 0.00060
  2. r = 0.00036 / 0.00060
r = 0.60

Interpretation

Plus one is an exact positive straight-line relationship, minus one an exact negative one, and zero no linear relationship at all. The square of the coefficient is the share of variance a linear fit explains. Nothing in the statistic indicates direction of causation.

Assumptions

  • Pairs must be contemporaneous and equally spaced. A 24/7 crypto series paired with a five-day equity series, or two assets trading in different sessions, must have their timestamps aligned before the statistic means anything.
  • Only linear dependence is captured. Rank-based measures such as Spearman's coefficient measure monotone dependence instead and give different answers on the same data.
  • The estimate is conditional on the window and the sampling frequency. Both must be reported alongside the number, because the same pair gives different coefficients at different frequencies.
  • It is a descriptive statistic. It carries no direction of causation and no assumption that the relationship will persist out of sample.
  • Returns, not price levels, should be used. Correlating two trending price series produces a high coefficient that reflects the trends rather than any co-movement.
Limits of the formula
  • Highly sensitive to outliers: a handful of extreme days can set the coefficient for a long window.
  • Correlations among risk assets are not stable and tend to rise towards one in a crisis, so a historical matrix understates the risk of a stressed portfolio -- the case where the estimate matters most is the case where it is least reliable.
  • A coefficient near zero rules out a linear relationship, not a relationship. Strong non-linear dependence is invisible to it.
