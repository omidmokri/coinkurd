Simple definition

How a pool of capital is split between different kinds of holdings, written as shares of the total. The split is a decision about risk, not a prediction about prices.

Professional definition

The set of portfolio weights across asset classes, chosen as a policy and then maintained by rebalancing. Because class returns are imperfectly correlated, portfolio volatility is generally lower than the weighted average of the parts, and the size of that reduction is what allocation is for.

Technical definition

A weight vector summing to one over a defined opportunity set. Portfolio variance is a quadratic form in those weights and the covariance matrix, so the cross terms, not the individual variances, carry the diversification effect. Correlations are unstable and tend toward one in stress, which is when the variance reduction the policy was built on is smallest.

Academic definition

The specification of portfolio weights over asset classes, whose risk follows from the quadratic form of the weight vector with the covariance matrix of class returns.

Mechanism

Allocation works through the cross terms of portfolio variance. Two holdings that do not move together partly cancel each other's fluctuations, so combined volatility falls below the weighted average while the combined expected return stays exactly the weighted average. That asymmetry is the whole mechanism, and it weakens precisely as correlations rise.

In practice

Who uses it
Fund trustees, multi-asset managers, treasury committees, and anyone writing an investment policy statement rather than a trade plan.
Why it matters
It makes risk a stated policy rather than an accident of the last few decisions, and it is the level at which rebalancing rules can be defined and audited.
Limitations
It relies on estimates of covariance that change over time and compress in crises. Reported asset classes can also share hidden exposures to the same factor.

Common mistakes

  • Treating historical correlations as fixed inputs.
  • Counting many instruments as diversification when they share one driver.
  • Letting weights drift and calling the result a policy.

Examples

market

A book split across equities, bonds and a crypto sleeve looks diversified on paper. In a liquidity shock all three fall together as holders sell what can be sold, and the measured correlations that justified the split rise toward one.

numeric

Sixty percent in an asset with ten percent volatility and forty percent in one with thirty percent, correlated at 0.2, gives portfolio volatility near 14.5 percent. The weighted average of the parts would have been 18 percent.

Given
  • w1 = 0.60, sigma1 = 0.10
  • w2 = 0.40, sigma2 = 0.30
  • correlation rho = 0.2
  • volatilities are annual and measured over the same window
Steps
  1. w1^2 sigma1^2 = 0.36 x 0.01 = 0.0036
  2. w2^2 sigma2^2 = 0.16 x 0.09 = 0.0144
  3. 2 w1 w2 rho sigma1 sigma2 = 2 x 0.60 x 0.40 x 0.2 x 0.10 x 0.30 = 0.00288
  4. portfolio variance = 0.0036 + 0.0144 + 0.00288 = 0.02088
  5. portfolio volatility = sqrt(0.02088) = 0.1445
  6. weighted average volatility = 0.60 x 0.10 + 0.40 x 0.30 = 0.18
portfolio volatility 14.45 percent against a weighted average of 18 percent

The 3.55 point gap is the whole diversification effect, and it comes entirely from the cross term. At a correlation of 1.0 the cross term grows until portfolio volatility equals the 18 percent weighted average.

Mathematical foundation

r_p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i r_i \quad \text{with} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i = 1, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho_{12} \sigma_1 \sigma_2

Variables

w_iWeight of asset class i as a share of total portfolio valuefraction
r_iReturn of asset class i over the periodfraction per period
r_pPortfolio return: exactly the weighted average of the partsfraction per period
\sigma_iVolatility, the standard deviation of returns of class ifraction per period
\rho_{12}Correlation between the returns of the two classesratio between -1 and 1
\sigma_pPortfolio volatility: generally below the weighted average of the partsfraction per period

Procedure

Express each holding as a share of total value and check the shares sum to one. Portfolio return is the weighted sum of class returns. For risk with two classes, add each squared weight times its squared volatility, then add twice the product of both weights, the correlation and both volatilities, and take the square root. With more classes, use the full covariance matrix.

Worked example

Given
  • w1 = 0.60 with sigma1 = 0.10
  • w2 = 0.40 with sigma2 = 0.30
  • rho = 0.2
Steps
  1. term 1 = 0.60^2 x 0.10^2 = 0.0036
  2. term 2 = 0.40^2 x 0.30^2 = 0.0144
  3. cross term = 2 x 0.60 x 0.40 x 0.2 x 0.10 x 0.30 = 0.00288
  4. variance = 0.02088
  5. sigma_p = sqrt(0.02088) = 0.1445
  6. weighted average sigma = 0.18
sigma_p = 14.45 percent against a weighted average of 18.00 percent

Interpretation

Return aggregates linearly and risk does not: that single fact is why a mix can be less volatile than its parts while earning their average. The gap is a function of correlation, so the benefit is only as reliable as the correlation estimate.

Assumptions

  • Weights are measured at a point in time and drift as prices move; the formula describes the portfolio as weighted, not as it was last rebalanced.
  • Volatilities and the correlation are estimated over a stated window and in the same frequency for both classes.
  • The variance form is exact for two classes; with more, every pairwise covariance enters and ignoring any of them understates risk.
  • No leverage, no cash flows during the period, and returns measured in one currency.
Limits of the formula
  • Correlations are not stable parameters. They rise in stressed markets, so the measured diversification benefit is smallest when it is most needed.
  • Variance is a symmetric measure and treats upside and downside dispersion alike, so an allocation optimised on it can still carry severe left-tail exposure.

Commonly confused with

Exchange-Traded Fund

An allocation is a policy about weights; an exchange-traded fund is one instrument that can be used to express a weight. Holding several funds that track the same exposure changes nothing about the allocation.
