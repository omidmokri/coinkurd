Commodity Channel Index

Simple definition

A reading of how far the current typical price sits from its recent average, measured in units of its own average deviation.

Professional definition

An unbounded oscillator. It takes typical price — the mean of high, low and close — subtracts a simple moving average of that series, and divides by the mean absolute deviation of the same window multiplied by 0.015. Readings beyond plus or minus 100 are conventionally called extended.

Technical definition

The denominator is mean absolute deviation, not standard deviation, which makes CCI a robust standardisation: a single outlier moves it less than it would move a z-score. The 0.015 constant is arbitrary. It was chosen so that roughly 70 to 80 per cent of readings fall inside plus or minus 100, which is the only reason those levels look natural. The output is unbounded, and readings from different lookbacks or inputs do not share a scale.

Academic definition

A scaled standardisation of typical price relative to its moving average, normalised by mean absolute deviation and multiplied by a fixed constant chosen to place a conventional proportion of observations within a nominal band.

Mechanism

The construction is a distance divided by a typical distance, so the output answers one question: how unusual is the current deviation by the standard of the last n periods. Because the yardstick is itself an average over the same window, a market that has been quiet makes a modest absolute move look extreme, and a market that has been wild makes the same move look ordinary.

In practice

Who uses it
Oscillator traders screening for stretched conditions, and developers who want a deviation measure less sensitive to outliers than a z-score.
Why it matters
It normalises distance from a mean by that market's own recent dispersion, so one threshold can screen instruments on very different price scales.
Limitations
The 0.015 constant and the plus or minus 100 levels are conventions, the series is unbounded so an extreme can keep extending, and values are comparable only within one fixed lookback.

Common mistakes

  • Treating plus or minus 100 as a statistical boundary rather than a convention.
  • Comparing CCI(20) with CCI(50) as if they shared a scale.
  • Selling every reading above 100 in a strong trend.

Examples

market

In a strong advance CCI can hold above 100 for weeks, exactly as RSI holds above 70. Because CCI has no upper bound, the reading keeps climbing while the band it exceeded stays at 100.

numeric

Typical price is 102 against a twenty-period average of 100, with a mean absolute deviation of 1.5. CCI comes out near 89. Had the deviation been 1.0, the same two-point gap would read above 133.

Given
  • typical price TP = 102
  • SMA(20) of TP = 100
  • mean absolute deviation over the same 20 periods = 1.5
  • constant = 0.015
Steps
  1. numerator = 102 - 100 = 2
  2. denominator = 0.015 x 1.5 = 0.0225
  3. CCI = 2 / 0.0225 = 88.9
  4. with MD = 1.0: denominator = 0.015, CCI = 2 / 0.015 = 133.3
CCI is about 88.9, just inside the conventional band; the same price gap reads 133.3 in a quieter market.

The reading is a statement about the current deviation relative to recent deviations, not about how far price is from fair value.

Mathematical foundation

CCI_n = \frac{TP_t - SMA_n(TP)}{0.015 \times MD_n}, \qquad TP_t = \frac{H_t + L_t + C_t}{3}, \qquad MD_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left| TP_i - SMA_n(TP) \right|

Procedure

Compute typical price for each bar as the mean of high, low and close. Take its simple moving average over n periods. For the same window, average the absolute distances between each typical price and that moving average to get the mean absolute deviation. Divide the current gap by 0.015 times that deviation.

Worked example

Given
  • TP = 102
  • SMA(20) of TP = 100
  • MD over the same window = 1.5
Steps
  1. gap = 102 - 100 = 2
  2. 0.015 x 1.5 = 0.0225
  3. CCI = 2 / 0.0225 = 88.89
CCI = 88.9, inside the conventional plus or minus 100 band.

Interpretation

The number says how large the current deviation is compared with the average deviation of the same window, rescaled so that most readings land inside plus or minus 100. A value of 200 means the deviation is twice that nominal band, not that a reversal is due.

Assumptions

  • Typical price, the mean of high, low and close, is the standard input. Implementations that feed the close instead produce different values from the same bars.
  • The denominator is the mean of absolute deviations about the same simple moving average. It is not the standard deviation, and it is not the deviation about a mean of absolute values.
  • The 0.015 constant is arbitrary. It was selected so that roughly 70 to 80 per cent of readings fall within plus or minus 100; no statistical result makes it the right number.
  • If the mean absolute deviation over the window is zero, the ratio is undefined; implementations conventionally return zero for that bar.
Limits of the formula
  • The output is unbounded, so an extreme reading can keep extending in a trend. There is no ceiling to lean against.
  • Because the yardstick is a trailing average deviation, the same price move scores very differently before and after a change in volatility.
  • Values computed with different lookbacks, or with the close instead of typical price, are not on a single scale and must not be compared.

Commonly confused with

Bollinger Bands

Both compare price with a moving average scaled by dispersion. Bollinger Bands use standard deviation and plot on the price chart; CCI uses mean absolute deviation and plots one line in a separate panel.
