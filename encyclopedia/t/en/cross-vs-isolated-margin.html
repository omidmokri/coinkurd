Cross versus Isolated Margin

Simple definition

Two ways of deciding what backs a position. Isolated risks only the collateral you assigned to it. Cross risks the whole account balance.

Professional definition

A collateral-mode setting. Under isolated margin a position's margin is ring-fenced, so the maximum loss from it is that allocation. Under cross margin every position draws on one shared balance, so free equity automatically supports a loser and the whole balance is exposed to it.

Technical definition

The choice changes which equity enters the maintenance test. Isolated runs the test on the allocated margin plus that position's own result, so the position dies early and the rest of the balance survives. Cross runs it on total equity, netting unrealised results across positions: genuine hedges then require far less collateral, while a single large loser can consume the equity of every profitable position before anything is closed. Neither mode alters the margin rate; they alter the pool.

Academic definition

A specification of the collateral pool against which an open position's maintenance requirement is assessed: either a position-specific allocation or the aggregate equity of the account.

Mechanism

Margin modes decide where the firewall sits. Isolated puts it around each position, which caps a single mistake at a known amount and forces the trader to size each allocation deliberately. Cross removes the firewall in exchange for efficiency: unrealised profits and offsetting positions now count as collateral, which is exactly why one position can quietly borrow the strength of all the others until none is left.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders on venues that offer both modes, market-neutral and spread traders who need netting, and anyone running several positions from one balance.
Why it matters
It is the clearest way to bound the loss from a single idea, or alternatively the only way to make a genuine hedge capital-efficient.
Limitations
Isolated is not safety: a tight allocation liquidates on noise. Cross is not strength: netting is only real if the positions truly offset, and correlated positions all lose at once.

Common mistakes

  • Running cross margin with directionally identical positions and calling it diversification.
  • Setting isolated margin so thin that normal volatility closes the trade.
  • Switching mode mid-trade without recomputing where the position now dies.

Examples

market

A trader is long one asset and short a closely related one. Under cross margin the two unrealised results offset and the requirement is small. Under isolated margin each leg must be funded separately and the losing leg can be closed while the hedge is still working.

numeric

A 1,000 balance supports a 5,000 long. Isolated, with 200 allocated, the position dies after a 3.5% fall and about 800 survives. Cross, the same 1,000 backs it, so a 20% fall wipes the account.

Given
  • Account balance = 1,000
  • One long position, notional = 5,000
  • Isolated allocation = 200
  • Maintenance rate m_m = 0.5% of notional, about 25
  • Fees and funding ignored
Steps
  1. Isolated: equity = 200 + 5,000 x r; fails when 200 + 5,000r < 25, i.e. r < -3.5%
  2. Isolated loss is capped at the 200 allocated, leaving about 800 of balance
  3. Cross: equity = 1,000 + 5,000 x r; fails when 1,000 + 5,000r < 25, i.e. r < -19.5%
  4. At r = -20% the position's loss is 1,000, the entire balance
The same 20% adverse move costs 200 under isolated margin and the whole 1,000 under cross

Isolated survives the move only because it exited early at a loss. It bounded the damage; it did not avoid being wrong.

Procedure

Identify which equity the venue tests. For an isolated position, add its allocated collateral to its own unrealised result and compare with its maintenance requirement. For cross margin, add the free balance to the net unrealised result of every open position and compare with the total requirement of the book.

Worked example

Given
  • B = 1,000
  • One long, notional 5,000
  • m_alloc = 200 (isolated case)
  • m_m = 0.5% of notional = 25
  • Adverse move r = -20%
Steps
  1. Isolated fails at 200 + 5,000r = 25, so r = -3.5%; loss capped at 200
  2. Cross fails at 1,000 + 5,000r = 25, so r = -19.5%
  3. At r = -20%: cross loss = 0.20 x 5,000 = 1,000
Isolated loses 200 and keeps about 800. Cross loses the full 1,000 balance.

Interpretation

The two expressions differ only in which pool is counted, and that is the entire difference in outcome. Isolated converts an unknown loss into a chosen one. Cross converts spare equity into a buffer the losing position may spend without asking.

Assumptions

  • A single venue's balance and a flat maintenance rate; implementations differ, including partial liquidation, tiered rates and per-asset collateral weightings.
  • Cross-margin netting is only as real as the offset between the positions; identical directional exposures net nothing.
  • Some venues let isolated positions have margin added automatically, which quietly reintroduces exposure to the wider balance.
Limits of the formula
  • Neither mode changes the probability of the adverse move; they change only how much of the account that move can reach.
  • Mode names and behaviour are venue conventions, not standards, and should be read from the venue's own documentation before sizing.

Commonly confused with

Margin

Switching to cross margin does not reduce the margin requirement of a position; the rate is unchanged. It only widens the pool that the requirement may draw on, and therefore widens what is at risk.
