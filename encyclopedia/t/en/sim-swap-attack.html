Simple definition

An attacker persuades a phone company to move your number to their SIM. Your text-message codes then arrive on their phone, and accounts secured by SMS fall.

Professional definition

Account takeover through the mobile carrier rather than through the victim's devices. Using leaked personal data, a convincing story or a bribed insider, the attacker has the number ported or re-issued, then drives SMS one-time codes and phone-based password resets to seize email, exchange and custodial accounts.

Technical definition

The attack targets the recovery path, not the password. Because many services treat a phone number as proof of identity, control of the number cascades: reset the email account, then every account whose reset points at that email. App-based codes and hardware security keys are not vulnerable to it, since neither is delivered over the carrier network. Self-custodied keys are untouched unless a seed phrase or key was stored somewhere the recovered accounts can reach.

Academic definition

An identity-infrastructure attack in which control of a subscriber telephone number is transferred to an adversary, defeating authentication and recovery mechanisms that treat that number as a possession factor.

Mechanism

A phone number is not a credential the user controls; it is an entry in a carrier's database that support staff can change. Once the same number is both a second factor and a reset channel, it becomes a single point of failure whose security has been outsourced to a helpdesk the account holder has never met.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone still using SMS codes, publicly known holders, and security teams writing account and recovery policy.
Why it matters
It explains why the ranking of second factors is not arbitrary, and which accounts to migrate off SMS first.
Limitations
The victim cannot patch the carrier. Defences are limited to removing the number from authentication and recovery, and to carrier-side locks where they are offered.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving a phone number as a recovery option on a high-value account.
  • Using one number for banking, email and exchange recovery.
  • Dismissing a sudden loss of mobile service as an outage.

Examples

security

Mobile service stops without warning. Minutes later the email password is reset by phone, and from that inbox the attacker resets the exchange account. The victim's devices were never touched.

numeric

An account lists three controls: a password, an SMS code, and a phone-based reset. Two of the three depend on one number, so control of that number is enough on its own; the effective count is one.

Given
  • Controls listed = password, SMS second factor, phone-based reset
  • Controls depending on the phone number = 2 of 3
Steps
  1. Number compromised, so the SMS factor is available to the attacker
  2. Phone-based reset then replaces the password, so the first control is bypassed rather than broken
One compromise defeats all three listed controls

Counting controls is misleading when they share a channel. Independence, not quantity, is what a second factor is supposed to add.

Commonly confused with

Phishing

Phishing deceives the victim into handing something over. A SIM swap deceives the carrier, and the victim may do nothing at all.
