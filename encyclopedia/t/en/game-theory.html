Simple definition

The study of choices when the outcome depends on what others choose too. It replaces asking what is best with asking what is best given what everyone else is likely to do.

Professional definition

The formal analysis of strategic interaction. A game specifies who is choosing, what each can choose, and how each values every combination of choices; solution concepts then identify which combinations can persist once everyone is reasoning about everyone else.

Technical definition

A modelling framework in which a game is a set of players, a strategy set for each, and a payoff function over strategy profiles. Information and timing convert one interaction into very different games: simultaneous or sequential, complete or incomplete information, one-shot or repeated. Predictions follow from the solution concept chosen, so a result is a property of the model's assumptions about rationality and information, not an empirical finding.

Academic definition

The mathematical study of interdependent decision problems, in which outcomes for each agent depend on the strategy profile of all agents and solutions are defined by stability conditions on such profiles.

Mechanism

Once your best move depends on someone else's, the problem stops being optimisation and becomes mutual anticipation. Solution concepts cut that regress off at a chosen depth: each one is an assumption about how far participants reason and what they know. Change that assumption and the predicted outcome changes, which is why the framework explains incentives well and forecasts behaviour poorly.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol and auction designers, researchers analysing consensus incentives, regulators studying collusion, and anyone reasoning about why a mechanism is or is not safe to attack.
Why it matters
It turns vague arguments about incentives into checkable statements: what each participant gains by deviating, and whether anyone can profit by doing so.
Limitations
Conclusions depend entirely on the assumed payoffs, information and rationality. Real participants misunderstand, coordinate off-platform, and act on motives the payoff function omits.

Common mistakes

  • Presenting a model's equilibrium as a prediction of what people will do.
  • Assuming a one-shot result carries over to a repeated setting.
  • Writing payoffs that quietly exclude the attack you should be worried about.

Examples

crypto

A consensus protocol is argued to be safe because attacking costs more than it returns. That is a game-theoretic claim: it holds only for the payoffs assumed, and an attacker with an external position that profits from disruption faces different payoffs entirely.

numeric

An opponent is believed to play A with probability 0.6 and B with 0.4. Strategy X pays 4 against A and minus 1 against B; strategy Y pays 1 and 2. Expected payoffs are 2.0 and 1.4, so X is better — but only under that belief.

Given
  • belief: opponent plays A with probability 0.6, B with 0.4
  • payoffs to X: 4 against A, -1 against B
  • payoffs to Y: 1 against A, 2 against B
Steps
  1. E[X] = 0.6 x 4 + 0.4 x (-1) = 2.4 - 0.4 = 2.0
  2. E[Y] = 0.6 x 1 + 0.4 x 2 = 0.6 + 0.8 = 1.4
E[X] = 2.0 beats E[Y] = 1.4

The ranking flips for beliefs below about 0.5 on A. Conclusions in game theory are conditional on beliefs, and the beliefs are an input, not a result.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

List the players, each player's available strategies, and the payoff to each player for every combination. State the information structure and whether play is simultaneous, sequential or repeated. Only then apply a solution concept, naming it: the answer belongs to the concept as much as to the game.

Worked example

Given
  • two players
  • player 1 strategies {X, Y}
  • beliefs over player 2 playing A with probability 0.6
  • payoffs as stated in the numeric example
Steps
  1. evaluate u_1 for each own strategy against the belief
  2. E[X] = 2.0
  3. E[Y] = 1.4
X is the best response to that belief

Interpretation

The triple is only a description: it fixes what the interaction is, not what happens. Every prediction comes from a solution concept layered on top, and naming that concept is part of stating the result honestly.

Assumptions

  • Payoffs are von Neumann-Morgenstern utilities, so expected utility comparisons are meaningful and payoffs are not simply money.
  • The normal form above compresses timing away. Sequential interactions need the extensive form, where the same payoffs can yield different solutions.
  • Players are assumed to know the game, including others' payoffs, unless the model is explicitly one of incomplete information.
Limits of the formula
  • A game is a model of an interaction, so its conclusions are only as good as the payoff and information assumptions, which are usually the contested part.
  • Many games have several equilibria and the framework alone does not say which occurs; selecting among them requires an extra argument from outside the model.

Commonly confused with

Expected Value

Expected value answers what one decision is worth against a fixed distribution. Game theory handles the case where the distribution is itself produced by others who are responding to you.
