Depreciation

Category
Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Spreading the cost of a long-lived asset over the years it is used, instead of treating the whole purchase as one year's expense.

Professional definition

The systematic allocation of a tangible asset's cost, less any expected residual value, across its useful life. It is an accounting allocation, not a cash outflow, which is why it is added back when moving from profit to operating cash flow.

Technical definition

A policy choice within a reporting framework rather than a measurement of wear. Method, useful life and residual value are all estimates set by management within the rules, so two firms holding identical assets can report materially different profits. The charge reduces book value on the balance sheet and taxable income where tax rules permit, but never moves cash in the period it is recorded.

Academic definition

The periodic allocation of the depreciable cost of a tangible long-lived asset to expense over its estimated useful life, under a systematic and rational method.

Mechanism

The cash left the business when the asset was bought; depreciation only decides which years carry the cost. Matching the charge to the years of use keeps profit from swinging wildly between a purchase year and the years that follow, at the price of making profit depend on estimates.

In practice

Who uses it
Accountants and auditors, equity analysts adjusting reported earnings, tax teams, and anyone comparing capital-heavy firms.
Why it matters
It is one of the largest non-cash items in a capital-intensive business and a frequent source of difference between accounting profit and cash generation.
Limitations
Method and life are estimates set within the reporting framework, and tax depreciation often follows separate rules from the accounts, so the two figures need not agree.

Common mistakes

  • Treating depreciation as a cash cost.
  • Comparing margins across firms with different useful-life assumptions.
  • Confusing it with currency depreciation.

Examples

market

Two carriers buy identical aircraft. One assumes a 20-year life, the other 25. The second reports higher profit every year without operating any differently, which is why analysts normalise these assumptions before comparing.

numeric

Equipment costing 120,000 with an expected residual of 20,000 and a ten-year life carries a straight-line charge of 10,000 a year. After three years the book value is 90,000, whatever the resale market says.

Given
  • cost = 120,000
  • residual value = 20,000
  • useful life = 10 years
  • straight-line method
Steps
  1. depreciable base = 120,000 - 20,000 = 100,000
  2. annual charge = 100,000 / 10 = 10,000
  3. book value after 3 years = 120,000 - 30,000
10,000 per year; book value 90,000 after three years

Straight line is one permitted method; declining-balance and units-of-production give different yearly figures from the same asset.

Procedure

Subtract the estimated residual value from capitalised cost to get the depreciable base, then divide by the useful life for the straight-line charge. Other permitted methods redistribute the same total across the years differently.

Worked example

Given
  • C = 120,000
  • S = 20,000
  • N = 10 years
Steps
  1. 120,000 - 20,000 = 100,000
  2. 100,000 / 10
D = 10,000 per year

Interpretation

The charge says how much cost the period is being asked to bear, not how much value the asset lost. Book value after depreciation is a cost allocation, not an estimate of what the asset would sell for.

Assumptions

  • Straight line is only one of several permitted methods; declining balance and units-of-production are equally legitimate under common frameworks and give different yearly charges.
  • Useful life and residual value are management estimates, reviewed periodically and revised prospectively.
  • Tax depreciation often follows a separate statutory schedule and does not have to match the reported figure.
Limits of the formula
  • Gives no information about the asset's market value or its remaining physical capability.
  • Because the inputs are estimates, depreciation is a common channel through which reported profit can be shaped within the rules.

Commonly confused with

Currency devaluation

Accounting depreciation allocates an asset's cost over time. Currency depreciation is a fall in one currency's value against another. Same English word, unrelated mechanisms.
