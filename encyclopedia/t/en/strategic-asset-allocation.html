Strategic Asset Allocation

Simple definition

The long-run split of a portfolio between asset classes, decided from the investor's objectives and constraints rather than from a market view, and left in place until those objectives change.

Professional definition

The policy weight vector that defines a portfolio's intended long-term exposures, derived from the return objective, risk tolerance, time horizon, liquidity needs, tax position and legal constraints. It is written down, usually in an investment policy statement, and maintained by rebalancing rather than rewritten at each market move.

Technical definition

A set of target weights over asset classes chosen to sit on or near the long-horizon efficient frontier implied by the investor's own constraints, then published with a permitted range around each target. Because the inputs are estimated from long samples, the policy is deliberately insensitive to short-term forecasts. Its risk follows from the covariance structure of the chosen classes, which is far more stable across decades than across crises. Revision is triggered by changed circumstances, not by recent performance.

Academic definition

The specification of long-horizon policy weights over asset classes, derived from an investor's objective function and constraint set, against which all subsequent portfolio deviations are measured.

Mechanism

Most of a diversified portfolio's return dispersion comes from which classes it holds and in what proportion, because class returns move together far more than individual holdings inside one class. Fixing those proportions in advance therefore fixes most of the risk profile, and turns every later decision into a small deviation that can be named and measured against the policy.

In practice

Who uses it
Pension funds, endowments, family offices, advisers, and individuals who write a plan down before choosing instruments.
Why it matters
It gives every later decision a reference point: a deviation can be named, sized and reviewed instead of disappearing into the portfolio.
Limitations
The estimated inputs are long-sample averages. Correlations rise under stress, so the diversification the policy relies on is weakest exactly when it is needed.

Common mistakes

  • Rewriting the policy weights after every drawdown instead of rebalancing back to them.
  • Setting weights from historical returns alone, ignoring horizon and liquidity needs.
  • Treating the policy as a forecast rather than a decision about risk.

Examples

market

An institution's policy sets 60% equities and 40% bonds with ranges of plus or minus 5 percentage points. A strong equity year pushes the equity weight above the upper limit. That is a rebalancing trigger; the policy itself changes only when the institution's spending needs or horizon change.

numeric

A 60/40 policy with plus or minus 5 point ranges. Over a year equities return 25% and bonds 2%; the equity weight drifts to 64.8%, still inside the 55 to 65 band, so the policy calls for no trade.

Given
  • policy weights: equities 60%, bonds 40%
  • permitted range: plus or minus 5 percentage points
  • one-year returns: equities +25%, bonds +2%
Steps
  1. equity value = 60 x 1.25 = 75
  2. bond value = 40 x 1.02 = 40.8
  3. portfolio value = 75 + 40.8 = 115.8
  4. equity weight = 75 / 115.8 = 0.648
equities drift to 64.8%, inside the 55-65% range

The same drift against a plus or minus 2 point range would have breached the limit. The width of the band, not the size of the drift, decides whether anything is done.

Commonly confused with

Tactical Asset Allocation

Strategic allocation is the policy itself, reviewed on a horizon of years. Tactical allocation is a temporary, measured deviation from that policy, reversed when the view behind it resolves.
