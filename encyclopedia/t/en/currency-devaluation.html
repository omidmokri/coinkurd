Currency devaluation

Simple definition

A country officially lowering the value of its currency against others. Imports become dearer, exports cheaper abroad, and savings held in that currency buy less of the world.

Professional definition

A deliberate downward adjustment of an official exchange rate or peg by the authorities. It is distinct from depreciation, which is a market-driven fall under a floating regime, though the economic effects on prices and trade overlap.

Technical definition

Under a fixed or managed regime the parity is a policy variable, so devaluation is an announcement; under a float the rate is an outcome and the same move is depreciation. The arithmetic is asymmetric: a currency losing a given percentage of its value implies a larger percentage rise in the foreign currency's price, and quoting the move in the other direction changes the number. Pass-through to domestic prices depends on import intensity and the currency of invoicing.

Academic definition

An administered reduction in the official parity of a currency within a fixed or managed exchange-rate regime, distinguished from market-determined depreciation under floating arrangements.

Mechanism

A weaker currency raises the home-currency price of everything imported, including inputs, so domestic inflation follows with a lag that depends on how much is imported and in which currency contracts are written. Debt owed in foreign currency grows in home-currency terms immediately, which is why devaluation and debt distress often arrive together.

In practice

Who uses it
Importers and exporters, borrowers with foreign-currency debt, savers in weak-currency economies, and investors holding local assets.
Why it matters
It changes the home-currency value of every foreign claim and obligation at once, so hedging decisions and pricing have to be revisited together.
Limitations
The effect on trade volumes is slow and depends on supply capacity; the effect on prices and foreign-currency debt is immediate. The two do not arrive on the same schedule.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting the move in one direction and the loss in the other.
  • Treating devaluation and market depreciation as the same event.
  • Assuming exports respond quickly.

Examples

numeric

A currency loses 20 percent of its value. The foreign currency has not risen 20 percent against it but 25 percent, because the two percentages are taken on different bases.

Given
  • fall in the home currency's value x = 20% = 0.20
  • quote direction stated explicitly in both steps
Steps
  1. home currency retains 1 - 0.20 = 0.80 of its value
  2. price of foreign currency rises by 1 / 0.80 - 1 = 0.25
a 20% devaluation equals a 25% rise in the foreign currency's price

The percentage is invented. The asymmetry is arithmetic and grows with the size of the move: a 50% devaluation is a 100% rise in the foreign currency.

market

A firm earning in local currency but owing in foreign currency sees its debt-service burden jump overnight, while the export revenue meant to offset it arrives only over the following quarters.

Mathematical foundation

y = \frac{x}{1 - x}, \qquad x = \frac{y}{1 + y}

Variables

xProportional fall in the home currency's value, measured in foreign-currency termsdecimal
yProportional rise in the price of the foreign currency in home-currency termsdecimal

Procedure

Decide which currency is in the numerator and say so. If the home currency loses a fraction x of its value, the price of foreign currency rises by x divided by one minus x. To go the other way, divide the rise by one plus itself.

Worked example

Given
  • x = 0.20 (home currency loses 20% of its value)
Steps
  1. 1 - x = 0.80
  2. y = 0.20 / 0.80 = 0.25
y = 25%: the foreign currency costs 25% more in home-currency terms

Interpretation

Two numbers describe one move, and neither is wrong: they are percentages of different bases. Any statement about a currency's fall is incomplete until the quote direction is named.

Assumptions

  • The relation is pure arithmetic between two ways of quoting one bilateral rate; it says nothing about why the rate moved.
  • It applies to a single currency pair. A trade-weighted measure of a currency's value requires a basket and weights, which is a different calculation.
  • Devaluation under a managed regime is an announced parity change; the same arithmetic applies to market depreciation, but the policy meaning differs.
Limits of the formula
  • It gives no information about pass-through to domestic prices, which depends on import intensity, invoicing currency and competitive conditions.
  • Nothing here predicts a move; the formula only converts one quotation of a move into the other.

Commonly confused with

Purchasing power

Devaluation is about the external price of a currency against another. Purchasing power is about what a currency buys at home. A currency can weaken abroad while domestic prices lag, and the two gaps close only partly.
