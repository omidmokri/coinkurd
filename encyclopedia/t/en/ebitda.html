Simple definition

Profit measured before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation are subtracted. It is deliberately incomplete: four real costs have been removed from it.

Professional definition

A non-GAAP earnings measure built by adding interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation back to net income, or by adding depreciation and amortisation to operating profit. Used as a rough proxy for operating cash generation and as the denominator of enterprise-value multiples and debt covenants.

Technical definition

Neither IFRS nor US GAAP defines EBITDA, so each reporter constructs its own, and adjusted EBITDA adds further management-selected add-backs such as share-based pay, restructuring and impairments. It excludes capital expenditure, working-capital movements, interest and tax, which means a capital-intensive or heavily indebted firm can report rising EBITDA while consuming cash every year.

Academic definition

An unstandardised earnings aggregate computed before financing cost, income tax and non-cash allocations of prior capital expenditure, widely used as a comparability device and widely criticised as a measure of economic performance.

Mechanism

The add-backs remove exactly the costs that differ most between firms: how they financed themselves, where they are taxed, and how much old capital spending they are writing off. That is why EBITDA travels across companies — and why it flatters any business whose assets wear out or whose debt is large, because those costs are real and still have to be paid in cash.

In practice

Who uses it
Leveraged-finance and credit analysts, private-equity and mergers teams, covenant drafters, and companies presenting results to investors.
Why it matters
It strips out financing and tax differences so operations can be compared, and it is the conventional base for enterprise-value multiples and leverage covenants.
Limitations
It is not a cash flow measure and not an audited standard figure. The critique is not that it is imprecise but that it omits costs the business cannot avoid.

Common mistakes

  • Treating EBITDA as cash flow and skipping capital expenditure.
  • Accepting management's adjusted EBITDA add-backs without listing them.
  • Comparing an asset-heavy firm with an asset-light one on EBITDA alone.

Examples

market

A cable or telecoms operator reports growing EBITDA for years while free cash flow stays near zero, because the network must be rebuilt continuously. The depreciation EBITDA adds back is, for this business, a reasonable estimate of recurring capital spending.

numeric

Net income 120, interest 30, tax 40, depreciation and amortisation 90 gives EBITDA 280. The same firm spends 110 on capital expenditure and pays 30 of interest, so cash available to owners is far below 280.

Given
  • Net income = 120
  • Interest expense = 30
  • Income tax = 40
  • Depreciation and amortisation = 90
  • Capital expenditure = 110
Steps
  1. EBITDA = 120 + 30 + 40 + 90 = 280
  2. Cash after interest and capital expenditure = 280 - 30 - 110 = 140
  3. Before tax of 40, leaving roughly 100
EBITDA = 280, but roughly 100 is left once interest, tax and capital spending are paid

The 180 difference is not an accounting subtlety. It is cash that left the business.

Procedure

Start from net income and add back interest, income tax, depreciation and amortisation, taking depreciation and amortisation from the cash flow statement rather than estimating them. Starting instead from operating profit and adding depreciation and amortisation gives the same figure only when there are no non-operating items.

Worked example

Given
  • Net income = 120
  • Interest expense = 30
  • Income tax = 40
  • Depreciation and amortisation = 90
Steps
  1. 120 + 30 = 150
  2. 150 + 40 = 190
  3. 190 + 90 = 280
EBITDA = 280

Interpretation

The output is operating earnings before financing, tax and the cost of using up long-lived assets. It answers how the operations performed on a financing-neutral basis, and nothing about whether the firm can pay its bills.

Assumptions

  • EBITDA is not defined by IFRS or US GAAP. Each reporter constructs its own, and 'adjusted EBITDA' adds management-selected items, so two companies' figures are not automatically comparable.
  • The add-back of depreciation and amortisation should come from the cash flow statement; estimating it from asset balances produces a different number.
  • Whether interest is gross or net of interest income, and whether lease costs are inside or outside the measure after IFRS 16, both change the result and must be stated.
Limits of the formula
  • Excludes capital expenditure, working-capital movements, interest and tax, all of which are paid in cash, so it is not a cash flow measure.
  • The standard criticism is that it makes capital-intensive and heavily indebted businesses look profitable when they are not: a firm can grow EBITDA every year and still never earn its cost of capital.

Commonly confused with

Free Cash Flow

EBITDA adds depreciation back and stops. Free cash flow subtracts the capital spending that depreciation stood for, plus working-capital movements. The difference is the whole point of the criticism.
