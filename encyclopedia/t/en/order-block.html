Simple definition

The last candle of the opposite colour before a sharp move away, drawn on the chart as a zone. Traders watch for price to come back to that zone and react there.

Professional definition

In smart-money teaching, the final down-candle before an impulsive rally (a bullish order block) or the final up-candle before an impulsive sell-off (a bearish one), marked as a zone from its open to its close or from its high to its low, and treated as an area where a return may meet renewed interest.

Technical definition

A codeable version: detect a displacement leg — k consecutive bars in one direction, or a leg whose range exceeds a volatility threshold such as a multiple of ATR, often required to break structure as well. Mark the last opposite-colour candle before that leg. The zone is body-only in some teachings and high-to-low in others, and it counts as mitigated once price has traded into part or all of it. Each of those choices is a different indicator wearing the same name.

Academic definition

A practitioner construct designating the final counter-direction candle preceding an impulsive displacement as a prospective reaction zone, functionally comparable to a supply or demand area and lacking an established empirical validation.

Mechanism

Mechanically, an order block is where a fast move began: it is the edge of the last region that traded before the move, and therefore the first region a pullback revisits. That much is a statement about the shape of the chart. The claim that unfilled institutional orders rest there is an interpretation — resting orders are not visible on a candle chart at all.

In practice

Who uses it
Price-action and smart-money-concepts traders, zone-based discretionary traders, and developers coding supply and demand zones.
Why it matters
It reduces entry location to a rule with an objective reference candle, which gives a defined invalidation point and so a measurable stop distance.
Limitations
Zone boundaries, the displacement filter and what counts as mitigation vary between teachers. No peer-reviewed evidence base exists, and the institutional-order story cannot be checked from price alone.

Common mistakes

  • Marking the zone after the move and calling it predictive.
  • Using body-only and high-to-low zones interchangeably.
  • Ignoring which timeframe the block was marked on.

Examples

market

Price grinds sideways, prints one small down-candle, then rallies hard for three bars and breaks the prior swing high. That small down-candle is marked as a bullish order block, and the next pullback into it is watched for a reaction.

numeric

The reference candle opens at 100.0, closes at 98.5, with a high of 100.8 and a low of 98.2. The body zone is 1.5 wide, the high-to-low zone 2.6. Choosing the wider zone moves the stop by 0.4 and the entry by 0.8.

Given
  • reference candle: open 100.0, close 98.5, high 100.8, low 98.2
  • bullish block, so the zone is drawn below price
Steps
  1. body zone = 100.0 - 98.5 = 1.5 wide, upper edge 100.0
  2. high-to-low zone = 100.8 - 98.2 = 2.6 wide, upper edge 100.8
  3. entry at the upper edge differs by 100.8 - 100.0 = 0.8
  4. a stop below the zone differs by 98.5 - 98.2 = 0.3
Two standard definitions give zones 1.5 and 2.6 wide from one candle.

Risk per unit changes with the definition, so a backtest must state which zone convention it used.

Commonly confused with

Support and Resistance

A support or resistance level is drawn from repeated reaction at a price. An order block is drawn from one candle preceding an impulsive move, whether or not price has ever reacted there before.
