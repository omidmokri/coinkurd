Simple definition

A contract where depositors put assets in to earn interest and borrowers take them out against collateral. No one negotiates: the rate comes from how much of the pool is already lent.

Professional definition

A pooled, overcollateralised credit market in smart contracts. Suppliers share one interest-bearing pool per asset, borrowers draw from it against pledged collateral, and the rate on both sides is set by a published function of utilisation rather than by matching individual lenders to individual loans.

Technical definition

The design trades borrower assessment for collateral and automation: it has no view on who is borrowing, only on what secures the loan and on a price feed. Three dependencies follow. Rates are mechanical, so they respond to pool withdrawals as readily as to credit demand. Liquidation relies on third parties finding the bonus worth taking. And because positions are composable, the same collateral can support borrowing elsewhere, so a single price feed failure propagates through protocols that never interacted directly.

Academic definition

A smart-contract intermediated credit market in which supply and demand for a fungible asset are matched through a shared pool with algorithmically determined rates, and default is managed ex ante by collateral and automated liquidation rather than ex post by recovery.

Mechanism

Pooling is what makes a rate function necessary. With no negotiation and no term, the only information the contract has about supply and demand is how much of the pool has been drawn, so utilisation becomes the price signal. That makes rates fast and transparent, and it also means a large withdrawal moves the borrow rate exactly like a surge in borrowing would.

In practice

Who uses it
Suppliers seeking a yield on idle assets, borrowers who want liquidity without selling, liquidators, and protocols that use a lending market as plumbing for leverage.
Why it matters
It provides borrowing without an application and lending without a counterparty choice, with rates and collateral rules visible to everyone in advance.
Limitations
Credit is only as sound as the collateral parameters and the price feed. Utilisation near full leaves suppliers unable to withdraw, and governance can change the rules under an open position.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a supply rate as a deposit rate with principal protection.
  • Borrowing at high utilisation without planning for a withdrawal squeeze.
  • Treating the published rate as fixed when it is recomputed every block.

Examples

market

A supplier deposits a pegged asset and watches the quoted rate triple over a week without depositing or withdrawing anything, because borrowing demand drew the pool closer to fully utilised.

numeric

A pool holds 2,000,000 of cash with 6,000,000 borrowed. Utilisation is 75 percent, so only a quarter of supplied value can be withdrawn at once; a request for 3,000,000 cannot be met at all until borrowers repay.

Given
  • cash in the pool = 2,000,000
  • borrowed = 6,000,000
Steps
  1. total supplied = 2,000,000 + 6,000,000 = 8,000,000
  2. utilisation = 6,000,000 / 8,000,000 = 75%
  3. immediately withdrawable = 2,000,000, i.e. 25% of supplied value
At 75% utilisation only the 2,000,000 of cash is available to withdraw.

This is why high utilisation is a liquidity risk for suppliers as well as a high-rate signal.

Commonly confused with

Yield Farming

Supplying to a lending pool earns borrower interest. Yield farming adds protocol-issued tokens on top, which is a different return with a different risk.
