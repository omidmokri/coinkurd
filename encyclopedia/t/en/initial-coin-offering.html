Simple definition

A public sale of a new token to raise money, usually before the product exists. Buyers send funds and receive tokens whose value depends on whether the team delivers.

Professional definition

A primary issuance in which an issuer sells newly created tokens to the public, typically against established cryptoassets or fiat, documented in a whitepaper rather than a prospectus. The structure concentrates risk at the point where capital has been taken and nothing has yet been built, and it attracts securities-law scrutiny in many jurisdictions.

Technical definition

The mechanism separates payment from delivery of any product, so the buyer's protection comes entirely from disclosure and enforceability — exactly the two things a whitepaper does not provide. Documented failure and fraud patterns in this market include claims of guaranteed returns, invented teams and credentials, copied documents, and projects abandoned after funds are raised. Where an offering is characterised as a securities offering, selling it without registration or a valid exemption is itself unlawful in that jurisdiction, independent of whether the project was sincere.

Academic definition

A primary distribution of newly created crypto-assets to the public in exchange for consideration, conducted outside conventional prospectus regimes, with disclosure quality and legal characterisation varying by issuer and jurisdiction.

Mechanism

Capital arrives before accountability exists, and that ordering is the whole risk: once funds are received the issuer's incentive to continue is weaker than its incentive was to raise. Conventional offerings answer this with prospectus liability, escrow and staged release; a token sale answers it with reputation, which is cheap to fabricate. This is why the documented abuse patterns cluster at exactly this stage rather than later.

In practice

Who uses it
Issuers raising capital, retail and venture buyers, exchanges listing post-sale, and regulators and enforcement bodies in each jurisdiction.
Why it matters
It remains a common route for funding a protocol before revenue exists, and it is where a token's initial allocation and price are set.
Limitations
Disclosure is voluntary and unaudited, there is usually no redemption right, and the legal characterisation of the offering differs by jurisdiction and may be decided only in hindsight.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a whitepaper as the equivalent of a prospectus.
  • Believing any promise of guaranteed or fixed returns.
  • Ignoring how much of supply insiders keep at what price.

Examples

numeric

A sale offers 100 million of a 1 billion supply at 0.10, raising 10 million. Insiders hold the other 900 million at a far lower cost basis, so public buyers fund the project and own a tenth of it.

Given
  • total supply = 1,000,000,000 tokens
  • tokens sold to the public = 100,000,000
  • sale price = 0.10 units of account per token
Steps
  1. amount raised = 100,000,000 x 0.10 = 10,000,000
  2. public share of supply = 100,000,000 / 1,000,000,000 = 10 percent
  3. implied value of full supply at sale price = 0.10 x 1,000,000,000 = 100,000,000
10,000,000 raised for 10 percent of supply, implying a 100,000,000 valuation of the whole token

Hypothetical figures. The implied valuation is the fully diluted construction and carries all of its weaknesses; it is not evidence of what the project is worth.

regulatory

In the EU, an offer of crypto-assets to the public brings disclosure and conduct duties under MiCA, while an asset that qualifies as a financial instrument falls under the instrument regime instead. Other jurisdictions apply their own tests.

Commonly confused with

Airdrop

An offering takes money from buyers in exchange for tokens. An airdrop gives tokens away with no payment. Both are primary distributions, but only one raises capital and only one creates a buyer with a loss to recover.
