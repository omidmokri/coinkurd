Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A payment a company makes to its shareholders out of profits. Unlike interest on a loan, it is a decision, not an obligation, and it can be cut at any time.

Professional definition

A discretionary distribution of a company's earnings or reserves to its owners, usually in cash and usually periodic. It is not a return in itself: the share price falls by roughly the dividend on the ex-dividend date, so the payment transfers value rather than creating it.

Technical definition

A transfer from the company's balance sheet to shareholders, reducing retained earnings and cash. In a frictionless setting the shareholder's total wealth is unchanged by the payment; in practice taxation, transaction costs and the signalling content of a change in policy all make dividend decisions consequential. A high yield can reflect a strong payout or a collapsed price, and the two cannot be distinguished from the ratio alone.

Academic definition

A discretionary distribution of corporate earnings or reserves to equity holders, reducing the firm's retained earnings and, at the moment of distribution, its market value by approximately the amount paid.

Mechanism

Cash leaving the company is cash the company no longer owns, so the share is worth less immediately after the payment than immediately before. What makes dividends informative is not the cash itself but the commitment: boards are reluctant to cut, so a sustained policy says something about expected future earnings.

In practice

Who uses it
Income investors, pension funds matching cash needs, company boards setting payout policy, and analysts modelling cash returns.
Why it matters
It converts paper ownership into received cash without selling, which matters for investors who need spending money from a portfolio.
Limitations
Discretionary and reversible; a high yield often signals a falling price or an unsustainable payout rather than a generous company.

Common mistakes

  • Buying just before the ex-date expecting free money.
  • Reading a high dividend yield as a quality signal.
  • Treating dividends as guaranteed income.

Examples

market

A share closes at 50 and pays a dividend of 2. On the ex-dividend date it opens near 48, all else equal. The holder has 48 of share and 2 of cash, not 50 of share plus a bonus.

numeric

A company pays 3 per share on earnings of 5 and a price of 60. The payout ratio is 60% and the dividend yield 5%. If the price halves with no change in policy, the yield reads 10% while nothing improved.

Given
  • dividend per share DPS = 3
  • earnings per share EPS = 5
  • price P = 60
Steps
  1. payout ratio = 3 / 5 = 60%
  2. dividend yield = 3 / 60 = 5%
  3. if P = 30: yield = 3 / 30 = 10%
payout 60%, yield 5%; yield doubles to 10% on a halved price

Illustrative figures. A rising yield driven by a falling price is a warning, not an improvement.

Mathematical foundation

\text{dividend yield} = \frac{DPS}{P}, \qquad \text{payout ratio} = \frac{DPS}{EPS}

Variables

DPSDividend per share over the stated periodcurrency per share
PShare price at the measurement datecurrency per share
EPSEarnings per share for the same periodcurrency per share

Procedure

Use dividends for a stated window, trailing or forecast, and divide by the current price for the yield or by earnings for the payout ratio. State which window you used, since trailing and forward yields often differ sharply.

Worked example

Given
  • DPS = 3
  • P = 60
  • EPS = 5
Steps
  1. 3 / 60 = 0.05
  2. 3 / 5 = 0.60
yield 5%, payout ratio 60%

Interpretation

The yield says what cash the current price buys, not what the investment returns. Total return also needs the price change, and a payout ratio near or above one means the company is paying out more than it earns.

Assumptions

  • Trailing yields use dividends already paid; forward yields use declared or forecast dividends and are not the same measure.
  • The ex-dividend price adjustment holds approximately; taxes and market frictions mean the observed drop is not exactly the dividend.
  • Share buybacks return cash without appearing in either ratio, so dividend measures understate total distributions for firms that favour buybacks.
Limits of the formula
  • Payout policy is a management choice and can be maintained with borrowed money for a time, which the ratios do not reveal.
  • Dividend yield is not comparable across tax regimes, since the after-tax amount received differs by holder and jurisdiction.

Commonly confused with

Coupon rate

A coupon is contractually owed by a borrower and missing it is default. A dividend is declared at the board's discretion and can be reduced or skipped without breaching anything.
