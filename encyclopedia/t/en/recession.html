Simple definition

A period when economic activity falls broadly and for long enough to be more than a blip: output, jobs and income weaken together, not just one of them.

Professional definition

A significant, broad-based and sustained decline in activity. There is no single universal definition: some bodies apply a mechanical rule of two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP, while others judge depth, diffusion and duration across output, employment and income.

Technical definition

A dating problem, not a measurement. The two-quarter rule is a convenient rule of thumb, not the official determination in the United States, where a committee of economists assigns peak and trough months using multiple series. Because GDP is revised and committees deliberate, recessions are identified retrospectively, often long after they have begun or ended, so no real-time statistic 'declares' one.

Academic definition

A phase of the business cycle marked by a significant and diffuse contraction in aggregate activity, conventionally dated retrospectively between a cyclical peak and the following trough.

Mechanism

Downturns spread because spending decisions are linked: weaker demand cuts revenue, revenue cuts hiring and investment, and lost income cuts demand again. Credit tightens as collateral values fall, which amplifies the loop. That feedback is why recessions are diffuse across sectors rather than confined to the one where the shock began.

In practice

Who uses it
Policymakers, allocators setting defensive or cyclical tilts, credit analysts, and anyone writing scenario assumptions for a plan or budget.
Why it matters
The cycle phase changes default rates, earnings, policy direction and correlations at once, so it is a shared input to almost every macro-linked decision.
Limitations
You cannot know you are in one at the time. Dating is retrospective, data are revised, and definitions differ, so real-time recession calls are forecasts, not facts.

Common mistakes

  • Treating two negative quarters as the official definition.
  • Calling a recession from one weak monthly release.
  • Assuming every recession brings the same market behaviour.

Examples

numeric

Real GDP falls 0.1 percent in one quarter and 0.1 percent in the next. The two-quarter rule of thumb is satisfied, yet employment and income kept rising, and later revisions turned both quarters positive.

Given
  • quarter 1 real GDP growth = -0.1%
  • quarter 2 real GDP growth = -0.1%
  • employment and real income rising over the same period
Steps
  1. two consecutive negative quarters: rule-of-thumb condition met
  2. breadth test across employment and income: not met
  3. later data vintage: both quarters revised to positive
rule-of-thumb recession, no broad-based contraction

Figures are invented to show why the two-quarter rule is a heuristic rather than a definition. Revisions of this kind are a normal feature of national accounts.

market

Equity markets fall through most of a contraction and often begin recovering before the trough is reached. By the time the downturn is officially dated, the dating adds information about history, not about what to do next.

Commonly confused with

Bull and Bear Market

A bear market is a decline in asset prices; a recession is a decline in economic activity. They overlap often but not always, and they start and end on different dates.

There is no single universal definition of a recession and the entry deliberately does not adopt one. The two-quarter rule is named as a rule of thumb and explicitly not as the official United States determination, which is made retrospectively by a committee of economists; this registry contains no document from that committee, so no dating methodology is described in detail and no historical episode, date or duration is cited.
