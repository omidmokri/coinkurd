Options Contract

Simple definition

A contract that gives its buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price by a set date. The buyer pays a premium for it.

Professional definition

A contract conveying an asymmetric payoff: the holder pays a premium for the right to transact at the strike price, and the writer receives the premium and carries the obligation. Calls confer the right to buy, puts the right to sell, and either can be bought or written.

Technical definition

A derivative whose price splits into intrinsic value, the payoff from exercising immediately floored at zero, and time value, the remaining chance that the underlying moves favourably. Four basic positions follow from two rights and two sides: long call, short call, long put, short put. A long position's loss is capped at the premium and the writer's risk is the mirror image, which is why writers post margin and buyers do not.

Academic definition

A contingent claim granting its holder the unilateral right to exchange a specified underlying at a predetermined strike price on or before expiry, in return for a premium paid to the writer.

Mechanism

The premium is the price of optionality. A buyer's loss is capped at the premium while the gain is open-ended, so the writer must be paid for an obligation of the opposite shape. Time value decays as expiry approaches because less opportunity for the underlying to move remains, and it is largest when the strike sits near the current price.

In practice

Who uses it
Hedgers buying downside protection, income-seeking writers, volatility traders, and desks shaping non-linear exposure.
Why it matters
Options price risk in a way linear instruments cannot: a bounded loss can be bought, or optionality can be sold and paid for.
Limitations
A long option can expire worthless even when the directional view was right but late. Writing options converts a small, frequent premium into a rare, large loss.

Common mistakes

  • Buying short-dated options and blaming direction when time value decayed.
  • Writing uncovered calls without sizing the unlimited tail.
  • Reading the premium as the probability of profit.

Examples

market

A holder who does not want to sell buys a put below the current price. If the market falls, the put gains and offsets part of the holding's loss. If it does not, the premium is lost, exactly like an insurance payment that was never claimed.

numeric

A call with a strike of 100 trades at a premium of 6 while the underlying is 104. Intrinsic value is 4 and time value 2. At expiry the buyer breaks even at 106, so being right on direction is not enough.

Given
  • call option, strike K = 100
  • underlying price S = 104
  • premium paid = 6
  • European-style exercise, no dividend or carry on the underlying
Steps
  1. intrinsic value = max(104 - 100, 0) = 4
  2. time value = premium - intrinsic = 6 - 4 = 2
  3. breakeven at expiry = K + premium = 100 + 6 = 106
intrinsic 4, time value 2, breakeven 106

Ignores commissions. At expiry only intrinsic value remains, so the 2 of time value is what the buyer pays for the chance of a further move.

Procedure

Compute intrinsic value from the strike and the current price, flooring it at zero because the right is never exercised at a loss. Subtract it from the traded premium: whatever remains is time value. Breakeven at expiry for a long call is the strike plus the premium, and for a long put the strike minus the premium.

Worked example

Given
  • call, K = 100
  • S = 104
  • premium = 6
Steps
  1. S - K = 4, which is positive, so intrinsic = 4
  2. time value = 6 - 4 = 2
  3. breakeven = 100 + 6 = 106
intrinsic 4, time value 2, breakeven 106

Interpretation

Intrinsic value is arithmetic and not in dispute. Time value is where the market and any model disagree, and it is the quantity implied volatility puts a number on. An option whose strike is far from the price has no intrinsic value at all, so its entire premium is time value.

Assumptions

  • The payoff identities are stated for European-style exercise at expiry. American-style options can be exercised earlier, which adds an early-exercise premium not captured by this decomposition.
  • Intrinsic value is defined on the underlying's price alone, ignoring dividends, carry and the cost of financing a position.
  • Premium equals intrinsic plus time value is a decomposition of an observed market price, not a pricing model. It tells you nothing about whether the premium is fair.
  • Pricing the time-value component requires a model. Black and Scholes (1973) is the standard reference, and it needs the underlying price, the strike, time to expiry, the risk-free rate and a volatility input. That full solution is deliberately not reproduced here, because stating it without defining every variable and every assumption would mislead more than it informs.
Limits of the formula
  • The decomposition is silent on fair value; that question needs a model and a volatility input, which is the subject of the implied-volatility entry.
  • Deep out-of-the-money options are entirely time value, which makes their price the most sensitive to a change in the volatility input and the least anchored to the underlying's current level.

Commonly confused with

Futures Contract

A futures contract obliges both sides to transact. An option obliges only the writer; the buyer holds a right they can let expire.
