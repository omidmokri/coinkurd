Stagflation

Simple definition

Prices rising while the economy is not growing and unemployment is high. The uncomfortable case where the usual trade-off between inflation and jobs breaks down.

Professional definition

The joint occurrence of persistent inflation and weak or negative growth with rising unemployment. It is awkward for policy because the standard response to one problem makes the other worse: tightening deepens the slowdown, easing feeds the inflation.

Technical definition

Typically traced to an adverse supply shock — energy, food, labour supply or productivity — which raises costs and lowers output at the same time, rather than to demand, which moves both in the same direction. Whether it persists depends heavily on whether inflation expectations stay anchored, since unanchored expectations turn a one-off cost shock into a wage-price process.

Academic definition

A macroeconomic state combining stagnant output and elevated unemployment with sustained inflation, usually attributed to adverse aggregate supply disturbances rather than demand.

Mechanism

A supply shock shifts cost and capacity at once, so output falls while prices rise. The policy rate is a single instrument facing two targets, and it cannot push both the right way. The outcome then turns on expectations: if households and firms expect the shock to pass, wage demands stay contained; if not, the shock becomes self-sustaining.

In practice

Who uses it
Central banks facing a mandate conflict, allocators holding both nominal bonds and equities, and businesses pricing contracts through a cost shock.
Why it matters
It is the regime where the usual hedge fails: nominal bonds and equities can fall together, so diversification built on their past relationship stops working.
Limitations
The word is used loosely for any uncomfortable mix of weak growth and high inflation; there is no agreed threshold that marks a stagflation.

Common mistakes

  • Labelling any single quarter of weak growth with high inflation a stagflation.
  • Assuming bonds will hedge equities in this regime.
  • Expecting a historical episode to repeat in detail.

Examples

everyday

A worker's pay rises 3 percent while the cost of food, rent and fuel rises faster, and the firm freezes hiring because its own input costs jumped. Pay is up, living standards are down, and jobs are scarcer.

market

A central bank with both a price-stability and an employment objective must choose which to miss. Markets then trade the choice itself, and the reaction can be larger than the reaction to the underlying data.

Commonly confused with

Recession

A recession is a contraction in activity whatever inflation is doing. Stagflation is specifically a contraction or stagnation together with persistent inflation, which is what makes the policy choice painful.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

There is no agreed quantitative threshold for stagflation, and the supply-shock account given here, while the standard textbook reading, remains debated in its details; this registry contains no study that settles the attribution. No historical episode, country or figure is cited, and the claim that bonds and equities can fall together in such a regime is stated as a possibility, not as a measured regularity.
