Capital Gains

Regulation and Compliance
Level
Professional

Simple definition

The profit measured when you dispose of an asset: what you received minus what it cost you. A gain on paper usually becomes taxable only when it is realised.

Professional definition

The excess of disposal proceeds over the adjusted cost basis of an asset. Realisation on disposal, rather than change in market value, is the usual trigger, and most systems distinguish gains by holding period or by the character of the asset.

Technical definition

Measurement requires three inputs the taxpayer must be able to evidence: proceeds, adjusted basis and the date pair that fixes the holding period. Basis is where disputes arise, because the method of identifying which units were sold materially changes the result, and permitted methods differ by jurisdiction. Acquisitions by means other than purchase — a reward, an airdrop, a fork — need a basis rule of their own, which each system supplies differently or not at all.

Academic definition

The realised excess of disposal proceeds over adjusted basis, recognised at disposition rather than on accrual of market value, with characterisation and holding-period treatment determined by the applicable tax regime.

Mechanism

Taxing value as it moves would require valuing everything continuously and would tax people who have no cash. Realisation solves that by waiting for a transaction, which fixes both a price and a date. The cost is a timing choice handed to the holder, and the consequence is that record keeping must survive for as long as the asset is held, not just for the year of sale.

In practice

Who uses it
Taxpayers and their accountants, platform reporting teams, and portfolio managers whose rebalancing creates disposals.
Why it matters
It is the quantity on which tax is computed in most systems, and the reason basis records are worth more than a profit-and-loss screen.
Limitations
Whether gains are taxed, at what rate, how holding period is treated and which basis methods are permitted all differ by jurisdiction and change. This is not tax advice.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a platform's unrealised profit display as a taxable figure.
  • Assuming the basis method used by one platform is the one the tax system permits.
  • Overlooking that an asset-for-asset swap can realise a gain in some systems.

Examples

numeric

Units bought for 6,500 including fees are sold for 9,000 net of fees. The realised gain is 2,500. Whether that amount is taxed, and at what rate, depends entirely on the jurisdiction.

Given
  • Adjusted cost basis including acquisition fees = 6,500
  • Disposal proceeds net of disposal fees = 9,000
  • Single lot, fully disposed of
Steps
  1. Gain = 9,000 - 6,500
  2. Gain = 2,500
Realised gain of 2,500

Arithmetic only. Whether fees adjust basis or proceeds, whether the gain is taxable, and at what rate, are jurisdiction-specific and are not addressed.

regulatory

The same disposal yields different taxable amounts under different unit-identification methods, because a different cost basis is matched to the sale. The arithmetic is simple; which method is permitted is not.

Mathematical foundation

G = P - B_{adj}

Variables

GRealised capital gain, or a loss when negativecurrency
PProceeds received on disposalcurrency
B_{adj}Adjusted cost basis of the units disposed ofcurrency

Procedure

Identify which units were disposed of under a permitted identification method. Determine their adjusted basis, including acquisition costs and any adjustments the tax system requires. Determine proceeds. Subtract basis from proceeds. Characterise the result by holding period or asset class as the applicable regime requires.

Worked example

Given
  • P = 9,000
  • B_adj = 6,500
Steps
  1. G = 9,000 - 6,500
G = 2,500

Interpretation

The output is a realised amount in currency, fixed by a transaction rather than by market value. It states what changed hands against what was paid; it says nothing about whether tax is due, which is a separate jurisdictional question.

Assumptions

  • Realisation basis: the gain is recognised on disposal, not as market value changes. Some regimes apply mark-to-market or deemed-disposal rules to some holders or assets, and the identity does not hold there.
  • Adjusted basis is determinable. Where units were acquired at different prices, the permitted identification method (for example first-in-first-out or specific identification) changes B_adj and therefore G; permitted methods differ by jurisdiction.
  • Fees are assumed to adjust basis or proceeds. Whether and how they do is a matter of the applicable tax rules.
Limits of the formula
  • The formula gives a pre-tax measurement. It is not a tax computation: rates, exemptions, allowances, loss offsets and holding-period characterisation are all outside it and all jurisdiction-specific.
  • Assets acquired other than by purchase, such as rewards, airdrops or forks, require a separate basis rule that this identity does not supply.

Commonly confused with

Real versus nominal return

A capital gain is a nominal realised amount. Whether it represents a real gain after inflation is a separate question, and tax is usually computed on the nominal figure.
