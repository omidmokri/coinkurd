Capital Asset Pricing Model

Simple definition

A model that derives an asset's required return from one thing only: how strongly it moves with the whole market. Risk that can be diversified away earns nothing.

Professional definition

In equilibrium, an asset's expected excess return equals its beta times the market's expected excess return. Expected return is therefore the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium, so only non-diversifiable market-related risk is compensated and an asset's own total volatility is irrelevant to its price.

Technical definition

Derived from mean-variance investors holding the tangency portfolio plus a risk-free asset, which in equilibrium forces the tangency portfolio to be the market portfolio. The security market line then plots expected return against beta, with intercept the risk-free rate and slope the market premium. Its empirical record is poor: the realised relation between beta and average return is flatter than predicted, and size, value and momentum sorts produce return spreads beta does not explain. Whether that refutes the model or only the market proxy used to test it is disputed.

Academic definition

An equilibrium single-factor pricing model in which an asset's expected excess return is proportional to its covariance with the market portfolio scaled by market variance, so that only systematic risk is priced.

Mechanism

If every investor optimises on the same means and covariances and can lend or borrow freely at one rate, they all want the same risky portfolio, which must therefore be the market itself. Diversifiable risk is then costless to remove, so nobody is paid for bearing it, and the only compensated quantity left is an asset's covariance with that market portfolio.

In practice

Who uses it
Valuation analysts estimating a discount rate, performance evaluators defining a required return, and corporate finance teams setting a cost of equity.
Why it matters
It produces a single auditable required-return figure from two observable inputs and one estimated slope, which is why it survives in practice despite its empirical problems.
Limitations
The market portfolio is unobservable, betas are estimated with error and drift over time, and the realised security market line is flatter than the model predicts.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a regression beta from one short window as the asset's true beta.
  • Reading a positive CAPM alpha as skill without testing other factor exposures.
  • Calling an equity index 'the market portfolio' and ignoring every other asset.

Examples

numeric

With a risk-free rate of 4% and an expected market return of 9%, the market premium is 5 points. An asset with beta 1.3 has a CAPM required return of 10.5%; one with beta 0.6 requires 7.0%.

Given
  • risk-free rate Rf = 4%
  • expected market return E(Rm) = 9%
  • beta of asset A = 1.3; beta of asset B = 0.6
Steps
  1. market risk premium = 9% - 4% = 5%
  2. asset A: 4% + 1.3 x 5% = 4% + 6.5% = 10.5%
  3. asset B: 4% + 0.6 x 5% = 4% + 3.0% = 7.0%
required returns of 10.5% and 7.0%

These are equilibrium required returns under the model's assumptions, not forecasts of what either asset will deliver. Both numbers inherit all the error in the estimated beta and premium.

market

Two holdings have the same total volatility but different betas. The model says the one that moves with the market carries the compensated risk, while the other's volatility is diversifiable and so earns no premium — a prediction the data support only weakly.

Mathematical foundation

E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i \left[ E(R_m) - R_f \right], \qquad \beta_i = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\mathrm{Var}(R_m)} = \frac{\sigma_{i,m}}{\sigma_m^2}

Procedure

Take a risk-free rate for the horizon in question. Estimate beta, usually by regressing the asset's excess returns on a market proxy's excess returns over a chosen window. Form the market premium as expected market return minus the risk-free rate. Multiply beta by that premium and add the risk-free rate. Every input except the risk-free rate is an estimate, and the result inherits their errors.

Worked example

Given
  • Rf = 4% per year
  • E(Rm) = 9% per year
  • beta = 1.3
Steps
  1. market risk premium = E(Rm) - Rf = 9% - 4% = 5%
  2. beta x premium = 1.3 x 5% = 6.5%
  3. E(Ri) = 4% + 6.5%
E(Ri) = 10.5% per year

Interpretation

The output is the return an asset must be expected to offer, under the model, to be held in equilibrium. A high figure means high non-diversifiable risk, not a better asset; and because the inputs are estimates, two analysts can defend materially different numbers for the same security.

Assumptions

  • Investors are mean-variance optimisers with homogeneous expectations over a single period.
  • Unlimited lending and borrowing at the same risk-free rate; no taxes, no transaction costs; all assets marketable and infinitely divisible; investors are price takers.
  • Beta is estimated by regression against a market proxy, so the figure depends on the proxy, the return frequency and the estimation window. Different reasonable choices give materially different betas.
Limits of the formula
  • Empirically the security market line is flatter than predicted: low-beta assets have historically earned more, and high-beta assets less, than the model implies.
  • Portfolios sorted on size, book-to-market and past return show average return differences that beta does not explain, which is the motivation for multifactor models.
  • The true market portfolio includes every risky asset and is unobservable, so any test of the model is also a test of the proxy chosen for it; this ambiguity is why the model's rejection remains disputed rather than settled.

Commonly confused with

Beta

Beta is a statistic: the estimated slope of an asset's return on the market's. The model is the separate claim that this slope alone determines required return — a claim the data treat far less kindly than the statistic.
