Factor Investing

Simple definition

Building a portfolio around measurable shared characteristics of assets — such as size, valuation or past return — rather than around individual names, on the argument that those characteristics explain returns.

Professional definition

Deliberate, sized exposure to systematic return characteristics documented in the cross-section of returns, implemented by rules rather than name-by-name selection. The factors are defined by sorting rules, their premia are estimated from historical samples, and whether those premia are compensation for risk or evidence of mispricing is unresolved.

Technical definition

A multifactor extension of single-factor pricing: an asset's excess return is regressed on a market factor plus long-short portfolios built by sorting on characteristics, leaving an intercept and a residual. Factor premia are sample statistics with wide standard errors, and several have been materially weaker in the periods after publication. Factor definitions are not standardised, so two providers can report different exposures for the same portfolio. The risk-versus-mispricing question cannot be settled by the regression, because both readings fit the same coefficients.

Academic definition

A portfolio construction approach targeting priced sources of common variation identified in the cross-section of asset returns, estimated through multifactor regressions of excess returns on characteristic-sorted portfolios.

Mechanism

Sorting assets by a characteristic and holding the extremes as a long-short portfolio isolates the return difference associated with that characteristic. If the difference persists, it behaves like a factor: a common source of variation that explains part of many assets' returns at once. Whether holders are paid for bearing a risk or for exploiting an error is not visible in the returns themselves.

In practice

Who uses it
Quantitative managers, index providers, allocation committees evaluating funds, and researchers decomposing a portfolio's return into exposures.
Why it matters
It turns vague style labels into measurable exposures, so a portfolio's tilts can be reported, compared across managers and charged against a risk budget.
Limitations
Premia are estimated rather than known, several weakened after publication, and crowding, implementation cost and definitional differences all reduce what a live portfolio captures.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a historical factor premium as an expected return.
  • Comparing two funds' loadings computed from different factor definitions.
  • Adding factors until the in-sample fit is high, which is overfitting.

Examples

numeric

A fund's estimated loadings are 0.95 on the market, 0.30 on size and 0.40 on value. With assumed annual premia of 5%, 2% and 3%, the model implies 6.55% of excess return; a realised 7.0% leaves 0.45 points unexplained.

Given
  • loadings: market 0.95, size 0.30, value 0.40
  • assumed annual factor premia: market 5%, size 2%, value 3%
  • realised excess return: 7.0%
Steps
  1. market term = 0.95 x 5% = 4.75%
  2. size term = 0.30 x 2% = 0.60%
  3. value term = 0.40 x 3% = 1.20%
  4. model-implied excess return = 6.55%
  5. unexplained residual = 7.0% - 6.55% = 0.45%
6.55% explained by factor exposures, 0.45 points left as the intercept

The premia here are assumptions for illustration, not estimates of future returns. The residual is model-dependent: adding a factor that the fund loads on would shrink it, possibly to zero.

market

A characteristic sort that produced a wide return spread in the sample behind its publication produced a much narrower one afterwards. Crowding, a changed regime and an originally overstated estimate all fit that pattern, and the return series alone cannot separate them.

Mathematical foundation

R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} = \alpha_i + b_i \left(R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}\right) + s_i \, \mathrm{SMB}_t + h_i \, \mathrm{HML}_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}

Variables

R_{i,t} - R_{f,t}Excess return of asset or portfolio i in period t over the risk-free rate% per period
\alpha_iIntercept: the average excess return left unexplained by the stated factors% per period
b_iLoading on the market factordimensionless
\mathrm{SMB}_tReturn of a portfolio long small-capitalisation and short large-capitalisation assets% per period
\mathrm{HML}_tReturn of a portfolio long high book-to-market and short low book-to-market assets% per period
s_i, h_iLoadings on the size and value factorsdimensionless
\varepsilon_{i,t}Period-by-period residual, assumed to have zero mean% per period

Procedure

Collect excess returns for the asset and for each factor over the same periods. Regress the asset's excess return on the factor returns by ordinary least squares. The slopes are the exposures and the intercept is what the chosen factors do not explain. Multiplying each slope by an assumed future premium gives a model-implied expected excess return.

Worked example

Given
  • estimated loadings: b = 0.95, s = 0.30, h = 0.40
  • assumed premia: market 5%, SMB 2%, HML 3% per year
Steps
  1. 0.95 x 5% = 4.75%
  2. 0.30 x 2% = 0.60%
  3. 0.40 x 3% = 1.20%
  4. sum = 6.55%
model-implied expected excess return of 6.55% per year for this exposure set

Interpretation

The loadings describe what the portfolio is exposed to, which is a fact about its holdings. The implied return is only as good as the assumed premia, which are the contested part: a loading is measured, a premium is forecast.

Assumptions

  • Factor returns are those of specific long-short sorted portfolios; SMB and HML as above are one published construction, and other providers sort differently on size, value, momentum, quality and low volatility.
  • Ordinary least squares with constant loadings over the window. Loadings in fact drift, and rolling estimates differ from full-sample estimates.
  • A positive intercept is unexplained return relative to the chosen factor set only, not evidence of skill in any absolute sense.
Limits of the formula
  • Premia are estimated from historical samples and carry wide confidence intervals; several published factors have been weaker out of sample and after publication.
  • The regression is agnostic between a risk interpretation and a mispricing interpretation, so it cannot establish why a premium exists.
  • Repeated searching across characteristics inflates apparent significance, which is why data-snooping controls matter as much as the regression itself.

Commonly confused with

Alpha

Measured against a single-factor benchmark, a persistent factor tilt shows up as alpha. Add the factor to the model and the alpha shrinks. What looks like skill can simply be an unmodelled exposure.
