Mean Reversion

Simple definition

The tendency of a series to be pulled back towards a long-run average after moving away from it. The further it strays, the stronger the pull.

Professional definition

A dynamic in which the expected next change is proportional to the current distance from a stable long-run level, with the opposite sign. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is the standard continuous-time model, and its speed of reversion can be summarised as a half-life.

Technical definition

In the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck specification the drift is theta times the gap between the long-run level and the current value, so the gap decays exponentially and the half-life is the natural log of two divided by theta. Its discrete analogue is an AR(1) with coefficient phi, where theta equals minus the natural log of phi. Mean reversion requires stationarity: at phi equal to one there is a unit root, no long-run level and no half-life. Reversion is horizon-specific: the same series can revert at one frequency and trend at another.

Academic definition

A stochastic dynamic in which the conditional expected increment is negatively proportional to the deviation from a stationary long-run level, formalised in continuous time by the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.

Mechanism

A restoring force makes the expected move depend on position rather than on momentum: the larger the deviation, the larger the expected correction. Exponential decay follows, which is why a single parameter — the half-life — summarises the whole dynamic. Nothing in the model guarantees the long-run level is constant; if it drifts, the apparent reversion was a level shift seen from inside.

In practice

Who uses it
Pairs and spread traders, market makers managing inventory, volatility traders, and anyone modelling a stationary spread rather than a price.
Why it matters
The half-life sets the holding period, the stop distance and the position size, so it turns a qualitative idea into a specification.
Limitations
Reversion holds only while the series stays stationary. A regime change breaks the long-run level and the trade becomes an open-ended loss with no reversion to wait for.

Common mistakes

  • Fitting the reversion rule on the same data used to find it.
  • Assuming a spread that reverted will keep reverting after a structural break.
  • Holding far beyond the estimated half-life because the gap widened.

Examples

numeric

An AR(1) fitted to a daily spread gives phi = 0.95. Then theta is 0.0513 per day and the half-life is 13.5 days: half of any current deviation is expected to be gone in under three trading weeks.

Given
  • AR(1) coefficient phi = 0.95 on daily data
  • zero-mean deviations from the fitted long-run level
Steps
  1. theta = -ln(0.95) = 0.051293 per day
  2. half-life = ln(2) / theta = 0.693147 / 0.051293
  3. = 13.513 days
half-life = 13.5 trading days

Verified arithmetic. The half-life does not depend on how far the series currently is from its mean — only on the speed parameter. At phi = 1 the logarithm is zero and the half-life is undefined.

pitfall

A ratio between two assets reverts cleanly on daily data for two years, so a rule is written around it. On weekly data over a decade the same ratio trends steadily. Reversion at one horizon is not reversion at another.

Procedure

Work on a series you have first tested for stationarity — usually a spread or a ratio, not a price. Regress the current value on its own previous value to estimate phi. Convert to a reversion speed with theta equal to minus the log of phi, then divide the log of two by theta for the half-life. Test the rule on data held back from the estimation.

Worked example

Given
  • daily AR(1) fit gives phi = 0.95
  • series tested stationary over the estimation window
Steps
  1. theta = -ln(0.95) = 0.051293 per day
  2. half-life = ln(2) / 0.051293 = 0.693147 / 0.051293
  3. = 13.513 days
half-life = 13.5 trading days

Interpretation

The half-life is the natural holding horizon implied by the fit. A deviation is expected to halve over that span, not to disappear, and the expectation is an average over paths rather than a schedule for any one of them.

Assumptions

  • Mean reversion presupposes stationarity: the long-run level and the reversion speed are assumed constant over the window.
  • The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model assumes Gaussian shocks of constant volatility, which return data violate through volatility clustering.
  • Phi and therefore the half-life are estimates with standard errors; a phi near one gives a half-life estimate that is extremely imprecise.
Limits of the formula
  • Horizon-dependent: a series can mean-revert at one sampling frequency and trend at another, so a half-life is meaningful only for the frequency it was fitted on.
  • Designing and testing a reversion rule on the same sample is data snooping, and the apparent edge usually does not survive out-of-sample.
  • A structural break removes the level the trade was betting on, converting a bounded-looking position into an unbounded loss.

Commonly confused with

Pullback

A pullback is a visible retracement inside a trend. Mean reversion is a statistical property of a stationary series with an estimable speed, not a chart observation.

Commonly confused with

Pairs Trading

Pairs trading is one application: it constructs a spread expected to be stationary and then trades its reversion. Mean reversion is the property; the pair is the vehicle.
