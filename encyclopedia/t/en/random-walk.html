Simple definition

A path built by adding an unpredictable step to the last value each period. Where it goes next does not depend on how it got here.

Professional definition

A process whose increments are independent and identically distributed, so the level is the running sum of those increments. Variance grows in proportion to the number of steps, and the best forecast of the next level is the current level plus any drift.

Technical definition

In discrete time, a level that is the cumulative sum of i.i.d. shocks, optionally with a constant drift. Independence constrains the whole conditional distribution, not just its mean, so a random walk forbids predictable volatility as well as predictable direction. A martingale imposes only that the conditional mean equals the current level; it permits predictable variance and higher moments. Every driftless i.i.d. walk with finite mean is a martingale, but most martingales are not random walks.

Academic definition

A stochastic process formed as the partial-sum sequence of independent, identically distributed increments, characterised by unit-root behaviour and variance linear in the horizon.

Mechanism

Because each step is independent of every earlier one, nothing in the history carries forecasting content and the shocks accumulate without ever being pulled back. Variance therefore rises with the horizon rather than settling, which is why a walk wanders far from where it started and why long swings in such a series are not evidence of a trend.

In practice

Who uses it
Econometricians testing predictability, derivatives modellers needing a baseline price process, and anyone building a benchmark against which a signal must beat chance.
Why it matters
It is the honest null: if a strategy cannot beat a simulated walk with the same drift and volatility, its results are consistent with luck.
Limitations
Real return series show volatility clustering and fat tails, both of which a true random walk forbids, so the model is a baseline rather than a description.

Common mistakes

  • Treating random walk and martingale as the same statement.
  • Reading long stretches of one direction as evidence against randomness.
  • Scaling volatility by the square root of time in a series with clustering.

Examples

numeric

Daily log returns have a standard deviation of 2% and are treated as independent. Over 25 trading days the standard deviation is 2% times the square root of 25, that is 10%. Doubling the horizon multiplies risk by about 1.41, not by 2.

Given
  • daily standard deviation sigma = 2%
  • k = 25 periods
  • increments assumed independent and identically distributed
Steps
  1. Var over k periods = k x sigma^2 = 25 x 0.0004 = 0.01
  2. standard deviation = sqrt(0.01) = 0.10
25-day standard deviation = 10%

This square-root scaling is a consequence of independence. With volatility clustering the increments are dependent in squares and the rule understates risk at some horizons.

research

A series passes a test for unpredictable direction but its squared returns are strongly autocorrelated. It is consistent with a martingale and inconsistent with a random walk — the mean is unforecastable while the variance is not.

Mathematical foundation

P_t = P_{t-1} + \mu + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, \sigma^2) \qquad \text{versus} \qquad \mathbb{E}\!\left[P_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right] = P_t

Procedure

To test the walk, check that increments are independent: look for autocorrelation in the increments and also in their squares and absolute values. To test the martingale property, test only whether the conditional mean of the next increment is zero. Passing the second says nothing about the first.

Worked example

Given
  • sigma = 2% per day
  • mu = 0
  • k = 25 days, increments i.i.d.
Steps
  1. Var(P_(t+25) - P_t) = 25 x (0.02)^2 = 0.01
  2. sd = 0.10
10% standard deviation over 25 days

Interpretation

The left-hand model is a statement about the entire distribution of increments; the right-hand condition is only about their conditional mean. Conflating them turns a weak, widely plausible claim about unforecastable direction into a strong, empirically false claim about unforecastable risk.

Assumptions

  • The i.i.d. form assumes a constant increment variance. Allowing the variance to depend on the past breaks the random walk while leaving the martingale property intact.
  • Mu is a drift in the level, so a walk with non-zero drift is not a martingale in the level even though its deviations from trend may be.
  • Fama's review discusses the related submartingale and fair-game formulations; these are weaker than the i.i.d. walk and should not be cited as the same model.
Limits of the formula
  • The square-root-of-time scaling of risk follows from independence alone and fails when increments are dependent, which real return series are in their squares.
  • A finite sample cannot distinguish a random walk from a slowly mean-reverting process with any confidence, so unit-root test outcomes should not be read as settled facts about the process.

Stochastic Process

The mathematical definition is not in dispute; the claim that asset prices follow a random walk is, which is why the claim type is contested. The entry keeps the random walk and the martingale strictly apart: the first restricts the whole conditional distribution, the second only its mean, so every driftless integrable i.i.d. walk is a martingale but not conversely.
