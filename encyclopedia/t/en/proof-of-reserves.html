Proof of Reserves

Simple definition

A published check meant to show that a platform really holds the assets it says it holds. On its own it does not show what the platform owes.

Professional definition

A disclosure in which a platform evidences control of assets at a point in time, often by proving address control and letting users verify their balance is included in a committed data structure. Without an equally verified statement of liabilities it demonstrates holdings, not solvency.

Technical definition

Three limitations are structural, not incidental. First, assets without liabilities prove very little: holdings of any size are consistent with a shortfall if obligations exceed them. Second, almost all published proofs are point-in-time, so they say nothing about the moment before or after, and borrowed assets can satisfy a snapshot. Third, an attestation is not an audit: it is a report on specified procedures at specified scope, not an opinion on financial statements, and it carries no assurance about internal control, going concern or undisclosed encumbrance.

Academic definition

A voluntary cryptographic and procedural disclosure evidencing asset control at a reference time, whose solvency relevance is conditional on an independently verified and contemporaneous statement of liabilities.

Mechanism

A balance sheet has two sides and solvency is the comparison between them. Cryptography can prove one side cheaply, because address control is verifiable by anyone, while the other side exists only in the platform's own books. So the easy half gets published and the hard half does not, and a disclosure that looks rigorous can be silent on the question that matters.

In practice

Who uses it
Platforms publishing attestations, the firms performing them, users trying to assess counterparty risk, and supervisors assessing disclosure quality.
Why it matters
It is the main voluntary disclosure users are offered about a custodian's holdings, so knowing what it does not cover is more useful than knowing what it does.
Limitations
Assets without liabilities do not evidence solvency; point-in-time snapshots can be arranged; an attestation gives no audit opinion. Mandatory requirements, where they exist, differ by jurisdiction.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a reserves attestation as a solvency statement or an audit.
  • Treating a single snapshot as continuous assurance about holdings.
  • Assuming inclusion of your own balance proves the aggregate is complete.

Examples

regulatory

A platform publishes verified control of large holdings and no liability statement. The disclosure is accurate and still compatible with insolvency, because nothing published addresses what customers are owed.

numeric

Reserves of 1,000 look reassuring. If customer claims are 1,200, coverage is 83 per cent and the shortfall is 200. The same reserves figure with claims of 900 means coverage of 111 per cent. Reserves alone carry no information.

Given
  • Attested reserves = 1,000 units
  • Case A: customer claims = 1,200 units
  • Case B: customer claims = 900 units
Steps
  1. Case A coverage = 1,000 / 1,200 = 0.833
  2. Case A shortfall = 1,200 - 1,000 = 200
  3. Case B coverage = 1,000 / 900 = 1.111
83% coverage or 111% coverage from the same attested reserves

Illustrative figures. The point is that the attested number is identical in both cases: without a verified contemporaneous liabilities figure, the reserves disclosure cannot distinguish solvency from a 200-unit hole.

Commonly confused with

Solvency

Solvency compares assets with liabilities. Proof of reserves usually evidences only the asset side, so the two are not interchangeable.

Commonly confused with

Smart Contract Audit

A code audit examines a contract's behaviour. A reserves attestation examines holdings at a date. Neither is a financial-statement audit.

JURISDICTION WARNING: rules differ by country and change. Proof of reserves is in most places a voluntary disclosure rather than a legal requirement; where a regime does mandate reporting on client assets, its content and frequency are set by that jurisdiction's own law and are not described here. The three limitations stated — assets without liabilities prove very little, published proofs are typically point-in-time, and an attestation is not an audit — are the honest core of this entry and are general observations about the form of the disclosure, not claims about any named platform. No published proof of reserves by any platform is assessed or endorsed. Figures in the numeric example are illustrative. Kurdish headword is this project's proposal and needs native-expert review.
