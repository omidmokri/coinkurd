Simple definition

A private agreement to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a fixed future date. Nothing changes hands today, and both sides are bound to settle later.

Professional definition

A bilateral, customisable contract to transact an asset at an agreed price on an agreed date. Unlike a futures contract it is not exchange-traded or centrally cleared, so quantity and date are negotiated, daily margining is often absent, and each side carries the other's default risk until settlement.

Technical definition

An over-the-counter obligation whose value at inception is normally zero, because the delivery price is set equal to the prevailing forward price. Afterwards its value moves with the spot price and with the financing cost of the remaining term. With no clearing house and frequently no variation margin, exposure accumulates unrealised until maturity; that credit asymmetry, rather than the payoff shape, is the substantive difference from a cleared future.

Academic definition

A bilateral derivative obligating one party to buy and the other to deliver a specified asset at a predetermined price on a predetermined date, struck at the price that makes the contract's initial net present value zero.

Mechanism

The forward price is not a forecast. It is the spot price carried to the delivery date at the cost of financing, less any income the asset pays. If it drifted away from that, a trader could borrow cash, buy the asset now and lock in the sale, so arbitrage pins the level. Customisation is paid for with counterparty risk.

In practice

Who uses it
Exporters, importers, commodity producers, treasury desks and OTC crypto desks that need an exact quantity and date no listed contract offers.
Why it matters
It fixes a future transaction price to the unit and day actually needed, removing price uncertainty from a planned purchase or sale.
Limitations
No clearing house, so default risk is real; positions are illiquid, hard to value independently, and usually closable only by agreeing an offsetting contract.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a forward as cancellable because no money moved at inception.
  • Hedging more than the real underlying exposure.
  • Ignoring the counterparty's creditworthiness over the contract's life.

Examples

market

An exporter expecting payment in dollars in six months sells dollars forward to its bank today at an agreed rate. The invoice is now insulated from the exchange rate, but if the bank fails before settlement the hedge fails with it.

numeric

Spot is 100, financing is 5% a year and the asset pays no income, so the one-year forward is struck near 105.13. If spot is 112 at maturity, the long side gains 6.87 per unit; if spot is 100, it loses 5.13.

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • r = 5% a year, continuously compounded
  • q = 0 (no income or storage)
  • T = 1 year
  • K set equal to F_0 at inception
Steps
  1. F_0 = 100 x e^(0.05 x 1) = 105.13
  2. K = 105.13
  3. Payoff if S_T = 112: 112 - 105.13 = 6.87
  4. Payoff if S_T = 100: 100 - 105.13 = -5.13
K = 105.13; long payoff is +6.87 per unit at S_T = 112 and -5.13 at S_T = 100

The forward price exceeds spot only because financing costs more than the asset yields. It is not a prediction that spot will rise.

Procedure

Take the spot price, carry it forward at the net cost of holding the asset to the delivery date, and write that figure in as the delivery price so neither side pays the other at inception. At maturity the buyer's gain or loss per unit is simply the spot price minus that written price.

Worked example

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • r = 0.05 per year, continuous
  • q = 0
  • T = 1
Steps
  1. e^(0.05) = 1.0513
  2. F_0 = 100 x 1.0513 = 105.13
  3. K = 105.13
  4. S_T = 112 gives 112 - 105.13
F_0 = K = 105.13; long payoff +6.87 per unit at S_T = 112

Interpretation

The formula prices the contract off arbitrage, not off opinion: it is what the asset costs to hold until delivery. A positive payoff for the long means spot finished above the carried cost, which is a different statement from the asset having performed well.

Assumptions

  • Continuous compounding, a single known financing rate and a known income yield q over the whole term.
  • No storage cost beyond what is netted into q, no transaction costs, and free short selling of the underlying.
  • No default by either side and no variation margin; with stochastic interest rates a forward price and a futures price on the same underlying are not identical.
Limits of the formula
  • For commodities the relationship needs explicit storage costs and a convenience yield, which is not directly observable and is usually inferred from the curve itself.
  • The payoff formula values the contract at maturity only; before maturity the mark-to-market value also depends on the remaining financing cost.

Commonly confused with

Futures Contract

Same economic promise, different plumbing. A future is standardised, exchange-traded, centrally cleared and margined daily; a forward is negotiated and settles once, with default risk left between the two parties.
