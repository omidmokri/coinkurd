Distributed Ledger

Simple definition

A record of who owns what and what was transferred, kept as identical copies by many independent computers, so no single operator can quietly change it.

Professional definition

A database replicated across independent operators that agree, by protocol rather than by trust, on one ordered set of entries. A blockchain is one way to build one; batching entries into hash-linked blocks is a design choice, not a requirement.

Technical definition

A replicated, append-only record maintained by nodes that apply the same validation rules to the same inputs and therefore converge on the same state. Three properties are separable: replication, who holds copies; validation, who checks the rules; and governance, who may change them. A permissioned ledger can be replicated and validated while governance stays with one consortium, which is why replication alone does not make a ledger trust-minimised.

Academic definition

A data structure maintained redundantly by mutually independent participants, in which a consensus protocol establishes a single agreed ordering of state transitions without reliance on a central record-keeper.

Mechanism

Agreement is not produced by copying; it is produced by every honest node rejecting the same invalid inputs. Identical rules plus identical history give identical state. The hard part is ordering: when two valid updates conflict, the consensus rule, not the data structure, decides which one the ledger keeps.

In practice

Who uses it
Settlement and custody teams, auditors reconciling records across firms, protocol engineers, and analysts who read the ledger directly as a data source.
Why it matters
It removes the single record-keeper, so parties who do not trust each other can still settle against one shared history.
Limitations
It proves what was recorded and in what order. It does not prove the entry was lawful or accurate, or that an address belongs to a named person.

Common mistakes

  • Calling any replicated database a distributed ledger when one party still controls the rules.
  • Reading immutability as mathematical rather than economic.
  • Assuming every participant validates, when most trust a provider's node.

Examples

everyday

Five shopkeepers each keep the same notebook of debts between them and read every new entry aloud before writing it down. No one needs a trusted bookkeeper, but a rule is still needed for who writes first when two speak at once.

crypto

A consortium ledger between banks replicates data across members but keeps rule changes with the consortium. A public chain replicates data and distributes validation and governance. Both are distributed ledgers; their trust assumptions are not comparable.

Commonly confused with

Blockchain

A blockchain is a distributed ledger built from hash-linked blocks. A distributed ledger need not use blocks or a chain at all.
