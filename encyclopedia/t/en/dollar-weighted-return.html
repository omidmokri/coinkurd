Dollar-Weighted Return

Simple definition

The return an investor actually experienced, which depends on how much money was invested at each moment. Add money just before a fall and your return is worse than the fund's.

Professional definition

A money-weighted return: the internal rate of return on the investor's own cash flows, including every contribution and withdrawal plus the ending value. It differs from the time-weighted return, which strips out the effect of flows and measures the manager's result; the gap between the two is the cost of the investor's own timing.

Technical definition

The money-weighted return is the single rate that sets the present value of all contributions, withdrawals and the terminal value to zero, so periods with more capital at work carry more weight. The time-weighted return instead chains sub-period returns, giving each period equal weight whatever the amount invested. Reporting practice uses the time-weighted figure to judge managers, who do not control flows, and the money-weighted figure to describe what an investor earned. Both are correct answers to different questions.

Academic definition

The internal rate of return of an investor's net cash-flow stream including terminal value, as distinguished from the geometrically chained time-weighted return, which is invariant to the size and timing of external flows.

Mechanism

A chained return multiplies period returns together, so each period counts once however much money was present. An internal rate of return weights each period by the capital exposed in it. When the largest contribution lands just before the worst period, the money-weighted figure falls below the chained one, and the whole difference is attributable to when the money arrived.

In practice

Who uses it
Investors judging their own result, advisers reporting to clients, and analysts separating a manager's performance from a client's cash flows.
Why it matters
It answers the question the investor actually has — what happened to my money — which a fund's reported return does not answer.
Limitations
An internal rate of return can be undefined or have several solutions when net flows change sign repeatedly, and it is not comparable between investors with different flow patterns.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a personal money-weighted return with a fund's published time-weighted figure as if they measured the same thing.
  • Blaming a manager for a gap that the investor's own contribution timing created.
  • Treating an internal rate of return as additive across accounts.

Examples

numeric

A fund gains 50% in year one and loses 30% in year two: a time-weighted return of plus 5% over the two years. An investor holding 1,000 at the start who added 9,000 after the gain ends with 7,350 on 10,000 contributed — about minus 24.6% a year.

Given
  • fund returns: year 1 +50%, year 2 -30%
  • investor contributes 1,000 at t = 0 and 9,000 at t = 1
  • no withdrawals before the end of year 2
Steps
  1. value at t = 1 before the contribution = 1,000 x 1.50 = 1,500
  2. invested at start of year 2 = 1,500 + 9,000 = 10,500
  3. terminal value = 10,500 x 0.70 = 7,350
  4. time-weighted: 1.50 x 0.70 = 1.05, so +5.0% over two years, 2.47% a year
  5. money-weighted: solve -1,000 - 9,000/(1+r) + 7,350/(1+r)^2 = 0, giving 1+r = 0.7536
time-weighted +2.47% a year against money-weighted -24.6% a year, same fund and same two years

The fund did not lose money over the period and the investor did. Nothing is inconsistent: the gap is the measured cost of contributing 9,000 immediately before the losing year.

everyday

Two people hold the same fund over the same two years, and the fund reports one number for both. The one who invested at the start and the one who added after a rally did not earn the same return, and at most one of them earned the reported figure.

Mathematical foundation

\sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{\left(1 + r_{MW}\right)^{t}} = 0, \qquad 1 + R_{TW} = \prod_{k=1}^{m} \left(1 + R_k\right)

Variables

CF_tNet cash flow at time t: contributions negative, withdrawals positive, and the terminal value entered as a positive final flowcurrency
r_{MW}Money-weighted (dollar-weighted) rate of return per perioddecimal rate per period
nIndex of the final cash flowperiods
R_kReturn of sub-period k, measured between two consecutive valuationsdecimal return
R_{TW}Time-weighted return over all m sub-periods, independent of the amounts investeddecimal return
mNumber of sub-periods, one between each pair of valuationscount

Procedure

For the money-weighted figure, list every external cash flow with its date and sign, add the ending value as a final positive flow, and solve numerically for the rate that makes the present value zero. For the time-weighted figure, value the portfolio at every external flow, compute each sub-period return, and multiply the growth factors together.

Worked example

Given
  • CF_0 = -1,000; CF_1 = -9,000; CF_2 = +7,350
  • sub-period returns: R_1 = +50%, R_2 = -30%
Steps
  1. let x = 1 + r; the equation becomes -1,000 x^2 - 9,000 x + 7,350 = 0
  2. divide by -50: 20 x^2 + 180 x - 147 = 0
  3. x = (-180 + sqrt(32,400 + 11,760)) / 40 = (-180 + 210.14) / 40 = 0.7536
  4. r_MW = 0.7536 - 1 = -0.2464
  5. 1 + R_TW = 1.50 x 0.70 = 1.05, so R_TW = +5.0% cumulative and 2.47% a year
money-weighted -24.6% a year against time-weighted +2.47% a year

Interpretation

The time-weighted figure describes the investment; the money-weighted figure describes the investor. A persistent gap between them is a measurement of that investor's flow timing, and it is the part of the result the manager never controlled.

Assumptions

  • Flows here sit at period boundaries and the rate is annual. Mid-period flows need either a shorter compounding interval or a day-weighted approximation, and the conventions give slightly different figures.
  • The time-weighted return requires a valuation at each external flow. Where those valuations do not exist, practice substitutes a day-weighted approximation of the money-weighted return.
  • Both measures are returns only; neither adjusts for the risk taken to produce them.
Limits of the formula
  • An internal rate of return need not be unique when net flows change sign more than once, and for some flow patterns no real solution exists.
  • Money-weighted returns cannot be compared between investors or averaged across accounts, because each reflects its own flow pattern.
  • A money-weighted return below a time-weighted one is evidence about timing, not about the quality of the holdings.

Commonly confused with

Internal Rate of Return

The internal rate of return is the general technique for finding a single rate from a cash-flow series. A dollar-weighted return is that technique applied to an investor's own contributions, withdrawals and ending value.
