VWAP Execution

Simple definition

Slicing an order in proportion to how much the market is expected to trade in each interval, so your fills land near the day's volume-weighted price.

Professional definition

A participation schedule built on a forecast volume profile: each interval's share of the parent order matches that interval's expected share of volume. Performance is reported as signed slippage, in basis points, against the window's realised volume-weighted average price.

Technical definition

The VWAP indicator and the VWAP execution benchmark share one formula and are used for different things: on a chart it is a reference level a trader looks at, in execution it is the yardstick a desk is judged by. A VWAP algorithm is therefore only as good as its volume forecast, and it is self-referential — a sufficiently large order is part of the volume that sets the benchmark it is measured against, which flatters the report without reducing the cost.

Academic definition

An execution schedule allocating child quantities in proportion to forecast interval volume shares, evaluated by the signed deviation of the realised average fill price from the interval's volume-weighted average price.

Mechanism

Participating in proportion to volume means trading most when depth is deepest, which is where a given quantity costs least. The cost of that is a loss of control: if the forecast profile is wrong, or a shock moves volume into an interval the schedule had down as quiet, the algorithm chases size exactly when the book is worst.

In practice

Who uses it
Buy-side desks whose brokers are measured against VWAP, brokers offering the benchmark as a service, and systematic traders sizing into liquid pairs.
Why it matters
It is the market's own cost standard: filling at the volume-weighted price of the window is a defensible, recomputable outcome a client can check.
Limitations
It needs a volume forecast, it concedes timing entirely to the market, and a large enough order influences the benchmark it is scored against.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the chart VWAP line as the execution benchmark without matching window and venue.
  • Participating at a fixed rate through a liquidity vacuum.
  • Averaging interval VWAPs instead of recomputing from value and volume.

Examples

institutional

A broker is given a buy order and a VWAP mandate. It works ten percent of each interval's volume; the client later recomputes the window's VWAP from public prints and compares it with the average fill.

numeric

A 10,000-unit order against a forecast profile of 10, 30 and 60 percent is sliced 1,000 / 3,000 / 6,000. The fills average 100.05 against a realised VWAP of 100.00, so the buy cost 5 basis points.

Given
  • Q = 10000
  • forecast volume shares: 10%, 30%, 60%
  • average fill = 100.05
  • realised window VWAP = 100.00
  • side = buy
Steps
  1. slices = 0.10 x 10000 = 1000; 0.30 x 10000 = 3000; 0.60 x 10000 = 6000
  2. (100.05 - 100.00) / 100.00 = 0.0005
  3. 0.0005 x 10000 = 5
slippage versus VWAP = 5 bps (a cost)

If this order were itself a large share of window volume, part of the 100.00 benchmark is the order's own prints, and the 5 bps understates the true cost of acquiring the position.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Estimate the volume profile for the window, usually from an average of recent comparable sessions. Normalise it to shares summing to one and multiply by the parent quantity to get each interval's target. Trade toward that target with limit or market child orders, re-estimating as actual volume arrives, then compute realised VWAP from the window's prints and compare it with the quantity-weighted average fill.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 10000
  • forecast shares 0.10, 0.30, 0.60
  • average fill 100.05
  • window VWAP 100.00
  • buy
Steps
  1. q = 1000, 3000, 6000
  2. (100.05 - 100.00) / 100.00 = 0.0005
  3. x 10000 = 5
schedule 1000/3000/6000; slippage 5 bps

Interpretation

A positive slippage is paying up relative to where the market's size traded. Zero means the fill matched the market's own average; it does not mean the trade was free, because spread and fees are inside that average too.

Assumptions

  • The window, the venue set and the price convention must be identical for the schedule and the benchmark, or the comparison is meaningless.
  • The volume forecast is an estimate; most implementations re-weight intraday as realised volume deviates from it.
  • The benchmark includes the order's own prints. For orders that are a material share of window volume the measured slippage is biased toward zero.
Limits of the formula
  • It optimises against a benchmark, not against cost: a desk can hit VWAP exactly in a session where any patient trader would have done better.
  • Interval VWAPs cannot be averaged; the benchmark must be recomputed from accumulated value and volume.
