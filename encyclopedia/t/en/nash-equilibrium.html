Nash Equilibrium

Simple definition

A combination of choices where nobody can do better by changing their own choice alone. Everyone is doing the best they can, given what everyone else is doing.

Professional definition

A strategy profile in which each player's strategy is a best response to the others'. It is a stability condition on a profile, not a claim that the outcome is good, fair or reached by any process of learning.

Technical definition

A profile such that no player can raise their own payoff by a unilateral deviation. Allowing mixed strategies, every finite game has at least one, which is the content of Nash's existence result; the equilibrium need not be unique, need not be efficient, and may require randomisation that no participant can observe. Nothing in the definition explains how a profile is arrived at.

Academic definition

A strategy profile that is a fixed point of the best-response correspondence, so that each player's strategy maximises their payoff conditional on the strategies of all others.

Mechanism

The condition is purely local: it rules out one-player changes and says nothing about two players moving together. That is why outcomes everyone dislikes can be equilibria, and why coordinated deviation breaks them. Existence rests on allowing mixed strategies, so an equilibrium can be a prescription to randomise rather than a recommendation anyone would recognise as a decision.

In practice

Who uses it
Mechanism and auction designers, protocol security researchers, and anyone asking whether a participant can profit by behaving differently from the intended way.
Why it matters
It gives a precise test for the only question a designer can actually answer: is unilateral deviation profitable, and if so, by how much.
Limitations
It is silent on how an equilibrium is reached, which one occurs when there are several, and whether coalitions form. It also assumes the payoffs written down are the real ones.

Common mistakes

  • Calling an outcome a Nash equilibrium without checking every player's deviations.
  • Assuming the equilibrium is the efficient or fair outcome.
  • Ignoring that coordinated deviation by two parties is not excluded.

Examples

numeric

Against an opponent playing A with probability p, strategy X pays 5p minus 1 and strategy Y pays 2 minus p. The two are equal at p equal to one half, so only at that belief can a player mix between them in equilibrium.

Given
  • payoffs to X: 4 against A, -1 against B
  • payoffs to Y: 1 against A, 2 against B
  • opponent plays A with probability p
Steps
  1. E[X] = 4p - 1(1 - p) = 5p - 1
  2. E[Y] = 1p + 2(1 - p) = 2 - p
  3. set 5p - 1 = 2 - p, so 6p = 3
  4. p = 0.5
indifference at p = 0.5

Indifference is a condition on the opponent's mixing probability, not on the player's own preference. This is why mixed equilibria are solved from the other side of the table.

crypto

A fee market where every participant bids just above the lowest accepted bid can be an equilibrium: no single bidder gains by bidding lower, since they would not be included. It is stable without being cheap for anyone.

Procedure

Take the candidate profile. For each player in turn, hold everyone else fixed and check that no alternative strategy, pure or mixed, gives a higher payoff. If that holds for every player, the profile is an equilibrium. In mixed equilibria the usual route is to impose indifference across the strategies a player randomises over.

Worked example

Given
  • payoffs to X: (4, -1) against (A, B)
  • payoffs to Y: (1, 2) against (A, B)
Steps
  1. E[X] = 5p - 1
  2. E[Y] = 2 - p
  3. equate: 6p = 3
p = 0.5 is the mixing probability that makes the player indifferent

Interpretation

The inequality is a no-regret condition against one deviation at a time. Satisfying it means the profile can persist, not that it will be found, that it is the only one, or that it serves anyone's interest well.

Assumptions

  • Only unilateral deviations are considered. Joint deviation by two or more players is outside the definition and is treated by other, stronger solution concepts.
  • Existence in finite games requires that mixed strategies be admissible; restricted to pure strategies many games have no equilibrium.
  • Players are assumed to know the game and to maximise expected payoff, and the payoffs are assumed to capture what they actually care about.
Limits of the formula
  • Multiplicity is common and the concept does not select among equilibria, so it cannot predict the outcome of a game with several.
  • It is a static stability condition with no dynamics: it says nothing about learning, adjustment or how long a profile takes to emerge, if it ever does.

Commonly confused with

Schelling Point

A Nash equilibrium is a profile nobody wants to leave alone. A Schelling point is the reason participants arrive at one particular profile among several, which is exactly what the equilibrium condition cannot supply.
