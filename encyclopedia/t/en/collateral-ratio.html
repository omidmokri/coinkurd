Simple definition

Collateral value divided by what you owe. A ratio of two means your deposit is worth twice your loan; the closer it gets to the protocol's floor, the closer you are to being sold out.

Professional definition

The live coverage measure of a borrowing position: pledged collateral valued at the protocol's feed, over outstanding debt including accrued interest. Loan-to-value is the same information inverted, and a health factor is the ratio restated against the liquidation parameter so that one means the floor.

Technical definition

The ratio moves with three things, only one of which the borrower controls: the collateral price, the debt's accrued interest, and any change the protocol makes to the valuation of that collateral. With several collateral assets each is weighted by its own parameter, so a position can be liquidatable while its naive total coverage still looks ample. Because the inputs come from a price feed, the ratio the protocol acts on may differ from the one implied by market prices at that instant.

Academic definition

The ratio of feed-valued pledged collateral to outstanding obligation, serving as the state variable on which automated liquidation conditions are evaluated.

Mechanism

The ratio is the protocol's only view of the position, so everything the protocol does follows from it: how much more can be borrowed, whether a withdrawal is allowed, and whether a liquidator may step in. It falls for two reasons that feel very different to a borrower — the market moving and interest accruing — and only the second is predictable.

In practice

Who uses it
Borrowers monitoring a position, liquidation bots scanning for eligible accounts, and risk teams setting per-asset parameters.
Why it matters
It is the single number that decides whether a position is safe, can borrow more, or is open to liquidation.
Limitations
It is only as good as the price feed behind it, and a ratio computed from one blended total hides which specific collateral is the weak leg.

Common mistakes

  • Monitoring the ratio at the market price instead of the protocol's feed price.
  • Forgetting that accruing interest lowers the ratio with no price move at all.
  • Treating a comfortable ratio as permanent when parameters can be changed by governance.

Examples

numeric

Collateral of 10 units at 2,000 against a debt of 8,000 gives a ratio of 2.5, or a loan-to-value of 40 percent. If the price halves to 1,000 the ratio falls to 1.25 and the loan-to-value doubles to 80 percent.

Given
  • Q = 10 units
  • P = 2,000 then 1,000
  • D = 8,000
Steps
  1. CR at 2,000 = (10 x 2,000) / 8,000 = 2.50, LTV = 1 / 2.50 = 40%
  2. CR at 1,000 = (10 x 1,000) / 8,000 = 1.25, LTV = 1 / 1.25 = 80%
CR 2.50 becomes 1.25; LTV 40% becomes 80%.

Debt is held constant here. In practice accrued interest raises D, so the ratio falls a little further even at an unchanged price.

market

A position backed by two collateral assets shows a blended ratio well above the floor, yet the weaker asset's own parameter is already breached. The protocol acts on the weighted test, not on the comfortable headline figure.

Mathematical foundation

CR = \frac{Q\,P}{D}, \qquad LTV = \frac{D}{Q\,P} = \frac{1}{CR}

Procedure

Value every pledged asset at the protocol's feed price and add them up, then divide by the total debt valued the same way. For a multi-asset position, apply each asset's own weight before summing, because that weighted figure is what the liquidation test uses. Invert the result for loan-to-value.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 10
  • P = 2,000
  • D = 8,000
Steps
  1. QP = 20,000
  2. CR = 20,000 / 8,000 = 2.50
  3. LTV = 8,000 / 20,000 = 0.40
CR = 2.50, LTV = 40%.

Interpretation

A higher ratio means more room before forced sale, nothing more: it is a coverage measure, not a judgement about the borrower or the collateral's quality. Because the denominator grows with interest, a static position drifts towards the floor on its own.

Assumptions

  • Both legs are valued in one unit of account at the protocol's own feed, which may lag or differ from traded prices.
  • Single-asset form shown. Protocols that weight collateral apply an asset-specific factor to each pledged asset before the comparison, and the weighted ratio is the one that governs liquidation.
  • Some protocols publish a health factor instead; it is this ratio divided by the required minimum, so a value of one marks the same boundary.
Limits of the formula
  • The ratio is a point-in-time measure and says nothing about how fast it can fall; two positions at the same ratio can carry very different gap risk.
  • It cannot capture correlation: if collateral and debt assets move together, the ratio is more stable than the real exposure, and if they move apart, less.

Commonly confused with

Liquidation Threshold

The ratio is where the position is now. The threshold is the fixed level at which the protocol allows it to be liquidated.

Commonly confused with

Maintenance Margin

Maintenance margin is the equity floor on a leveraged derivatives position. The collateral ratio covers a spot loan and is not topped up by a call.
