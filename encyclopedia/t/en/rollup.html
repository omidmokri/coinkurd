Simple definition

A separate chain that processes transactions off the main chain but posts its data and proofs back to it, so the main chain stays the referee.

Professional definition

An execution layer that batches transactions off-chain and publishes their data, plus either a validity proof or a fraud-proof challenge window, to a base chain. Users get much cheaper execution while settlement and data availability stay anchored to the base layer.

Technical definition

Two families. Optimistic rollups post state roots with a challenge period in which anyone can prove a transition invalid, so safety rests on at least one honest, funded challenger and withdrawals must wait out the window. Validity rollups post a succinct proof that the transition is correct, so verification is immediate, at the cost of a proving system and its setup assumptions. In both, data availability binds: a proof of correctness is worth nothing if the inputs cannot be reconstructed.

Academic definition

A layer-two construction executing transactions outside the base chain while publishing sufficient data and either succinct validity proofs or fraud-proof challenges, in order to inherit the base chain's settlement guarantees.

Mechanism

Inheriting security means moving only the expensive part off-chain: execution leaves, while data and dispute resolution stay. That is the whole trick, and also the limit — a rollup can be no safer than the base chain's availability guarantees, and no faster to exit than its proof or challenge mechanism allows.

In practice

Who uses it
Application developers, bridge and wallet teams, traders paying execution fees, and risk teams assessing exit and upgrade assumptions.
Why it matters
It cuts execution cost by orders of magnitude while keeping the base chain as the court of final appeal for the state it reports.
Limitations
Many deployments keep privileged sequencers or upgrade keys, and an optimistic design's withdrawal delay is a real liquidity constraint rather than a formality.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a rollup bridge balance as base-chain custody.
  • Assuming a validity proof removes data-availability risk.
  • Ignoring sequencer centralisation and upgrade-key control.

Examples

numeric

A batch of 500 transactions shares one base-layer data posting. If that posting costs the equivalent of 50 single transactions, each user bears a tenth of a base-layer transaction's data cost instead of a whole one.

Given
  • batch = 500 transactions
  • data posting cost = 50 base-layer transaction equivalents (illustrative)
Steps
  1. cost per user = 50 / 500
  2. = 0.1 base-layer transaction equivalents
about one tenth of a base-layer transaction's data cost per user

Illustrative amortisation only. Real costs depend on compression, base-layer fee levels and proof costs, and carry no currency figure here.

crypto

On an optimistic design, a withdrawal to the base chain waits out the challenge window. Third-party liquidity providers will front the funds for a fee, which is a market service, not a protocol guarantee.

Commonly confused with

Layer 2

Layer two is the general category of systems settling to a base chain. A rollup is the specific kind that publishes its transaction data to that chain, which is what lets anyone reconstruct and challenge its state.
