Simple definition

A rule that makes the firm sending a transfer pass the sender's and receiver's details to the firm receiving it, so the information travels alongside the money.

Professional definition

The requirement that an originating service provider obtain, hold and transmit identifying information on the originator and beneficiary of a transfer to the receiving provider, and that the receiving provider obtain and hold it. Extended to virtual-asset transfers by FATF Recommendation 16.

Technical definition

The duty attaches to the obliged entity, not to the ledger entry, so it binds only where both ends are regulated providers. Transfers to or from wallets held by no provider fall outside provider-to-provider messaging and are instead handled by risk measures on the regulated side. Implementation requires an interoperable messaging channel separate from the chain, which creates the sunrise problem: the rule is in force in some jurisdictions and not others, so a compliant sender may have no compliant counterparty to send to.

Academic definition

The extension of originator and beneficiary information-transmission requirements from wire transfers to virtual-asset transfers, imposing reciprocal collection, retention and transmission duties on obliged intermediaries at both ends.

Mechanism

Wire transfers already carried payer data because banks exchange messages. A public chain carries no such field, so the information cannot ride the transaction; it must ride a parallel channel between the two providers. Every design consequence follows from that split: the need for shared protocols, the dependence on both ends being regulated, and the gap left by wallets no provider controls.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchange and custodian compliance teams, payment providers, supervisors, and vendors operating travel-rule messaging networks.
Why it matters
It determines whether a transfer can be sent at all, what data must accompany it, and when a receiving firm must hold funds pending information.
Limitations
Adoption is uneven, data formats and thresholds differ, and transfers involving wallets no provider controls are outside provider-to-provider messaging altogether.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting the data to be written on-chain; it travels on a separate channel.
  • Assuming every counterparty jurisdiction has implemented the rule to the same scope.
  • Treating a self-custody withdrawal as subject to the same provider-to-provider duty.

Examples

regulatory

A compliant provider prepares the required originator data and finds the destination platform operates where the rule has not been implemented, with no messaging channel to receive it. The transfer is lawful at one end and unaddressable at the other.

numeric

If providers in 60 of 100 relevant jurisdictions can exchange travel-rule messages, then only 36 per cent of random provider-to-provider corridors have both ends live. The rule binds each firm fully while covering a minority of routes.

Given
  • Share of relevant jurisdictions with operative travel-rule messaging = 0.60
  • Sending and receiving jurisdictions drawn independently
  • Corridor works only if both ends are operative
Steps
  1. P(both ends operative) = 0.60 x 0.60
  2. 0.60 x 0.60 = 0.36
36% of corridors have both ends operative

The 60 per cent figure is an arbitrary illustration, not a measured implementation rate, and independence between the two ends is a simplifying assumption. FATF's targeted update documents that implementation remains uneven without supporting this arithmetic.

JURISDICTION WARNING: rules differ by country and change. The travel rule exists as an FATF recommendation addressed to states; what binds any firm is the national law that implements it, and scope, thresholds, data fields and treatment of wallets held by no provider all differ between implementing jurisdictions. No monetary threshold or required data field is stated here, because none could be cited from the closed source registry. The 60-per-cent coverage figure in the numeric example is an invented illustration, not a measured implementation rate. Described as at 5 November 2026; nothing here is legal advice.
