Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The price of the most recent completed trade. It is what the ticker and the chart show, and it is history rather than an offer.

Professional definition

The print of the latest match on that venue: one trade, at one size, already done. It anchors charts, candles and many trigger conditions, but it is not a price you can transact at, and on a thin book it can sit far from the current bid and ask.

Technical definition

A single realised transaction price, venue-local and path-dependent. One unit trading on an otherwise empty book produces a last price, which is why it is the cheapest number in a market to push and why venues avoid it for liquidation decisions. It can also fall outside the current spread after a quote update, so marking a book at last price systematically differs from marking longs at the bid and shorts at the ask.

Academic definition

The transaction price of the most recently executed trade on a given venue: a realisation of the price process rather than a currently available quote.

Mechanism

A last price is a sample of one. How much information it carries depends entirely on the size and the aggressor behind it: a one-unit print says almost nothing, while a large print that cleared several levels says a great deal. Treating all prints as equal is why low-volume candles mislead.

In practice

Who uses it
Charting and candle construction, ticker displays, index constituent feeds, and trigger conditions on many venues.
Why it matters
It is the only price that is a fact: a trade really happened there. It is the raw material of every chart, candle and indicator.
Limitations
One trade of any size sets it, it is local to one venue, and on illiquid pairs or at session boundaries it is cheap to manipulate.

Common mistakes

  • Valuing a position at last price when the bid is far below it.
  • Comparing last prices across venues as if they were one market.
  • Reading a thin-volume wick as real price information.

Examples

crypto

A small token prints a sharp wick on one venue while its price elsewhere barely moves. The candle is real — a trade happened — but it described a few units on an empty book, not a change in the market's valuation.

numeric

A holding of 1,000 units shows 100,000 at a last price of 100.00. With a best bid of 99.00 for 200 units and 97.50 behind it, the realisable value on an immediate exit is about 97.80.

Given
  • 1,000 units held
  • last price 100.00
  • bids: 200 at 99.00, 800 at 97.50
  • fees excluded
Steps
  1. mark at last = 1000 x 100.00 = 100000
  2. immediate proceeds = 200 x 99.00 + 800 x 97.50 = 19800 + 78000 = 97800
  3. average realised = 97800 / 1000 = 97.80
97,800 realisable against 100,000 marked at last price: a 2.2% gap

Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions. Reporting standards differ on which to use, so state the convention.

Commonly confused with

Mark Price

Last price is a trade that occurred on one venue. Mark price is the venue's computed reference for margin and liquidation, and is usually derived from an external index instead.
