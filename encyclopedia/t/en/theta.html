Simple definition

How much an option loses in value simply because a day has passed, with nothing else changing. Buyers pay this; writers collect it.

Professional definition

The partial derivative of option value with respect to calendar time, ∂V/∂t, equivalently the negative of the derivative with respect to time remaining. It is quoted per calendar day by market convention, and is normally negative for a long call and a long put: time left is what the buyer paid for.

Technical definition

A model-derived rate of change, not a schedule. Under Black-Scholes the at-the-money time value scales roughly with the square root of time remaining, so decay accelerates as expiry approaches, while a deep in- or out-of-the-money option has little time value to lose and decays slowly and almost linearly. Long theta is not always positive for the holder's counterparty: a deep in-the-money European put can carry positive theta because its floor is the discounted strike.

Academic definition

The first-order sensitivity of a contingent claim's value to the passage of calendar time, holding the underlying price, volatility and rates fixed.

Mechanism

Time value is the price of possibilities still open. Each day removes some of them, so the premium falls even if the underlying stands still. The removal is not even: near the money a day matters because the outcome is still in the balance, while far from the strike almost nothing was priced into the day in the first place.

In practice

Who uses it
Holders budgeting how long a view may take to work, premium sellers accounting for carry, and desks reporting the daily cost of an options book.
Why it matters
It puts a number on the cost of being early, which is the main way a correct directional view still loses money in options.
Limitations
A theta figure is a snapshot of an instantaneous rate under one model. It does not predict tomorrow's premium, because the underlying and the volatility input will also have moved.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying today's theta by thirty to forecast a month of decay.
  • Selling options for the theta alone while ignoring the gamma that comes with it.
  • Assuming weekends and holidays decay like trading days.

Examples

market

A trader buys a near-dated at-the-money call, the underlying stays flat for a week, and the premium is materially smaller. Nothing went wrong with the view; the position simply paid rent on time it did not use.

numeric

An at-the-money option worth 3.20 with 30 days left is worth about 2.26 with 15 days left, all else equal: 0.94 lost in the first half of the window and 2.26 still to go in the second. Decay is not a straight line.

Given
  • at-the-money option, premium 3.20 with 30 days to expiry
  • underlying, volatility input and rate unchanged
  • Black-Scholes at-the-money time value approximately proportional to the square root of time remaining
Steps
  1. sqrt(15 / 30) = 0.7071
  2. 3.20 x 0.7071 = 2.26
  3. decay over the first 15 days = 3.20 - 2.26 = 0.94
about 0.94 lost in the first 15 days, about 2.26 still to be lost in the last 15

The square-root scaling is an approximation that holds near the money under Black-Scholes. It is not a general law and does not apply to deep in- or out-of-the-money contracts.

Procedure

Theta is defined as the derivative of value with respect to calendar time, every other input held fixed. The Black-Scholes closed form for a European call is given above; dividing the annualised figure by 365 produces the per-calendar-day number platforms display. The put differs only in the sign and argument of the rate term.

Worked example

Given
  • premium = 3.20
  • theta = -0.045 per calendar day
  • five days pass with the underlying, volatility input and rate unchanged
Steps
  1. 5 x (-0.045) = -0.225
  2. 3.20 - 0.225 = 2.975
about 2.98, if theta stayed constant over those five days — which it does not

Interpretation

Theta is the rent on optionality, charged continuously and collected by whoever wrote the contract. Read it as a rate, not as a forecast: it tells you what the next instant costs at today's inputs, nothing about the path.

Assumptions

  • The per-day figure is a convention. Dividing by 365 gives calendar-day theta; dividing by 252 gives a trading-day figure. Platforms differ, and the two numbers are not comparable.
  • The closed form is Black-Scholes for a European option on a non-dividend underlying, and inherits the model's constant-volatility and frictionless assumptions.
  • Theta is defined with every other input frozen. In practice a day never passes alone, so realised premium changes mix theta with delta, gamma and vega effects.
  • Sign is not universal: a deep in-the-money European put, and a deep in-the-money call on an underlying with a large dividend, can carry positive theta because of the discounting term.
  • Weekend decay is a venue and model-implementation question, not a property of the derivative. Some desks advance time only on trading days, others continuously.
Limits of the formula
  • Decay is non-linear. An at-the-money option loses time value roughly with the square root of remaining time, so most of the loss falls in the final stretch, while deep in- or out-of-the-money contracts decay slowly and almost evenly.
  • Theta changes as the underlying moves, so a long-dated figure cannot be extrapolated across a move in price or in the volatility input.
  • Collecting theta is not a strategy on its own: the same position that earns it is short gamma, and the two cannot be separated.

Commonly confused with

Intrinsic and Time Value

Time value is a stock: the amount of extrinsic premium currently in the price. Theta is a flow: the rate at which that stock is draining right now.
