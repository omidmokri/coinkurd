Spoofing

Simple definition

Putting orders into the market that you never mean to have filled, so the visible book misleads everyone else. It is prohibited in regulated markets.

Professional definition

Prohibited conduct consisting of entering orders without a genuine intention that they trade, in order to create a misleading impression of resting supply or demand. The offence is the false representation of intent carried by a public order, not the cancellation itself, which is ordinary and lawful behaviour.

Technical definition

The legal test turns on intent at the time of entry, which is why surveillance looks for signatures that imply it rather than for cancellations alone: order-to-trade ratios far from venue norms, resting size that disappears when it would otherwise be reached, and a fill rate inconsistent with the quoted exposure. Legitimate market making also cancels heavily, so detection requires separating withdrawal under adverse selection from orders never meant to execute. The harm is to the informational value of visible depth.

Academic definition

The submission of orders unaccompanied by a bona fide intention to execute, prohibited because the resulting display of depth conveys a false representation of market interest to other participants.

Mechanism

Visible depth works as information because resting orders are commitments: anyone may take them. An order entered without that commitment borrows the credibility of real depth while carrying none of its risk. Once participants suspect the display, they stop reading it, and the venue loses the very signal the order book exists to provide — which is why surveillance treats the informational damage, not the cancellation, as the harm.

In practice

Who uses it
Venue surveillance, market-integrity supervisors, compliance at trading firms, and algorithm owners who must evidence genuine quoting intent.
Why it matters
It separates prohibited conduct from the heavy, lawful order cancellation that normal electronic market making produces.
Limitations
Intent is inferred, not observed. Metrics flag candidates and generate false positives; prohibitions and venue coverage differ by jurisdiction.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a high cancellation rate as proof of prohibited conduct.
  • Assuming venues without surveillance mean the conduct is lawful everywhere.
  • Treating displayed depth as committed liquidity when quotes can be withdrawn in milliseconds.

Examples

regulatory

Surveillance reviews two accounts with identical cancellation rates. One withdraws when volatility rises, across both sides of the book. The other withdraws only when price approaches its own resting size. The pattern, not the rate, distinguishes them.

numeric

An account enters 5,000 orders and trades 50 times, an order-to-trade ratio of 100 against a venue median near 10. The ratio does not prove anything; it selects the account for review.

Given
  • Orders entered = 5,000
  • Executions = 50
  • Venue median order-to-trade ratio = 10 (illustrative)
Steps
  1. Order-to-trade ratio = 5,000 / 50 = 100
  2. Ratio relative to venue median = 100 / 10 = 10x
An order-to-trade ratio ten times the venue median

A surveillance screening metric, not a legal test. High ratios are normal for some legitimate market-making strategies; the metric ranks accounts for human review and nothing more. Venue medians are illustrative.

Commonly confused with

Iceberg Order

An iceberg order hides size that is genuinely available to trade. Spoofing displays size that is not. Concealment is permitted; false representation is not.

Commonly confused with

Market Making

Market makers cancel constantly and legitimately, withdrawing as risk changes. The prohibited case is an order entered with no intention that it ever trade.
