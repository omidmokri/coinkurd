Revenge Trading

Simple definition

Opening the next trade mainly to win back the last loss. The size usually goes up and the plan usually goes out, which is where the damage comes from.

Professional definition

Practitioner vocabulary for a sequence in which a loss triggers an immediate, larger and unplanned re-entry. The term has no research literature of its own, but two of its components are documented: risk-seeking after a loss in the prospect-theory sense, and the measurable cost of raising trading frequency.

Technical definition

What makes the pattern costly is not the emotion but the two mechanical changes that usually accompany it. Position size rises while the quality of the setup does not, so expectancy per trade falls at the same time as the amount at risk rises; and the number of round trips rises, so execution costs compound. Both effects are arithmetic and both can be measured in a trade log after the fact.

Academic definition

A practitioner term for post-loss escalation of trading activity and position size; its components correspond to documented findings on risk-seeking in the loss domain and on the cost of elevated turnover, though the pattern itself is not an established research construct.

Mechanism

After a loss the reference point has not moved, so the account sits in the loss region where prospect theory predicts a preference for the gamble over the certain shortfall. Getting back to even is a nearby, vivid target, and raising size is the fastest route to it. The same step shortens the time to a serious drawdown, because risk scales with size while the setup does not improve.

In practice

Who uses it
Active traders after a losing session, and anyone whose rules do not specify what happens following a loss.
Why it matters
Written in advance, a rule about the next trade after a loss is easy to follow; written during one, it is a negotiation.
Limitations
Re-entering after a loss is not itself an error. A setup that is still valid is still valid. What the term points at is the size change and the missing plan, not the act of trading again.

Common mistakes

  • Doubling size to recover in one trade.
  • Taking a setup that would not have qualified an hour earlier.
  • Leaving the daily loss limit undefined until it is needed.

Examples

numeric

An account risking 2 percent a trade takes one loss, then doubles to 4 percent for two trades and loses both. Equity falls to about 90.3 from 100, against about 94.1 had the original size been kept.

Given
  • starting equity = 100
  • three consecutive losing trades
  • path A: risk 2 percent each time
  • path B: risk 2 percent, then 4 percent, then 4 percent
  • risk expressed as a fraction of current equity
Steps
  1. path A: 100 x 0.98 x 0.98 x 0.98 = 94.12
  2. path B: 100 x 0.98 x 0.96 x 0.96 = 90.32
  3. difference = 3.8 percent of starting equity
the escalated path loses about 3.8 more points of equity over the same three losses

An arithmetic illustration with the number of losses held fixed. It makes no claim about how likely a losing run is.

everyday

A rule that says no new position for one hour after hitting the daily loss limit costs nothing on the days it is not needed. It is written while calm precisely because it will be read while not.

Commonly confused with

Overtrading

Overtrading is diagnosed on cost and frequency, whatever the reason. Revenge trading names one common trigger for it, and adds a size change that overtrading alone does not imply.
