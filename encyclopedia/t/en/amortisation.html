Amortisation

Simple definition

Paying off a debt in equal instalments, where each payment covers the interest due and chips away at the amount still owed.

Professional definition

The scheduled retirement of a debt through level payments, each split into interest on the outstanding balance and principal reduction. Early payments are mostly interest; the principal share grows every period. In accounting the same word names the periodic write-down of an intangible asset.

Technical definition

A recursive process: interest for the period is the rate times the opening balance, and principal repaid is the payment minus that interest, so the balance declines at an accelerating rate. The level payment is the annuity payment that sets the present value of all instalments equal to the loan amount, which is why amortisation and annuity arithmetic are the same mathematics viewed from opposite ends.

Academic definition

The systematic extinguishment of a liability through periodic payments allocated between interest expense and principal reduction, or correspondingly the periodic allocation of an intangible asset's cost to expense.

Mechanism

Because interest is charged on what is still owed, the interest portion shrinks as the balance falls, leaving more of a constant payment for principal. The result is a convex path: barely any principal is repaid in the early years of a long loan, and almost all of it in the final ones.

In practice

Who uses it
Mortgage and instalment borrowers, bank loan operations, accountants writing down intangibles, and analysts reading debt schedules.
Why it matters
It shows how little principal early payments retire, which is what makes early repayment or refinancing decisions concrete rather than intuitive.
Limitations
The schedule assumes a fixed rate and no prepayment; floating rates, payment holidays and balloon structures all break the clean split.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming half the loan is repaid at the halfway point.
  • Confusing loan amortisation with the accounting write-down of intangibles.
  • Ignoring fees outside the schedule.

Examples

everyday

On a long mortgage, the first payment can be more than four-fifths interest. A borrower who sells after a few years discovers the outstanding balance has barely moved from the original loan.

numeric

A loan of 100,000 at 0.5% a month over 240 months needs a payment of 716.43. In month one, 500 is interest and only 216.43 reduces the debt.

Given
  • P = 100,000
  • r = 0.5% per month
  • n = 240 months
  • level payments, end of month
Steps
  1. A = 100,000 x 0.005 / (1 - 1.005^-240) = 716.43
  2. month 1 interest = 100,000 x 0.005 = 500
  3. principal = 716.43 - 500
payment 716.43; first-month principal 216.43

Illustrative rate. Total paid over the term is 171,943, of which 71,943 is interest.

Mathematical foundation

A = P \times \frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}, \qquad I_{k} = r B_{k-1}, \qquad B_{k} = B_{k-1} - (A - I_{k})

Procedure

Compute the level payment from the loan amount, the periodic rate and the number of periods. Then iterate: interest equals the rate times the opening balance, principal equals the payment minus that interest, and the new balance is the old one less the principal.

Worked example

Given
  • P = 100,000
  • r = 0.005
  • n = 240
Steps
  1. 1.005^-240 = 0.302096
  2. 0.005 / (1 - 0.302096) = 0.0071643
  3. A = 100,000 x 0.0071643
A = 716.43 per month

Interpretation

The schedule shows where each payment goes, not whether the loan was wise. A long term lowers the instalment and raises total interest; the schedule makes that trade-off explicit instead of leaving it to intuition.

Assumptions

  • Fixed rate, fixed payment, no prepayment and no payment holidays.
  • r is expressed per payment period; an annual nominal rate must be converted according to the lender's stated convention, which is not always simple division.
  • The accounting sense of amortisation, the write-down of an intangible asset, follows a different allocation rule and is not described by these equations.
Limits of the formula
  • Floating-rate loans re-solve for a new payment or a new term at each reset, so a single schedule is only indicative.
  • Fees, insurance and early-repayment penalties sit outside the schedule and can change the effective cost materially.

Commonly confused with

Depreciation

Amortisation in accounting allocates the cost of an intangible asset; depreciation does the same for a tangible one. In lending, amortisation instead means paying down a debt.
