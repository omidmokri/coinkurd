Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Something of value you pledge so a lender can be made whole if you do not repay. A mortgaged house and crypto locked in a lending protocol are both collateral.

Professional definition

An asset pledged to secure an obligation, giving the lender a claim that ranks ahead of unsecured creditors. Lenders value it below market — the difference is the haircut — because they must assume they will be selling it in exactly the conditions that caused the borrower to fail.

Technical definition

A credit-risk mitigant that converts an unsecured claim into a claim on a specific asset. Its usefulness is limited by three correlations: the value of the collateral with the borrower's own distress, the collateral's liquidity in stress, and the speed of enforcement. Haircuts, margining frequency and overcollateralisation requirements are all attempts to price those correlations in advance.

Academic definition

An asset pledged by an obligor to secure performance, conferring on the secured party a priority claim whose protective value depends on the asset's price volatility, liquidity and enforceability.

Mechanism

Collateral does not remove credit risk, it converts it into market and liquidity risk on the pledged asset. The protection is strongest when the collateral's value is independent of the borrower's fortunes and can be sold quickly — and weakest precisely when a shared shock hits both at once.

In practice

Who uses it
Secured lenders, repo and margin desks, borrowers posting assets, and users of lending protocols that enforce ratios automatically.
Why it matters
It is what lets a lender extend credit to a borrower it would otherwise refuse, and it sets the size of the loan through the haircut.
Limitations
A haircut set in calm conditions can be far too small in a gap move, and enforcement speed differs enormously between a court process and an automated protocol.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a collateral value as a floor in a fast market.
  • Posting the same asset class you are borrowing against.
  • Ignoring that pledged assets are unavailable to other creditors.

Examples

crypto

A borrower locks 1 unit of a volatile token to borrow stablecoins. If the token falls far enough, the protocol sells the collateral automatically rather than waiting for a missed payment — enforcement here is code, not a court.

numeric

Collateral worth 10,000 with a 30% haircut supports 7,000 of borrowing. A 20% fall in the collateral leaves 8,000 of value against 7,000 owed — still covered, but the buffer has shrunk from 3,000 to 1,000.

Given
  • market value V = 10,000
  • haircut h = 30%
  • borrowing = V x (1 - h)
  • subsequent price fall = 20%
Steps
  1. lending value = 10,000 x 0.70 = 7,000
  2. collateral after fall = 10,000 x 0.80 = 8,000
  3. buffer = 8,000 - 7,000
7,000 borrowed; buffer falls from 3,000 to 1,000

Illustrative haircut. A second fall of similar size would breach the loan, which is why haircuts scale with volatility.

Mathematical foundation

L_{max} = V \times (1 - h)

Variables

Procedure

Mark the collateral to market, apply the haircut for that asset class and horizon, and lend no more than the result. Re-mark on a stated schedule and call for more collateral when the ratio breaches its trigger.

Worked example

Given
  • V = 10,000
  • h = 30%
Steps
  1. 1 - 0.30 = 0.70
  2. L_max = 10,000 x 0.70
L_max = 7,000

Interpretation

The haircut is the lender's estimate of how far the asset could fall before it can be sold. It is a risk parameter chosen by the lender, not a market price, and different lenders set it very differently for the same asset.

Assumptions

  • The asset can be sold near its marked value within the enforcement window, which is exactly what fails in a stressed market.
  • Haircuts are set by the lender or the venue and are not standardised across institutions or protocols.
  • Overcollateralisation in crypto lending is often expressed as a collateral ratio instead; the two formulations describe the same buffer from different directions.
Limits of the formula
  • Offers no protection against a shock that hits the collateral and the borrower at the same time, which is the common case in a single-sector downturn.
  • Says nothing about legal perfection of the security interest, which determines whether the claim is actually enforceable.

Commonly confused with

Margin

Collateral is the pledged asset itself. Margin is the specific collateral a trading venue requires against an open position, with its own maintenance rules and automatic enforcement.
