Simple definition

Using algorithms that learn patterns from data to make trading or risk decisions. The hard part is not the algorithm; it is that financial data is mostly noise and does not stay the same.

Professional definition

The application of statistical learning methods to market data for prediction, classification or control. The binding constraints are a very low signal-to-noise ratio, non-stationary data-generating processes, and the ease with which flexible models fit noise that will not repeat.

Technical definition

Supervised and unsupervised learning applied to series that violate the assumptions most methods rest on. Observations are not independent and identically distributed: returns are serially dependent in their squares, labels built over overlapping horizons are correlated, and the relationship being learned changes over time. Greater model flexibility therefore raises, not lowers, the risk of fitting an unrepeatable sample, and standard cross-validation is invalid on time-ordered data.

Academic definition

The application of statistical learning to financial time series under low signal-to-noise ratios and non-stationarity, conditions that invalidate the independence assumptions underlying conventional model selection and inference.

Mechanism

A learning method succeeds when there is a stable relationship and enough independent observations to find it. Markets supply neither in abundance: the predictable component of returns is small next to their variation, and whatever is found gets competed away or changed by policy and structure. Flexibility that helps in image or language tasks becomes a liability here, because it lets the model describe one history exactly.

In practice

Who uses it
Quantitative researchers, risk teams building non-linear models, data engineers preparing market features, and reviewers assessing a model-based claim.
Why it matters
Non-linear methods handle interactions and many inputs more naturally than linear models, which makes them useful where the question is representation rather than forecasting.
Limitations
Low signal-to-noise and non-stationarity bound what any method can achieve, and more flexible models make backtest overfitting easier rather than harder. No claim is made here that any method works.

Common mistakes

  • Using shuffled cross-validation on time-ordered data.
  • Reporting in-sample or leaked results as performance.
  • Judging a model by accuracy rather than by net result after costs.

Examples

numeric

Suppose daily returns have a standard deviation of 2% and a genuine predictable mean of 0.05%. Detecting that edge at a t-statistic of 2 needs about 6,400 daily observations — roughly 25 years of data for one hypothesis.

Given
  • daily return standard deviation = 2% (assumed)
  • true predictable mean = 0.05% per day (assumed)
  • target t-statistic = 2
  • observations assumed independent, which overstates the available information
Steps
  1. t = mean / (sd / sqrt(n)) = 0.0005 x sqrt(n) / 0.02 = 0.025 x sqrt(n)
  2. set 0.025 x sqrt(n) = 2, so sqrt(n) = 80
  3. n = 6,400 days, about 25 years at 252 trading days
about 6,400 independent daily observations for a single test

Overlapping labels and serial dependence reduce the effective sample below the nominal count, and testing many models at once raises the threshold further. Both make the real requirement larger, never smaller.

research

A team trials several hundred model and feature combinations and reports the best. The reported performance is the maximum of many noisy estimates, so it is biased upward by the search itself even when every individual test was run correctly.

Commonly confused with

Linear Regression

Linear regression is one learning method with a closed-form solution and well-understood inference. Machine learning covers far more flexible families whose flexibility is precisely what makes inference on noisy financial data unreliable.
