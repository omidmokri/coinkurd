Simple definition

A quantity that changes over time with an element of chance, described by the rules governing its possible paths rather than by one fixed path.

Professional definition

A collection of random variables indexed by time, together with the probability law that links them. Modelling choices are the state space, whether time is discrete or continuous, and how much of the past the next value depends on.

Technical definition

A family of random variables on a common probability space, indexed by a time set, with the dependence structure specified through its finite-dimensional distributions or a conditional rule. Properties are defined on this structure rather than observed: the Markov property restricts dependence to the current state, stationarity restricts how the law changes with time shifts, and a martingale restricts the conditional mean. Each is a separate assumption, and a single observed path can never confirm any of them outright.

Academic definition

An indexed family of random variables defined on a common probability space, characterised by its finite-dimensional distributions and any assumed dependence or invariance structure.

Mechanism

The model lives in the conditional law, not in the realised path. Fixing how the next value depends on the past is what makes a process tractable, and each restriction buys mathematics at the cost of realism: Markov dependence allows recursive computation, stationarity allows estimation from one long sample, and finite variance allows limit theorems. Data constrain these choices weakly, so the process is always partly a modelling decision.

In practice

Who uses it
Derivatives modellers, risk engineers running simulations, econometricians specifying time-series models, and anyone writing a Monte Carlo engine.
Why it matters
Pricing, simulation and risk aggregation all require a stated law for how the quantity evolves; without one there is nothing to integrate over.
Limitations
The choice of process is an assumption that the data rarely settles, and results such as a value-at-risk number inherit that choice completely.

Common mistakes

  • Reporting simulation output without naming the assumed process.
  • Treating a Markov assumption as harmless in a series with memory.
  • Estimating a stationary model on a series whose regime changed mid-sample.

Examples

market

The same price history can be modelled as a walk with constant volatility, as a walk with time-varying volatility, or as a two-regime switching process. All three fit the past; they disagree sharply about the chance of a large move next week.

numeric

A two-state chain stays calm with probability 0.95 and switches to turbulent with 0.05 each period. The expected length of a calm spell is 20 periods — a number that comes entirely from the assumed transition rule, not from any observation.

Given
  • two states: calm and turbulent
  • P(calm -> calm) = 0.95, so P(calm -> turbulent) = 0.05
  • transitions assumed independent across periods (Markov)
Steps
  1. spell length is geometric with success probability 0.05
  2. expected length = 1 / 0.05
expected calm spell = 20 periods

The geometric result follows from the Markov assumption. If exit probability rises with the length of the spell, the chain is not Markov in that state and 20 is wrong.

Commonly confused with

Random Variable

A random variable is one uncertain quantity. A stochastic process is a whole time-indexed family of them, plus the law describing how they are related, which is where all the time-series content lives.
