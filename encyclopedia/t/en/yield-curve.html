Simple definition

A line showing what interest rate the market pays for lending over different lengths of time — three months, two years, ten years — for the same kind of borrower.

Professional definition

The set of yields on comparable credit-quality instruments plotted against maturity. Its usual upward slope reflects term premia and expectations; its shape, not just its level, is what practitioners read when they speak of steepening, flattening or inversion.

Technical definition

A cross-sectional mapping from maturity to yield for one issuer class at one instant. Curves can be built from par yields, spot (zero-coupon) rates or forwards, and the three differ systematically, so the construction method must be stated. Inversion means short maturities yield more than long ones, which has historically tended to precede recessions in a small number of observed episodes.

Academic definition

The contemporaneous relationship between yield and term to maturity for instruments of comparable credit quality, representing the term structure of interest rates.

Mechanism

Lenders choose between rolling short loans and locking a long one, so the curve encodes both expected future short rates and the extra compensation demanded for committing. When policy rates are expected to fall, long yields can sit below short ones; the resulting inversion is a reading of expectations, not a cause of anything.

In practice

Who uses it
Rate strategists, bank asset-liability committees, mortgage and corporate borrowers choosing a term, and macro traders reading policy expectations.
Why it matters
It prices time itself, so it sets the cost of borrowing long versus short and anchors the discount rates used elsewhere.
Limitations
Shape is read as a signal far more often than the evidence supports: the inversion record rests on a handful of episodes, with varying lags and several ambiguous cases.

Common mistakes

  • Treating inversion as a dated forecast of recession.
  • Comparing curves built on different methods.
  • Reading a single spread as if it were the whole curve.

Examples

market

When the market expects policy rates to be cut, buyers accept lower yields on long maturities to lock today's level in. Short yields stay high while long ones fall, and the curve inverts without any change in credit quality.

numeric

Two-year yield 4.80%, ten-year 4.20%: the 10-year-minus-2-year spread is minus 0.60 percentage points, or minus 60 basis points. The curve is inverted on this pair, which says nothing on its own about timing.

Given
  • 2-year yield = 4.80%
  • 10-year yield = 4.20%
  • same issuer class, same quoting convention
Steps
  1. spread = 4.20% - 4.80% = -0.60%
  2. in basis points: -0.60 x 100
spread = -60 bp, an inverted 2s10s

Illustrative levels, not market observations. Other pairs, such as 3-month against 10-year, can disagree with this one.

Commonly confused with

Yield curve inversion

The curve is the whole mapping from maturity to yield. Inversion is one shape that mapping can take, usually measured by a single spread between two chosen maturities.

The claim that inversion has tended to precede recessions is stated as an empirical regularity observed in a small number of episodes, with no mechanism asserted and no lag promised; the registry holds no dedicated study of the inversion record, so the claim is kept deliberately weak. Curve construction method (par, spot or forward) changes the picture and is flagged. Yield levels in the examples are illustrative.
