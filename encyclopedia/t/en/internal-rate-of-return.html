Internal Rate of Return

Simple definition

The discount rate at which a project exactly breaks even — the rate that makes the value of its future cash equal to its cost. Higher is usually read as better.

Professional definition

The rate that sets net present value to zero for a given cash flow series. It is reported as a single percentage, which makes projects of different sizes look comparable, and it is solved for numerically because no closed-form solution exists in general.

Technical definition

An implicit solution: the root of an n-degree polynomial in the discount factor. Where cash flows change sign more than once, several real roots can satisfy the equation and the measure is not unique; where they never change sign it does not exist. It also implicitly reinvests interim flows at the solved rate, which is why modified variants were introduced and why a high value on a short-lived project can be economically meaningless.

Academic definition

The discount rate at which the net present value of a cash flow stream equals zero, equivalently the root of the present-value polynomial, unique only under a single sign change in the flows.

Mechanism

Net present value falls as the discount rate rises, so somewhere it crosses zero: that crossing is the internal rate. The crossing is unique only when the cash flows change sign once. Add a later outflow — a refit, a clean-up cost, a second funding round — and the curve can cross zero twice, giving two rates that are both correct answers to a badly posed question.

In practice

Who uses it
Project appraisal teams, private equity and venture funds reporting fund performance, lease and infrastructure analysts, and bond investors computing yield to maturity.
Why it matters
A percentage needs no scale to interpret and compares directly with a cost of capital, which is why committees and fund marketing both prefer it.
Limitations
It can fail to exist, can have several values, is sensitive to the timing of interim flows, and ranks a small quick project above a large valuable one.

Common mistakes

  • Ranking mutually exclusive projects by rate instead of by value added.
  • Quoting one rate when the flows change sign more than once.
  • Comparing a fund's rate with a market return without matching the cash flow timing.

Examples

numeric

A machine costing 1,000 returns 400 a year for three years. Its internal rate of return is about 9.7 per cent, so it is accepted against an 8 per cent hurdle and rejected against 10 per cent — the same conclusions the net present value gave.

Given
  • Outflow of 1,000 at t = 0
  • Inflows of 400 at t = 1, 2, 3
  • Solve for r such that NPV(r) = 0
Steps
  1. NPV(8%) = +30.84, so the root is above 8%
  2. NPV(10%) = -5.26, so the root is below 10%
  3. Iterating between the two brackets the root to r = 0.09701
IRR = 9.70% (approximately)

There is no formula for this root. It was found by iteration, which is what every spreadsheet function does.

edge-case

Flows of minus 100, plus 230 and minus 132 change sign twice. Both 10 per cent and 20 per cent set net present value to exactly zero, so the project has two internal rates of return and neither can be compared with a hurdle rate.

Given
  • CF_0 = -100, CF_1 = +230, CF_2 = -132
  • Sign changes: two
Steps
  1. At r = 10%: -100 + 230/1.10 - 132/1.21 = -100 + 209.09 - 109.09 = 0
  2. At r = 20%: -100 + 230/1.20 - 132/1.44 = -100 + 191.67 - 91.67 = 0
Two internal rates of return: 10% and 20%

Both roots are mathematically correct. The decision has to be made on net present value at the relevant cost of capital instead.

Mathematical foundation

\sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t} = 0

Variables

IRRThe discount rate that makes net present value zero; solved numericallyratio per period
CF_tNet cash flow at the end of period t, with its signcurrency
nFinal period of the cash flow seriesperiods

Procedure

Write net present value as a function of the rate, then search for the rate where it equals zero — bracket it between a rate giving a positive value and one giving a negative value, and bisect or iterate until the gap is small enough. First count the sign changes in the flows: more than one means the root may not be unique.

Worked example

Given
  • CF_0 = -1,000
  • CF_1 = CF_2 = CF_3 = 400
Steps
  1. NPV(0.08) = +30.84
  2. NPV(0.10) = -5.26
  3. Root lies between 0.08 and 0.10
  4. Bisection converges to 0.09701
IRR = 9.70%

Interpretation

The result is the break-even compound rate earned on the capital while it is tied up. It is comparable with a cost of capital only when it is unique and when the project's scale and life are already accounted for separately.

Assumptions

  • Uniqueness requires a single sign change in the cash flow series. With more than one, multiple real roots can exist and the measure is not well defined.
  • The equation implicitly assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the solved rate, which is why modified internal rate of return was introduced with an explicit reinvestment rate.
  • No closed-form solution exists in general; every reported figure is the output of an iterative search and depends on the convergence tolerance and the starting bracket.
Limits of the formula
  • Does not exist for flows that never change sign, and is undefined as a single number when they change sign repeatedly.
  • Favours short, small projects over long, large ones, so it cannot rank mutually exclusive investments; net present value must do that.

Commonly confused with

Dollar-Weighted Return

A money-weighted portfolio return is an internal rate of return applied to an investor's own contributions and withdrawals. Same mathematics, different object: one appraises a project, the other measures an account.
