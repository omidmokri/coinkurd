Central Limit Theorem

Simple definition

Averages of many independent random numbers form a bell curve, even when the numbers themselves do not. It describes the average, never the raw data.

Professional definition

For independent draws with finite variance, the standardised sample mean converges in distribution to a standard normal as the sample grows. The practical consequence is that the standard error of a mean falls with the square root of the sample size.

Technical definition

If X1 to Xn are independent and identically distributed with mean mu and finite variance sigma squared, then the root-n scaled deviation of the sample mean from mu converges in distribution to N(0, sigma squared). Finite variance is essential: with infinite variance a different, stable limit applies. The theorem constrains the sampling distribution of the mean and says nothing about the tails of the underlying variable, which stay as heavy as they were.

Academic definition

A limit theorem establishing that the normalised sum of independent, identically distributed random variables with finite variance converges in distribution to a Gaussian law, irrespective of the shape of the parent distribution.

Mechanism

Summing independent variables adds their variances but also averages away their individual shapes; every distribution with a finite second moment contributes the same quadratic term and nothing else survives rescaling. That is why the limit is universal. It is also why the universality breaks when the second moment is infinite — there is then no finite quadratic term to dominate.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone computing a confidence interval, a t-statistic or a p-value on strategy returns, and risk teams converting a sample mean into a decision.
Why it matters
It supplies the standard error, which is what turns a measured average into a range and tells you how many observations a conclusion needs.
Limitations
It governs the mean, not the extremes. Position sizing and ruin depend on the tails, where the normal approximation is worst.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming returns themselves are normal because the theorem exists.
  • Applying it to serially dependent or clustered trades.
  • Using normal quantiles to size for a tail event.

Examples

numeric

Mean edge 0.2% per trade, dispersion 2%. After 100 trades the standard error is 0.2%, so the 95% interval runs from -0.19% to +0.59% — the sample cannot yet tell an edge from nothing.

Given
  • mean = 0.2% per trade
  • sigma = 2% per trade
  • n = 100
  • trades assumed independent
Steps
  1. SE = sigma / sqrt(n) = 2% / 10 = 0.2%
  2. 95% interval = 0.2% +/- 1.96 * 0.2%
  3. = 0.2% +/- 0.392%
95% interval -0.192% to +0.592%; zero is inside it

For the interval to exclude zero at the same dispersion, 1.96 * SE must fall below 0.2%, which first happens at n = 385.

market

A monthly return series averaged over long windows looks close to normal, while the daily series behind it still contains single sessions many standard deviations wide. Averaging normalised the mean, not the market.

Mathematical foundation

\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0,1), \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

Procedure

Estimate the mean and the standard deviation of the per-observation series. Divide the standard deviation by the square root of the number of independent observations to get the standard error. Multiply by the normal quantile for the confidence level wanted — 1.96 for 95% — and place that band either side of the measured mean.

Worked example

Given
  • sigma = 2% per trade
  • n = 100 independent trades
  • measured mean = 0.2%
Steps
  1. SE = 0.02 / sqrt(100) = 0.002 (0.2%)
  2. half-width = 1.96 * 0.002 = 0.00392 (0.392%)
  3. interval = 0.002 - 0.00392 to 0.002 + 0.00392
95% interval = -0.192% to +0.592%

Interpretation

The standard error states how tightly a measured average pins down the true one. Wide bands mean the sample is too small to support a conclusion; they are not evidence that the mean is zero.

Assumptions

  • Independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance.
  • The normal quantile is itself an approximation at small n; a t-distribution is the usual correction when sigma is estimated from the same sample.
  • Serial dependence inflates the effective standard error, so the plain root-n formula understates uncertainty for autocorrelated returns.
Limits of the formula
  • It is a statement about the sample mean, not about the parent distribution, whose skewness and kurtosis are untouched.
  • With infinite variance the Gaussian limit fails and a stable law with heavier tails applies instead.
  • Convergence speed depends on the parent's shape; strongly skewed or heavy-tailed data need far larger n than the formula suggests.

Commonly confused with

Normal Distribution

The theorem makes the sampling distribution of the mean normal. It does not make returns normal, and heavy tails in the data survive it intact.

Commonly confused with

Law of Large Numbers

Large numbers fixes the destination of the average; the central limit theorem fixes the shape of the error around it and so yields a sample size.
