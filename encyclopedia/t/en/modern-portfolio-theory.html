Simple definition

The idea that a portfolio should be judged by the risk and return of the whole, not of each holding separately, because holdings that do not move together partly cancel each other's swings.

Professional definition

Markowitz's mean-variance framework. An investor who cares only about expected return and variance should hold the portfolio whose variance, given the target return, is smallest. Because covariance terms enter that variance, combining imperfectly correlated assets puts portfolio volatility below the weighted average of the components' volatilities.

Technical definition

A single-period optimisation over weight vectors in which expected return is linear in the weights and variance is quadratic. The off-diagonal covariance terms are the whole source of the diversification effect: for two assets the variance is the two squared-weight variance terms plus twice the cross term of weights, volatilities and correlation, and any correlation below one makes portfolio volatility strictly smaller than the weighted average. Its weaknesses are empirical rather than logical: the optimiser magnifies error in estimated means, and variance treats upside and downside alike.

Academic definition

A single-period mean-variance optimisation in which portfolio expected return is linear and portfolio variance quadratic in the weight vector, so that the diversification benefit derives entirely from the off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix.

Mechanism

Variance does not add across holdings. Each pair contributes a cross term proportional to the product of its two weights, its two volatilities and its correlation, and that term is smaller than the product of volatilities whenever correlation is below one. Diversification is nothing more than this arithmetic: it is a property of the covariance matrix, and it vanishes as correlations approach one.

In practice

Who uses it
Portfolio managers, allocation committees, risk teams, and anyone computing a portfolio's volatility from its holdings rather than guessing it.
Why it matters
It gives an exact expression for whole-portfolio risk, so the effect of changing one weight can be computed rather than argued about.
Limitations
Optimisers amplify estimation error in expected returns, variance treats gains and losses symmetrically, and the covariance inputs are least stable in the periods that matter most.

Common mistakes

  • Feeding in historical mean returns and treating the output weights as precise.
  • Assuming a covariance matrix estimated in calm markets survives a crisis.
  • Reading low portfolio variance as a low probability of large loss.

Examples

numeric

Two holdings at equal weight, one with 20% volatility and one with 10%, correlation 0.2. The weighted average volatility is 15%, but the portfolio's is 12.04%. That 2.96 point gap is the entire mathematical content of diversification.

Given
  • w1 = w2 = 0.5
  • sigma1 = 20%, sigma2 = 10%
  • correlation rho = 0.2
Steps
  1. first term = 0.5^2 x 0.20^2 = 0.010000
  2. second term = 0.5^2 x 0.10^2 = 0.002500
  3. cross term = 2 x 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.20 x 0.10 x 0.2 = 0.002000
  4. portfolio variance = 0.014500
  5. portfolio volatility = sqrt(0.014500) = 0.1204
portfolio volatility 12.04% versus a 15.00% weighted average of the two volatilities

Set the correlation to 1 and the cross term becomes 0.010000, the variance 0.022500 and the volatility exactly 15.00%. The benefit is the correlation, nothing else.

market

A portfolio built on historical correlations near zero can behave like a single position in a crisis, when most risky assets fall together. The weights did not change; the covariance matrix did, and with it the variance reduction the construction relied on.

Procedure

Expected portfolio return is the weighted sum of component expected returns, so it is simply linear. Variance is not: square each weight against its own variance, then add two times the product of each pair's weights, volatilities and correlation. For more than two assets this is the quadratic form of the weight vector with the covariance matrix. Take the square root for volatility.

Worked example

Given
  • two assets, w1 = w2 = 0.5
  • sigma1 = 0.20 (20%), sigma2 = 0.10 (10%)
  • rho = 0.2
Steps
  1. w1^2 sigma1^2 = 0.25 x 0.0400 = 0.010000
  2. w2^2 sigma2^2 = 0.25 x 0.0100 = 0.002500
  3. 2 w1 w2 sigma1 sigma2 rho = 2 x 0.25 x 0.20 x 0.10 x 0.2 = 0.002000
  4. sigma_p^2 = 0.010000 + 0.002500 + 0.002000 = 0.014500
  5. sigma_p = sqrt(0.014500) = 0.120416
  6. weighted average of volatilities = 0.5 x 0.20 + 0.5 x 0.10 = 0.150000
portfolio volatility 12.04% against a 15.00% weighted average: a reduction of 2.96 percentage points produced only by the cross term

Interpretation

Portfolio volatility is below the weighted average of its components whenever correlation is under one, equal to it when correlation is exactly one, and lower still as correlation falls. That inequality is the whole case for diversification; nothing in it promises a higher return.

Assumptions

  • A single holding period, with returns fully described by expected values, variances and covariances; higher moments are ignored.
  • The parameters are treated as known. In practice they are sample estimates, and the optimiser's output is far more sensitive to the estimated means than to the estimated covariances.
  • Variance as the risk measure implies symmetric treatment of gains and losses. Downside measures such as semi-variance give different optimal weights from the same data.
Limits of the formula
  • Correlations are not constants. They rise toward one across risky assets in stress periods, so the measured variance reduction is smallest when it is most needed.
  • Minimising variance is not the same as minimising the chance of a large loss when returns have fat tails.

Commonly confused with

Efficient Market Hypothesis

Modern portfolio theory says how to combine assets given their covariances. The efficient market hypothesis is a separate claim about how fast information reaches prices. Either can hold without the other.
