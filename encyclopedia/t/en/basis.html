Basis

Simple definition

The gap between the futures price and the spot price of the same asset. It shrinks toward zero as the contract approaches expiry.

Professional definition

Futures price minus spot price. A positive basis, called contango, usually reflects the cost of carrying the asset or demand for leveraged long exposure; a negative basis, backwardation, reflects scarcity of the deliverable or demand to be short. Arbitrage and settlement force convergence at expiry.

Technical definition

The observable residual between a futures quote and its spot reference, equal in a frictionless market to the net cost of carry over the remaining life. It is not constant: it widens with time to expiry, financing costs and leverage demand, and narrows mechanically toward zero at settlement because the contract settles against the same reference. Basis risk is the risk that this residual moves while a hedge is open.

Academic definition

The difference between the futures price and the contemporaneous spot price of the underlying, converging to zero at delivery where cash or physical settlement references the same price.

Mechanism

Convergence is enforced by settlement, not by sentiment: at expiry the contract pays against the spot reference, so any remaining gap is a profit an arbitrageur can take. Before expiry the gap persists only to the extent that capturing it is expensive, because it requires capital on two legs and the patience to hold to settlement.

In practice

Who uses it
Hedgers whose exposure is not the exact deliverable, basis traders, and anyone rolling a futures position between contract months.
Why it matters
It is the price of carry and the price of leverage, and it decides whether rolling a position costs money or pays.
Limitations
A hedge is only as good as the stability of the basis: the hedger has not removed risk, they have exchanged price risk for basis risk.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a hedge risk-free when the hedged asset differs from the deliverable.
  • Reading a wide positive basis as a forecast of a higher spot price.
  • Comparing two sources that define basis with opposite signs.

Examples

market

An index fund holds a long position through dated futures and must roll before each expiry. In contango it sells the cheaper expiring contract and buys the dearer next month, so the roll costs money every cycle even if spot never moves.

numeric

A dated future trades at 51,000 while spot is 50,000, with 30 days to expiry. The basis is 1,000, which is 2% of spot, and about 24.3% when annualised simply on a 365-day year.

Given
  • futures price = 51,000
  • spot price = 50,000
  • days to expiry = 30
  • crypto convention: 365-day year, simple (not compounded) annualisation
Steps
  1. basis = 51,000 - 50,000 = 1,000
  2. basis as a share of spot = 1,000 / 50,000 = 2.0%
  3. annualised = 2.0% x 365 / 30 = 24.33%
basis 1,000, that is 2.0% of spot, about 24.3% annualised

Illustrative figures. Capturing this requires funding both legs, the ability to hold to expiry, and survival of any widening in between; fees and borrow costs come out of the 24.3%.

Mathematical foundation

B_t = F_t - S_t, \qquad B_T \to 0 \ \text{as} \ t \to T, \qquad r_{\text{ann}} = \frac{F_t - S_t}{S_t} \times \frac{365}{d}

Procedure

Subtract the spot reference from the futures quote to get the basis in price units. Divide by spot to express it as a share, then scale by the year length over the days remaining to state it as an annual rate comparable with a financing cost.

Worked example

Given
  • F_t = 51,000
  • S_t = 50,000
  • d = 30
  • year length = 365 days
Steps
  1. B_t = 51,000 - 50,000 = 1,000
  2. B_t / S_t = 0.02
  3. 365 / 30 = 12.1667
  4. r_ann = 0.02 x 12.1667 = 0.2433
basis 1,000 (2.0% of spot), annualised about 24.3%

Interpretation

A positive basis says the future is dearer than spot, so a long position in the future is paying for carry or for leverage. The annualised figure is the rate at which that premium decays if the gap closes evenly to expiry; it is a cost, not a forecast.

Assumptions

  • Both sign conventions are in use. This entry defines basis as futures minus spot, the convention common in equity-index and crypto markets; much of the commodity hedging literature defines it as spot minus futures, which reverses every sign. Check the convention before comparing two sources.
  • F_t and S_t must reference the same underlying and the same index. A futures contract settling on one venue's index and a spot price taken from another venue produce a basis that partly measures the difference between the two references.
  • The annualisation shown is simple, not compounded, and uses a 365-day year, the common crypto convention. Money-market quoting often uses 360 days and equity work about 252 trading days; the figure changes with the choice.
  • Convergence to zero assumes cash or physical settlement against the same reference used as spot. A contract settling on a different index converges to that index, not to the spot price being compared.
Limits of the formula
  • A positive basis is not free money: the annualised rate is gross of fees, borrow, funding of margin and the risk that the gap widens before it closes.
  • For physically delivered commodities the relation is bounded by storage and convenience yield rather than being an identity, so the basis can sit far from any financing rate.

Commonly confused with

Funding Rate

Basis is a price difference on a dated contract, closed by settlement. Funding is a recurring cash payment that does the same job on a contract with no expiry.
