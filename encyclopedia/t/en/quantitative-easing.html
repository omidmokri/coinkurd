Quantitative Easing

Simple definition

A central bank buying large amounts of bonds with newly created reserves. The aim is to push down longer-term interest rates when the short-term policy rate is already close to zero.

Professional definition

Large-scale purchases of government and sometimes mortgage-backed securities, paid for by crediting bank reserves. The purchases raise the price and lower the yield of the securities bought, lengthen the central bank's balance sheet, and are intended to ease financial conditions once conventional rate cuts are exhausted.

Technical definition

QE exchanges duration for reserves: the central bank removes long-dated securities from private portfolios and issues overnight central-bank money in their place. The intended channels are signalling about the future policy path, a term-premium or portfolio-balance effect as price-sensitive holders rebalance, and support for market functioning under stress. Reserves created this way sit inside the banking system and are not automatically lent out; the size of each channel is disputed.

Academic definition

A balance-sheet policy in which a central bank purchases longer-maturity assets against newly created reserves, intended to compress term premia and ease financial conditions when the short-term policy rate is constrained.

Mechanism

Portfolio balance is the core idea: if investors do not treat long bonds and overnight reserves as perfect substitutes, taking duration out of the market forces remaining holders to accept a lower term premium. Whether that spills into risk assets depends on who sold, what they bought instead, and what the purchases signalled about the policy path.

In practice

Who uses it
Central banks at or near the effective lower bound, and anyone trading rates, duration or liquidity-sensitive assets.
Why it matters
It changes the quantity and maturity composition of safe assets available to private portfolios, which shows up in term premia and funding conditions.
Limitations
Effects on risk-asset prices are contested. Purchase programmes coincide with recessions, fiscal expansion and shifting risk appetite, so clean identification is very hard.

Common mistakes

  • Describing reserve creation as money handed directly to the public.
  • Reading a balance-sheet chart as a mechanical driver of crypto prices.
  • Assuming the exit is a mirror image of the entry.

Examples

market

Announcement effects are often larger than the settled purchases: yields can move on the statement, before a single bond changes hands. That is evidence that signalling works alongside quantities.

debate

Three readings circulate: that purchases eased financial conditions broadly and supported risk assets; that most of the effect sat in the bonds bought and came from signalling; and that the co-movement reflects the recession and recovery around the purchases.

numeric

If purchases compress a ten-year yield by 25 basis points, a bond with modified duration 8 gains roughly 2 percent in price. Duration is a first-order approximation; convexity makes the true move slightly larger.

Given
  • change in yield = -25 basis points = -0.0025
  • modified duration = 8 years
  • convexity ignored
Steps
  1. price change approximately = -D x dy
  2. = -8 x (-0.0025)
  3. = +0.02
about a 2 percent price gain

A first-order approximation only, and an illustrative yield move rather than an observed one. It does not tell you what any other asset class did.

Commonly confused with

Interest Rates and the Federal Reserve

Rate policy sets the price of overnight money; QE acts on the quantity and maturity of assets held. They can be used together, and markets read them together.
