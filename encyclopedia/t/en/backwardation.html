Backwardation

ENGLISHBackwardationreference

Category
Derivatives
Level
Professional

Simple definition

When contracts for later delivery cost less than the price today. The futures curve slopes downward, so waiting is cheaper than holding.

Professional definition

A term structure in which futures prices sit below spot and fall with time to delivery. It signals that having the asset now is worth more than the cost of carrying it — typically because supply is tight, borrowing it is expensive, or someone needs it urgently.

Technical definition

A downward-sloping forward curve, with the futures price below spot and distant contracts below near ones. Under the cost-of-carry relation it requires a convenience yield above financing plus storage, which cannot be arbitraged away because the cash-and-carry trade only works in one direction: you can buy and store, but you cannot un-store an asset you do not hold. That asymmetry is why backwardation can persist while extreme contango usually cannot.

Academic definition

A configuration of the forward curve in which futures prices are decreasing in time to maturity and lie below the prevailing spot price, implying a convenience yield exceeding the net cost of carry.

Mechanism

The cash-and-carry arbitrage runs one way only. If the far contract is too expensive, anyone can buy the asset, store it and sell forward, which caps contango at the cost of carry. If it is too cheap, the reverse trade needs the physical asset in hand, and in a shortage nobody lends it. So scarcity can hold the curve inverted while the shortage lasts.

In practice

Who uses it
Commodity traders watching inventories, crypto desks reading a perpetual trading below its index, and anyone whose position must be rolled forward.
Why it matters
An inverted curve makes rolling a long position cheaper rather than costlier, and it is one of the cleaner read-outs of physical tightness.
Limitations
It is a statement about scarcity and borrowing conditions now, not a forecast of lower prices later, and in illiquid far contracts the slope may be an artefact of thin quotes.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a downward curve as a bearish forecast.
  • Treating roll gains in backwardation as a durable return stream.
  • Inferring tightness from a far-month quote that barely trades.

Examples

market

A supply disruption leaves buyers competing for immediate barrels. Spot rises above every futures month and the curve inverts, because owning the commodity today solves a problem that owning a contract for next quarter does not.

numeric

Spot is 100 while the three-month contract is 97.5 and the six-month 96.0. A long roller sells the expiring contract and buys a cheaper one, so each roll adds value — about 2.5% for the first quarter if the shape holds.

Given
  • S = 100
  • F(3m) = 97.5
  • F(6m) = 96.0
Steps
  1. Discount to spot = (97.5 - 100) / 100 = -2.50%
  2. Three-to-six-month segment = (96.0 - 97.5) / 97.5 = -1.54%
  3. A long rolling into the cheaper contract gains that difference at each roll
A downward curve: the near contract trades 2.5% under spot and the far contract a further 1.5% under the near one

The roll gain is not free: it comes with full exposure to the price falling, and the shape can revert before the next roll.

Mathematical foundation

F_{t,T} = S_t e^{(r + u - y)(T-t)}, \qquad \text{backwardation} \iff y > r + u

Variables

S_tSpot price at time tprice units

Procedure

Use the same carry relation as for contango and check the sign of the exponent. When the convenience yield of having the asset in hand exceeds financing plus storage, the exponent is negative, deferred contracts price below nearer ones, and the curve is inverted.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 100
  • r = 4% a year
  • u = 2% a year
  • y = 16% a year
  • T - t = 0.25 years
Steps
  1. r + u - y = -0.10
  2. -0.10 x 0.25 = -0.025
  3. e^-0.025 = 0.97531
  4. F = 100 x 0.97531 = 97.53
F(3m) = 97.53, below spot: backwardation of about 2.5% for the quarter

Interpretation

A negative exponent means the market is paying for immediacy. Because the convenience yield is inferred rather than observed, the formula is best read as an accounting of why the curve is inverted, not as a measurement of scarcity that could be checked independently.

Assumptions

  • Convenience yield y is a residual inferred from observed prices, not an independently measured quantity.
  • The relation is an identity only where the full cash-and-carry trade is possible in both directions; in a shortage the reverse leg is unavailable, so backwardation can exceed any arbitrage bound.
  • Rates and storage costs are treated as known and constant over the term.
Limits of the formula
  • The slope of a thin far-month contract may reflect absent liquidity rather than real scarcity.
  • A separate and much stronger claim, Keynes's normal backwardation, says futures sit below the expected future spot price so that hedgers pay speculators a risk premium. That is a theory about unobservable expectations and is not what the observable curve shape by itself demonstrates.

Commonly confused with

Contango

They are the two signs of the same slope. Contango: far contracts dearer, carry costs money. Backwardation: far contracts cheaper, immediacy costs money. The same market can switch between them within weeks.
