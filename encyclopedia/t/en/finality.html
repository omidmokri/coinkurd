Simple definition

The point at which a recorded transaction can no longer be undone. On most chains this is a matter of degree rather than a switch being flipped.

Professional definition

Under proof of work, finality is probabilistic: each extra block makes reversal costlier and the chance of a reorganisation smaller, but never zero. Under Ethereum's proof of stake, checkpoints are finalised by a two-thirds supermajority of staked validators, and undoing one would require a large share of that stake to be destroyed.

Technical definition

Probabilistic finality: against an honest majority, an attacker starting z blocks behind must out-produce the chain, and the catch-up probability decays geometrically in z. Economic finality: once a checkpoint is finalised, certifying a conflicting one requires at least a third of the staked capital to have provably broken the rules, which the protocol slashes — so reversal carries a measurable price rather than a measurable probability. Neither notion is legal settlement finality.

Academic definition

The property that a committed state transition cannot be reverted, realised either as a probabilistic bound decaying with chain depth or as an economic guarantee enforced by slashing conditions on equivocating validators.

Mechanism

Depth buys security under proof of work because rewriting history means redoing work while honest producers extend the tip, so the gap widens unless the attacker is faster. Checkpointing buys it differently: validators sign commitments they cannot contradict without provable misbehaviour, so reversal stops being a race and becomes a loss of capital.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchanges and custodians setting deposit policy, payment processors, bridge designers, and settlement or legal teams mapping protocol events to obligations.
Why it matters
It tells you how long to wait before treating value as received, and what it would cost an adversary to take it back.
Limitations
Protocol finality is not legal settlement finality, and a client bug or a successful majority attack can still revert what the rules call final.

Common mistakes

  • Using one fixed confirmation count on every chain regardless of security budget.
  • Reading the word finalised as legally irreversible.
  • Forgetting that exchange policy, not the protocol, decides when funds become usable.

Examples

crypto

On Ethereum's proof of stake, a checkpoint is finalised after two epochs. With thirty-two twelve-second slots per epoch, that is about thirteen minutes of waiting for a guarantee that is economic rather than probabilistic.

numeric

An attacker with a tenth of the production power, six blocks behind, has about a two-in-a-million chance of ever catching up under the simple catch-up bound. The same attacker with a majority has no decay at all.

Given
  • q = 0.10 attacker share
  • p = 0.90 honest share
  • z = 6 blocks behind
Steps
  1. q/p = 0.1111
  2. (q/p)^6 = 1.88 x 10^-6
about 0.00019 percent

This is the gambler's-ruin catch-up bound for an attacker already z blocks behind. The Bitcoin paper's fuller calculation also accounts for the progress the attacker makes while the recipient waits, giving a slightly larger figure.

Mathematical foundation

P_{catch-up}(z) = \left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^{z}, \qquad q < p

Variables

qAttacker's share of block productionfraction
pHonest participants' share, equal to one minus qfraction
zNumber of blocks the attacker is behindblocks

Procedure

Take the attacker's production share and divide by the honest share. Raise that ratio to the power of the depth the attacker must make up. If the ratio is one or more, the expression no longer decays and the bound gives no protection.

Worked example

Given
  • q = 0.10
  • p = 0.90
  • z = 6
Steps
  1. q/p = 0.1111
  2. 0.1111^6 = 1.88 x 10^-6
P = 1.88 x 10^-6, that is about two chances in a million

Interpretation

Each additional block multiplies the attacker's odds by q over p, so waiting is exponentially effective — but only while the attacker is in the minority. The bound is the reason confirmations exist and the reason they stop helping above fifty percent.

Assumptions

  • Attacker and honest producers draw blocks as independent events with constant shares q and p.
  • This is the catch-up bound from a fixed deficit of z blocks; the Bitcoin paper's Poisson treatment additionally accounts for progress during the recipient's wait.
  • It applies to chain-selection consensus only, and says nothing about finality under stake-based checkpointing.
Limits of the formula
  • At q greater than or equal to p the expression is not a probability bound at all: success becomes a matter of time.
  • Real risk also includes selfish-mining strategies, network partitions and client bugs, none of which this expression models.

Commonly confused with

Settlement

Protocol finality is about whether the ledger can be rewritten. Settlement is about whether an obligation is legally discharged. A finalised block does not by itself settle anything in law.
