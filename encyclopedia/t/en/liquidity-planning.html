Liquidity Planning

Simple definition

Arranging a portfolio so money needed on a date can be raised on that date without having to sell something at a bad price.

Professional definition

The practice of matching expected and contingent cash needs to holdings that can be realised at acceptable cost within the required time. It covers scheduled outflows, margin and collateral calls, and the possibility that a position's liquidity disappears exactly when it is needed.

Technical definition

Three things are planned separately: the amount, the date, and the realisation cost. Holdings are grouped by the time and the price concession needed to turn them into cash, and each planned or contingent outflow is matched to a group that can meet it. The binding case is correlated stress, when a drawdown, a margin call and a scheduled outflow arrive together and the only assets available have fallen. Crypto venues add realisation risk: withdrawal suspensions and thin weekend books make the time to cash itself uncertain.

Academic definition

The alignment of a portfolio's realisable cash profile with its scheduled and contingent obligations, accounting for realisation cost, settlement time, and the correlation between funding needs and market stress.

Mechanism

A forced sale turns a paper loss into a realised one and removes the holding from any later recovery. Planning liquidity in advance is what keeps the decision of what to sell independent of the decision of when cash is needed. The cost of the plan is the return given up by holding realisable assets; the cost of having no plan is paid at the worst available moment.

In practice

Who uses it
Treasury teams, funds with redemption terms, leveraged traders facing margin calls, and households with dated obligations.
Why it matters
It separates when money is needed from what must be sold to provide it, which is the only way to avoid selling into weakness by necessity.
Limitations
Liquidity is a property of the market, not of the holding. Depth measured in calm conditions overstates what is available in stress, and venue restrictions can block realisation entirely.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a position's quoted value as its realisable value.
  • Planning for scheduled outflows but not for margin or collateral calls.
  • Assuming a venue that allowed withdrawals yesterday will allow them tomorrow.

Examples

numeric

Known outflows over the next twelve months are 240,000 and a contingent margin requirement is 60,000. Against a 2,000,000 portfolio, 15% must be realisable inside that window before any other allocation question is reached.

Given
  • scheduled outflows over twelve months: 240,000
  • contingent margin or collateral requirement: 60,000
  • portfolio value: 2,000,000
Steps
  1. total near-term requirement = 240,000 + 60,000 = 300,000
  2. as a share of the portfolio = 300,000 / 2,000,000 = 0.15
15% of the portfolio is committed by the liquidity requirement

If the contingent amount is itself correlated with a market fall, it has to be met from assets that do not fall with it. That is a stricter requirement than holding 15% in liquid form, and the figures here are illustrative.

crypto

A trader holds collateral on one venue and plans a payment from it. The venue suspends withdrawals during a volatile weekend. The balance is unchanged on screen and unavailable in fact: a liquidity failure, not a price loss.

Commonly confused with

Liquidity

Liquidity is a property of a market at a moment. Liquidity planning is a portfolio process about dates and amounts, which uses that property as an input and does not control it.
