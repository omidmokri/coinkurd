Intrinsic Value

Professional

Simple definition

What an asset is judged to be worth on its own merits, from the cash it can produce, rather than what someone will pay for it today.

Professional definition

An estimate of value derived from an asset's expected cash flows, growth and risk, independent of its current market price. It exists only as the output of a model: change the assumptions and the estimate changes, often by more than the margin a decision rests on.

Technical definition

Formally the present value of expected future cash to the holder, so every estimate is a function of three analyst-chosen inputs: the cash flow path, the discount rate and the terminal growth rate. Because the terminal term divides by the difference between the latter two, small changes in either move the result disproportionately. The concept is useful as a discipline for stating assumptions, not as a fact that can be located and compared with price.

Academic definition

The present value of the cash flows an asset is expected to generate over its life, conditional on a specified forecast, a specified discount rate and a specified terminal condition.

Mechanism

In the constant-growth form the value of a perpetual stream is the next cash flow divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate. The denominator is a difference between two uncertain numbers, so as growth approaches the discount rate the quotient explodes. That is the whole reason intrinsic-value estimates are fragile: the model concentrates all the uncertainty into one small subtraction.

In practice

Who uses it
Fundamental analysts, long-horizon allocators, the value-investing tradition that pairs an estimate with a margin of safety, and audit and impairment teams.
Why it matters
Forcing a view into explicit assumptions makes a disagreement about price into a disagreement about growth and risk, which can be examined and argued.
Limitations
The estimate is a function of assumptions the analyst chose. There is no procedure that makes it objective, and a single number without a sensitivity range overstates what is known.

Common mistakes

  • Presenting one figure as the value instead of a range.
  • Reverse-engineering assumptions until the estimate matches a desired conclusion.
  • Confusing an estimate with the option-pricing sense of intrinsic value.

Examples

numeric

On cash flows of 100, 110 and 120, a 10 per cent discount rate and 3 per cent terminal growth give a value of 1,599. Shifting to 9 and 4 per cent gives 2,204; shifting to 11 and 2 gives 1,262. The forecast never changed.

Given
  • Free cash flow 100, 110, 120 in years 1 to 3
  • Terminal value from year 4 by perpetuity growth
  • Three assumption pairs: (r = 10%, g = 3%), (9%, 4%), (11%, 2%)
Steps
  1. Base case: explicit flows 271.98 + terminal 1,326.61 = 1,598.6
  2. Optimistic pair: 1 percentage point lower r and 1 higher g gives 2,204.4
  3. Cautious pair: 1 point higher r and 1 lower g gives 1,261.5
  4. Range as a share of the base case: +37.9% to -21.1%
1,262 to 2,204 — a spread of 75 per cent of the base value

Two cells moved by one percentage point each. This is the single most useful fact about intrinsic-value estimates.

case

A committee rejects an investment because the price is above the analyst's estimate of 1,599. Asked to re-run the model with a terminal growth rate one point higher, the same analyst produces an estimate above the price. Nothing about the company was re-examined.

Mathematical foundation

V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{E[CF_t]}{(1+r)^t}, \qquad V_0 = \frac{CF_1}{r-g} \text{ when growth is constant}

Procedure

State the expected cash flow path, the discount rate and the terminal condition in writing before computing anything. Discount the explicit flows, add the discounted terminal value, then re-run the whole calculation over a grid of plausible discount and growth rates and report the resulting range, not the midpoint alone.

Worked example

Given
  • CF = 100, 110, 120 for years 1 to 3, then perpetual growth
  • Case A: r = 0.10, g = 0.03
  • Case B: r = 0.09, g = 0.04
  • Case C: r = 0.11, g = 0.02
Steps
  1. Case A: 271.98 + 123.60/(0.07) discounted three years = 1,598.6
  2. Case B: same flows, denominator 0.05 and lower discounting = 2,204.4
  3. Case C: same flows, denominator 0.09 and higher discounting = 1,261.5
1,261.5 / 1,598.6 / 2,204.4 — the estimate moves -21% to +38% on one-point assumption changes

Interpretation

The output is the value implied by one stated set of assumptions. Its honest form is a range with the assumptions attached; a single figure presented as the intrinsic value conceals the fact that the analyst chose it.

Assumptions

  • The perpetuity form requires g strictly below r and a stable reinvestment rate. Long-run growth above the growth rate of the wider economy is not a sustainable assumption for a going concern.
  • Cash flow, discount rate and terminal condition must be consistent in nominal or real terms, and in the claim being valued.
  • The estimate is conditional on the model chosen. A dividend discount, free cash flow or residual income construction of the same company will not generally agree.
Limits of the formula
  • Highly sensitive to the terminal assumptions: in the worked example a one-point move in each of two inputs shifted the answer by more than seventy per cent of the base value.
  • Not an observable quantity, so it cannot be verified against anything; it can only be audited for the coherence of its assumptions.

Commonly confused with

Intrinsic and Time Value

In options, intrinsic value is an exact arithmetic fact: how far the option is in the money right now. In fundamental analysis it is a model estimate. Same words, opposite epistemic status.
