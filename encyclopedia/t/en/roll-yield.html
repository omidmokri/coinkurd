Roll Yield

Simple definition

The gain or loss that comes purely from closing an expiring contract and opening the next one. In an upward-sloping curve it is a cost; in a downward-sloping one it is a gain.

Professional definition

The component of a futures position's return attributable to moving exposure along the curve at each roll, rather than to the price of the underlying moving. It is negative in contango, because the replacement contract is dearer, and positive in backwardation.

Technical definition

A decomposition term, not an independent source of money. The total return of a continuously rolled futures position is conventionally split into a spot or price component, a roll component from the curve shape at each roll, and a collateral return on posted cash. The split is an identity given a stated roll rule and price convention, so roll yield changes if the roll schedule, the contract chosen or the reference price changes. It cannot be harvested without carrying the full price risk of the position.

Academic definition

The component of the realised return on a rolled futures position attributable to the term structure at each roll date, under a specified decomposition of total return into price, roll and collateral components.

Mechanism

Exposure must be moved before the contract dies, and the new contract has its own price. If the curve is upward-sloping, the same exposure costs more units of money to maintain, so value leaks at every roll even with an unchanged spot price. Nothing is being charged: the loss is the difference between the two prices the holder actually trades.

In practice

Who uses it
Managers of rolled futures programmes, commodity index investors, and anyone comparing a futures-based product with holding the underlying.
Why it matters
It explains why a long futures product can lag the spot price it tracks over years, and how much of a strategy's result came from curve shape rather than from the view.
Limitations
It is an attribution, not a strategy. Decomposition conventions differ, the sign flips for a short position, and a curve that paid last quarter can invert before the next roll.

Common mistakes

  • Calling roll yield a return you can collect without price risk.
  • Comparing two decompositions built on different roll rules.
  • Projecting a historical roll figure forward as if the curve were fixed.

Examples

market

A fund tracking a commodity through futures must sell each expiring contract and buy the next. Over a long contango stretch it ends up buying slightly higher every quarter, so its chart drifts below the spot price it was meant to follow.

numeric

A long roller sells the expiring contract at 100 and buys the next at 103. The roll return is about minus 2.9%. Repeated four times a year on a curve that keeps its shape, a flat spot price still leaves the position down roughly 11%.

Given
  • Expiring contract sold at F_1 = 100
  • Replacement contract bought at F_2 = 103
  • Long position
  • Four rolls a year
  • Spot unchanged and curve shape unchanged
Steps
  1. Roll return per roll = (100 - 103) / 103 = -0.02913
  2. Growth factor = 1 - 0.02913 = 0.97087
  3. 0.97087^4 = 0.8886
  4. Cumulative = 0.8886 - 1 = -0.1114
About -2.91% per roll and roughly -11.1% over four rolls, with no move in spot at all

A short position in the same curve would show the opposite sign. The figure is an attribution of what the holder traded, not a fee and not a forecast.

Mathematical foundation

R_{\text{roll}} = \frac{F_1 - F_2}{F_2}, \qquad R_{\text{total}} \approx R_{\text{price}} + R_{\text{roll}} + R_{\text{collateral}}

Procedure

At each roll, record the price at which the old contract is closed and the price at which the new one is opened. The roll return for a long is the first minus the second, divided by the second. Chain the per-roll figures over the period, then read the residual between total return and the price and collateral components as the roll contribution.

Worked example

Given
  • F_1 = 100 (expiring contract sold)
  • F_2 = 103 (next contract bought)
  • Long position
  • 4 rolls, spot and curve shape unchanged
Steps
  1. R_roll = (100 - 103) / 103 = -0.02913
  2. (1 - 0.02913)^4 = 0.8886
  3. Cumulative roll effect = -11.14%
R_roll = -2.91% per roll; about -11.1% compounded over four rolls

Interpretation

A negative figure means maintaining the exposure cost money on the curve. It is bookkeeping: it attributes a result after the fact and adds no return of its own. Treating a positive roll figure as income ignores that the same position carried the full price risk that produced it.

Assumptions

  • A stated roll rule: which contract is entered, how many days before expiry, and over how many days the roll is spread. Change the rule and the roll figure changes.
  • The decomposition is an identity only under one price convention. Defining the price component on the front contract, on spot, or on an index gives different splits of the same total return.
  • Competing definitions exist: some desks write roll yield as (F_1 - F_2) / F_1, some annualise it, and the sign convention here is stated for a long position — a short reverses it. Figures from different sources are not comparable without reading the convention.
  • Ignores transaction costs, the bid-ask spread paid twice at each roll, and any difference between the roll price and a closing print.
Limits of the formula
  • Roll yield is not a return source and cannot be isolated: capturing it requires holding the position and its full price risk.
  • Historical roll figures do not forecast future ones, because they depend entirely on a curve shape that changes with inventory, rates and borrowing conditions.
  • The concept presumes dated contracts. A perpetual contract never rolls, so its analogous cost is funding, not roll yield.

Commonly confused with

Funding Rate

Both are the cost of keeping leveraged exposure alive, but they arrive differently. Roll yield is realised when a dated contract is replaced by the next one; funding is a periodic payment on a contract that never expires.
