Unemployment rate

ENGLISHUnemployment ratereference

Category
Macroeconomics
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The share of people who want paid work, are available for it and are looking, but do not have it. Those not looking are outside the calculation entirely.

Professional definition

Unemployed persons as a percentage of the labour force, where the labour force is the employed plus the unemployed. Status comes from a household survey: being without work, available to start, and having searched recently. People outside the labour force do not raise the rate.

Technical definition

A survey-based ratio whose denominator is itself behavioural. Because search activity defines membership of the labour force, discouraged workers leaving the search pool lower the measured rate while employment is unchanged, and returning workers raise it. Agencies therefore publish a participation rate and broader underutilisation measures alongside it. Reference periods, age cut-offs and search-window rules differ by country.

Academic definition

The ratio of persons classified as unemployed to the economically active population, where classification rests on survey criteria of availability and active job search over a defined reference period.

Mechanism

The rate is a ratio, so it moves for two unrelated reasons: a change in how many people hold jobs, or a change in how many are counted as looking. A falling rate therefore does not prove a stronger labour market until the participation rate is checked alongside it.

In practice

Who uses it
Central banks with an employment mandate, macro strategists, and traders positioning around scheduled labour-market releases.
Why it matters
It is one of the two variables most central banks weigh against inflation, so surprises in it move expectations about the policy path and therefore most asset prices.
Limitations
It is a survey estimate with sampling error, published with a lag, revised, and blind to hours worked, underemployment and wage levels.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a lower rate as more jobs without checking participation.
  • Comparing national rates with different survey definitions.
  • Treating one month's change as a trend.

Examples

numeric

In a survey, 96 million are employed and 4 million unemployed. The labour force is 100 million and the rate is 4 percent. If 1 million stop searching, the rate falls to about 3.03 percent with no new jobs created.

Given
  • employed = 96 million
  • unemployed = 4 million
  • then 1 million of the unemployed stop searching and leave the labour force
Steps
  1. labour force = 96 + 4 = 100; rate = 4 / 100 = 4.0%
  2. after: unemployed = 3, labour force = 99
  3. rate = 3 / 99 = 0.030303...
rate falls from 4.00% to 3.03% with employment unchanged

Population figures are invented to show the arithmetic of the denominator, not drawn from any published survey.

market

A labour-market release that is weaker than expected can lift bond prices and risk assets at once, because traders read it as a softer policy path rather than as good news about the economy.

Mathematical foundation

u = \frac{U}{E + U} \times 100, \qquad LFPR = \frac{E + U}{POP} \times 100

Variables

UPersons classified as unemployed: without work, available, actively searchingpersons
EPersons classified as employed in the reference periodpersons

Procedure

Classify each surveyed person as employed, unemployed, or outside the labour force using the agency's criteria. Sum the first two for the labour force, divide the unemployed by it, and multiply by 100. Publish the participation rate beside it so denominator moves are visible.

Worked example

Given
  • E = 96 million
  • U = 4 million
  • POP = 160 million
Steps
  1. labour force = 96 + 4 = 100 million
  2. u = 4 / 100 x 100 = 4.0%
  3. LFPR = 100 / 160 x 100 = 62.5%
u = 4.0%, LFPR = 62.5%

Interpretation

A percentage of the measured labour force, not of the population. Read it only together with participation: the same unemployment rate can describe a tight market or a shrinking one depending on how many people are counted as searching.

Assumptions

  • Classification follows survey criteria for availability and active search over a defined reference week or weeks; different national rules give different levels for the same labour market.
  • The figure is a sample estimate and carries sampling error, so small month-to-month changes may not be statistically distinguishable from zero.
  • Age limits, treatment of students, military personnel and informal work vary by country.
Limits of the formula
  • It counts heads, not hours: a shift from full-time to part-time work can leave the rate unchanged.
  • Discouraged and marginally attached workers sit outside the numerator and the denominator, which is why broader underutilisation measures exist.

Commonly confused with

Non-farm payrolls

Payrolls count jobs on employer records; the unemployment rate comes from a household survey of people. Different samples, different definitions, and they can disagree for months.
