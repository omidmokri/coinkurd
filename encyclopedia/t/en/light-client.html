Simple definition

A wallet or app that keeps only block headers and asks others for the few details it needs, instead of storing the whole chain.

Professional definition

A client that verifies block headers and uses inclusion proofs for the specific transactions or state it cares about, downloading nothing more. It trades independent validation for a far smaller footprint, under extra trust assumptions.

Technical definition

Header verification plus a Merkle proof shows that a transaction sits in a block with a given header. What it cannot show is that the header's chain is the valid one, or that no conflicting state exists: the client assumes the chain it was served follows the rules, which simplified payment verification framed as relying on honest-majority block production. Later designs narrow the gap with signed commitments or validity proofs, but a trust or data-availability assumption always remains.

Academic definition

A verifier that authenticates block headers and membership proofs rather than re-executing the chain, achieving sublinear resource cost at the price of explicit trust or data-availability assumptions.

Mechanism

Headers are tiny and self-linking, so a phone can hold the chain's skeleton for a fraction of the cost of its body, and Merkle proofs then fill in only what is asked about. The saving is real and so is the gap: a light client inherits the chain-selection rule's security without being able to audit anything it did not download.

In practice

Who uses it
Mobile and hardware wallets, embedded devices, and bridges or contracts that must verify another chain from inside a chain.
Why it matters
It puts verification on phones and inside smart contracts, where downloading and executing an entire chain is simply not possible.
Limitations
Its security rests on assumptions a full node does not need: that it is served a valid chain, and that data it never sees is actually available.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a provider-hosted wallet a light client when it verifies nothing.
  • Assuming a light client would notice an invalid block.
  • Reading a Merkle proof as evidence that the chain itself is honest.

Examples

crypto

A phone wallet confirms an incoming payment from a header and a Merkle path of a few hundred bytes. It never learns whether some other block in that chain broke a rule.

everyday

It is like checking a bank statement's page number and stamp without auditing the bank. You can tell the page belongs to the book you were handed; you cannot tell the book is honest.

Commonly confused with

Custodial Wallet

A light client verifies proofs against headers it holds. A custodial wallet verifies nothing and simply shows a balance its operator asserts.
