Yield curve inversion

Simple definition

When short-dated government debt yields more than long-dated debt, the opposite of the usual shape. Lending for longer normally pays more, and in an inversion it does not.

Professional definition

A negative term spread: the yield on a short maturity exceeds that on a longer one for the same issuer. Commonly quoted as a specific pair, and different pairs invert on different dates, so the choice of pair determines when an inversion is said to begin.

Technical definition

Mechanically it means the market prices short rates to fall: long yields embed an average of expected future short rates plus a term premium, so an inversion implies expected policy easing, a compressed or negative term premium, or both. It has preceded recessions in a small number of United States episodes with highly variable lead times, which is far too small a sample to support confident prediction.

Academic definition

A negative slope of the term structure, interpretable through the expectations hypothesis plus a time-varying term premium, whose empirical association with subsequent contractions rests on few non-overlapping observations.

Mechanism

Long yields are roughly an average of expected short rates plus compensation for holding duration. A central bank that has tightened leaves short rates high, while investors expecting slower growth bid up long bonds, pushing their yields down. The curve inverts as those two forces meet — which is why it is better read as a market expectation than as a cause.

In practice

Who uses it
Rates strategists, bank treasury and ALM desks, and allocators who use the term spread as one input among several cycle indicators.
Why it matters
An inversion changes bank funding economics directly, since borrowing short to lend long stops being profitable, and it summarises the market's expected policy path in one number.
Limitations
The historical record is a handful of episodes with lead times ranging from months to years, and at least one inversion without a following recession, so it cannot carry a probability claim.

Common mistakes

  • Reading an inversion as a dated forecast of a recession.
  • Switching pairs until one inverts and calling that a signal.
  • Ignoring that the curve usually re-steepens before the downturn.

Examples

numeric

The two-year yields 4.6 percent and the ten-year 4.2 percent. The spread is minus 40 basis points: an inversion on this pair. A three-month versus ten-year pair might still be positive on the same day.

Given
  • two-year yield = 4.60%
  • ten-year yield = 4.20%
  • pair chosen: 10-year minus 2-year
Steps
  1. spread = 4.20 - 4.60
  2. = -0.40 percentage points = -40 basis points
term spread = -40 bp, an inversion on this pair

Yields are invented for the arithmetic and are not current or historical market levels. A different maturity pair can give a different sign on the same date.

market

A curve inverts, stays inverted for many months while equities keep rising, then re-steepens as the central bank cuts. Traders who shorted risk on the first day of inversion carried that position through the whole delay.

Mathematical foundation

S_{m,n} = y_{n} - y_{m}, \qquad n > m, \quad \text{inversion when } S_{m,n} < 0

Variables

S_{m,n}Term spread between the longer maturity n and the shorter maturity mpercentage points or basis points
y_{n}Yield on the longer maturity, same issuer and credit quality%
y_{m}Yield on the shorter maturity%

Procedure

Choose the maturity pair and state it explicitly. Take both yields from the same issuer curve, on the same construction basis and the same observation time, and subtract the shorter from the longer. A negative result is an inversion on that pair only.

Worked example

Given
  • y_10y = 4.20%
  • y_2y = 4.60%
Steps
  1. S = 4.20 - 4.60
  2. = -0.40 percentage points
S = -40 basis points

Interpretation

The sign tells you the slope between two points, nothing more. It is a market-implied statement about expected short rates and term premia, and it cannot be decomposed into those two parts without a model.

Assumptions

  • Both yields must come from the same issuer and the same curve construction; mixing par yields with zero-coupon rates changes the spread.
  • Which pair is used is a convention, not a fact: 10-year minus 2-year and 10-year minus 3-month invert on different dates and can disagree for months.
  • The decomposition into expected rates and term premium requires an estimated model and is not identified by the spread itself.
Limits of the formula
  • The spread is arithmetic; the recession association attributed to it is a separate empirical claim resting on very few episodes.
  • Zero or near-zero spreads are economically ambiguous, so a threshold-based signal is sensitive to small data revisions and to the pair chosen.

Commonly confused with

Yield curve

The yield curve is the whole mapping from maturity to yield. An inversion is one property of its shape between two chosen points, and the choice of points is part of the claim.
