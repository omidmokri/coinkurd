Simple definition

The extra risks of investing in a developing economy: the currency can fall, money can stop arriving from abroad, rules can change, and selling can become hard exactly when you need to.

Professional definition

The bundle of currency, external funding, policy, legal and liquidity risks that attach to assets in economies dependent on foreign capital. These risks are correlated: a global funding shock usually hits the currency, the bond market and local equities at the same time.

Technical definition

The characteristic pattern is a sudden stop: capital inflows reverse, the currency depreciates, foreign-currency debt service rises in local terms, and the authorities must choose between defending the currency, cutting rates to support activity and restricting flows. Diversification within the asset class works poorly in exactly those episodes, because the shared driver is external funding conditions rather than local fundamentals.

Academic definition

The joint distribution of currency, sovereign, policy and liquidity risks borne by investors in capital-importing economies, characterised by correlated tail outcomes driven by global financial conditions.

Mechanism

Capital-importing economies fund part of their investment abroad, often in a currency they do not issue. When global funding tightens, the lender's willingness to roll that financing falls for reasons unconnected to the borrower, and the exchange rate adjusts. The currency move then raises the local cost of the foreign debt, which deepens the shock that caused it.

In practice

Who uses it
Cross-border allocators, local-currency bond investors, corporate treasurers funding abroad, and risk teams setting country limits.
Why it matters
It explains why local yields can look attractive and still deliver losses after currency and funding effects, and why country limits exist separately from sector limits.
Limitations
The label covers very different economies, and grouping them hides large differences in reserves, debt currency and institutions; correlations also shift between episodes.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing local-currency yields without accounting for currency risk.
  • Treating the asset class as one exposure.
  • Relying on diversification within the class during a funding shock.

Examples

numeric

A local bond yields 12 percent in its own currency. If that currency falls 15 percent against the investor's base currency over the year, the return in base terms is about minus 4.8 percent.

Given
  • local-currency return = +12% = 0.12
  • currency depreciation against the base currency = 15%, so the rate factor is 0.85
  • no hedge
Steps
  1. 1.12 x 0.85 = 0.952
  2. return in base currency = 0.952 - 1 = -0.048
about -4.8% in base-currency terms

Both inputs are invented. The point is that a local yield is not a return until the currency leg is included.

market

External funding conditions tighten. Several unrelated economies see outflows in the same week, their currencies and local bonds fall together, and local macro news explains almost none of the timing.

Commonly confused with

Credit risk

Credit risk is the chance a specific borrower fails to pay. Emerging market risk includes that, but also currency, policy, legal and liquidity risks that can cause losses while every borrower keeps paying.

The category label groups economies that differ enormously in reserves, debt currency composition and institutions, and the entry says so rather than treating them as one. The sudden-stop pattern is described qualitatively; no episode, country, spread or frequency is cited, and the claim that correlations rise in funding shocks is stated as a documented tendency rather than a measured constant.
