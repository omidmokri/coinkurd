Trading Session

Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The period in which a market accepts trades. Stock exchanges open and close; most crypto venues never close, which changes how risk builds up.

Professional definition

The venue's defined trading window, with its own opening and closing procedures, auctions and breaks. Liquidity, spread and volatility are not uniform inside it: the open and the close concentrate volume, and overlaps between regional sessions concentrate it further.

Technical definition

A schedule, not a property of the asset. Session design decides when price discovery happens: opening and closing auctions aggregate orders into one print, continuous trading applies the venue's matching rule in between, and pre- and post-market phases often run under thinner rules. Instruments that trade continuously, most crypto among them, have no session boundary and so no auction to absorb accumulated information; it is absorbed in a thin book instead. Session times, breaks and holidays are venue calendars and differ.

Academic definition

The institutionally defined interval during which a venue accepts and matches orders, including any auction phases that bound it.

Mechanism

Information arrives continuously but a session does not trade continuously. Whatever accumulates while a market is shut must be priced at once when it reopens, which is why gaps and opening volatility concentrate at the boundary. A market that never closes converts that gap into a thin-book move instead.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone scheduling execution, risk teams setting overnight exposure, and traders holding across a weekend or a holiday.
Why it matters
Execution cost, gap risk and the usefulness of a stop all depend on where in the session you are, and on whether your market closes at all.
Limitations
Calendars are venue-specific and shift with holidays and contract rolls. Crypto has no session yet still shows weekday and regional liquidity patterns.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a stop protects you while the market is closed.
  • Comparing volume between a full session and a half day.
  • Treating a continuously traded market as uniformly liquid.

Examples

market

An exchange collects orders before the bell and crosses them in one opening auction. The first print is a single price agreed by everyone who participated, rather than the result of whoever happened to be fastest.

numeric

A market trading 09:30 to 16:00 is closed 17.5 hours each weekday and 65.5 hours from Friday's close to Monday's open. A stop cannot act in any of that time.

Given
  • continuous session 09:30 to 16:00, so 6.5 trading hours
  • no extended-hours trading considered
  • Friday close 16:00, Monday open 09:30
Steps
  1. closed per weekday = 24 - 6.5 = 17.5 hours
  2. Friday 16:00 to Monday 16:00 = 72 hours
  3. subtract the 6.5 hours to Monday's open: 72 - 6.5 = 65.5 hours
17.5 hours closed on a normal weekday, 65.5 hours over a weekend

Arithmetic on a stated schedule, not an observation of any particular exchange. Holidays and half days lengthen the closed window further.

Commonly confused with

Gap

A gap is the price jump observed across a closed period. The session is the schedule that creates the closed period in the first place; a market without sessions rarely gaps and instead moves through a thin book.
