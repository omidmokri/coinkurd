Simple definition

A shared record of transactions kept by many computers at once. Entries are grouped into blocks, and each block is cryptographically tied to the one before it, so old records cannot be quietly rewritten.

Professional definition

An append-only ledger replicated across independent nodes, where every block commits to its predecessor through a hash. A consensus rule decides which chain counts as canonical, so participants who do not trust one another can still agree on a single ordered history.

Technical definition

A replicated state machine whose state transitions are batched into hash-linked blocks. Tamper evidence comes from the hash chain: altering any past transaction changes every later block header. Settlement is probabilistic or economically final depending on the consensus mechanism, never instantaneous. Immutability is therefore an economic property — the cost of rewriting history — not a mathematical guarantee, and it holds only while no single party controls the resource consensus measures.

Academic definition

A distributed, append-only data structure in which batches of state transitions are sequenced into cryptographically hash-linked blocks, with a consensus protocol establishing one canonical ordering among mutually untrusting participants.

Mechanism

Each block header carries the hash of the one before it, so the tip of the chain summarises everything beneath it. Rewriting an old transaction means redoing the work, or re-staking the capital, behind every block since — while the rest of the network extends the honest chain. Security is that cost asymmetry, not secrecy.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol engineers, custody and settlement teams, auditors reconciling on-chain records, and analysts who treat the ledger itself as a data source.
Why it matters
It gives several parties one agreed history without a central record-keeper, which is what makes settlement, self-custody and on-chain analysis possible at all.
Limitations
A public chain proves what was recorded, not that the record is true, lawful, or that an address belongs to whoever a dashboard says it does.

Common mistakes

  • Treating confirmed on-chain data as verified real-world fact.
  • Calling a permissioned database a blockchain because it stores hashes.
  • Assuming one confirmation is final on every chain.

Examples

everyday

A village keeps one shared book of debts. Every page is numbered and stamped with a summary of the page before it, and every household holds a copy. Tearing a page out is possible; everyone else's copy exposes it.

numeric

Bitcoin aims for one block every ten minutes, so the common six-confirmation rule means about an hour of expected waiting. Because block discovery is random, a particular wait is often shorter and sometimes far longer.

Given
  • target block interval = 10 minutes
  • confirmations required = 6
Steps
  1. 6 x 10 = 60 minutes of expected waiting
  2. inter-block times are exponentially distributed, so the realised wait varies widely around 60
about 60 minutes expected, not guaranteed

A confirmation count is a risk setting chosen by the party crediting the deposit, not a protocol constant.

crypto

A one-block reorganisation can orphan a transaction that already showed as confirmed. This is why venues credit deposits only after several confirmations, and why depth of confirmation is a risk parameter rather than a yes-or-no state.

Commonly confused with

Smart Contract

The chain orders and stores; a smart contract is deployed code the chain executes. Every smart contract lives on a blockchain, but a blockchain needs no contracts to work.
