Simple definition

The risk that someone who owes you money does not pay. It is the oldest risk in finance and the reason lenders charge more to weaker borrowers.

Professional definition

The risk of loss from a borrower or counterparty failing to meet contractual payments, or from a deterioration in their creditworthiness that reprices the exposure. Lenders decompose it into the chance of default, the loss if it happens, and the amount at stake.

Technical definition

Conventionally expressed as expected loss, the product of probability of default, loss given default and exposure at default. The decomposition is an industry and regulatory convention rather than a derived law: the three components are correlated, estimated with wide error, and tend to worsen together in stress, so expected loss understates tail outcomes and is paired with unexpected-loss measures.

Academic definition

The risk of economic loss arising from an obligor's failure to perform contractual payment obligations, or from a change in the market's assessment of that likelihood.

Mechanism

A lender holds a fixed upside and an open-ended downside: the best case is being repaid as agreed, the worst is losing most of the principal. That asymmetry is why credit is priced as a spread over a risk-free proxy, and why spreads widen more violently than they narrow.

In practice

Who uses it
Banks and credit funds, trade-finance teams, bond investors reading spreads, and anyone extending settlement terms to a counterparty.
Why it matters
It determines the premium a lender must charge and the capital that must sit behind the exposure.
Limitations
Every input is an estimate, ratings are opinions rather than measurements, and correlations between defaults rise exactly when the model is most needed.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a credit rating as a probability.
  • Modelling defaults as independent across a portfolio.
  • Ignoring that collateral values fall in the same stress that triggers default.

Examples

market

Two bonds mature on the same date with the same coupon, but one yields three percentage points more. That gap is the market's price for the extra chance of not being paid, plus compensation for thinner liquidity.

numeric

A 1,000,000 loan with a 2% chance of default this year and an expected recovery of 60% carries an expected loss of 8,000 — which is a portfolio average, not a prediction about this one loan.

Given
  • EAD = 1,000,000
  • PD = 2% over one year
  • recovery = 60%, so LGD = 40%
Steps
  1. EL = 0.02 x 0.40 x 1,000,000
expected loss = 8,000 for the year

Illustrative inputs. The single loan either defaults or does not; 8,000 is the mean of a two-outcome distribution.

Mathematical foundation

EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD

Variables

ELExpected loss over the horizoncurrency
PDProbability of default over that horizondecimal
LGDLoss given default, one minus the recovery ratedecimal
EADExposure at default: the amount at risk when it occurscurrency

Procedure

Fix a horizon, usually one year. Estimate the default probability for that horizon, the fraction of exposure not recovered, and the exposure expected at the moment of default, then multiply. Keep the horizon identical across all three inputs.

Worked example

Given
  • PD = 2%
  • LGD = 40%
  • EAD = 1,000,000
Steps
  1. 0.02 x 0.40 = 0.008
  2. 0.008 x 1,000,000
EL = 8,000

Interpretation

Expected loss is a provisioning and pricing number, meaningful across many similar exposures. For a single loan the realised loss is almost never equal to it, which is why unexpected loss and capital buffers exist alongside.

Assumptions

  • The three components are treated as separable and multiplicative; in practice they are correlated and the product understates stressed outcomes.
  • PD estimates come from ratings, historical frequencies or market spreads, and these three sources routinely disagree.
  • Recovery rates are themselves cyclical, falling in the same conditions that raise default rates.
Limits of the formula
  • Expected loss says nothing about the distribution's tail, which is what causes insolvency; it must be read alongside stress testing.
  • For a single exposure the figure is a mean of a binary outcome and should never be presented as the likely loss.

Commonly confused with

Counterparty Risk

Credit risk is usually about a borrower failing to repay a loan or bond. Counterparty risk is the same failure on the other side of a trade or derivative, where exposure varies with market moves.
