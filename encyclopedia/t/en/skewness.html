Skewness

ENGLISHSkewnessreference

Simple definition

A number saying which side of the average the rare big moves sit on. Negative means the big surprises are losses; positive means they are gains.

Professional definition

The third standardised moment of a distribution. Zero means symmetry around the mean; negative means a long left tail, so most observations are small gains punctuated by occasional large losses — the shape of most short-volatility and carry returns.

Technical definition

The ratio of the third central moment to the cube of the standard deviation, which makes it scale-free and comparable across instruments. It is dominated by the cubed extreme observations, so it is unstable in small samples and undefined when the third moment fails to exist, as it can for sufficiently heavy tails.

Academic definition

The third standardised central moment of a distribution, a scale-invariant measure of asymmetry about the mean.

Mechanism

Cubing the deviations keeps their sign, so losses and gains do not cancel: a single very large loss pushes the whole statistic negative no matter how many small gains surround it. That is precisely the asymmetry that selling insurance, writing options and carrying leveraged positions all create.

In practice

Who uses it
Option writers, carry and short-volatility traders, risk managers reviewing a strategy's return shape, and allocators comparing managers with similar Sharpe ratios.
Why it matters
Negative skew matters far more to a leveraged trader than to a long-only investor: the unleveraged holder can sit through a large loss and wait for the mean, while leverage closes the position first.
Limitations
Sample skewness is dominated by the few largest observations, so it is noisy, sensitive to the sample window, and easy to make look favourable by excluding a crisis.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing two track records on Sharpe ratio while ignoring opposite skew.
  • Reading a long run of small gains as low risk.
  • Trusting a skewness estimate from a few dozen observations.

Examples

market

A short-volatility book collects a small premium almost every month, then gives back years of it in one shock. Its mean return can look excellent and its skewness is strongly negative; the leveraged version of the same book may not survive the shock at all.

numeric

Five monthly results: plus 1, plus 1, plus 1, plus 1, minus 4. The mean is zero and the second moment is 4, but the third moment is minus 12, giving a skewness of minus 1.5 — four comfortable months and one that erases them.

Given
  • observations: +1, +1, +1, +1, -4
  • n = 5
  • method-of-moments (population) formula applied to the sample
Steps
  1. mean = (1+1+1+1-4)/5 = 0
  2. m2 = (1+1+1+1+16)/5 = 20/5 = 4
  3. m3 = (1+1+1+1-64)/5 = -60/5 = -12
  4. skewness = m3 / m2^1.5 = -12 / 4^1.5 = -12 / 8
skewness = -1.5

Verified numerically. This is the moment-ratio form. The bias-adjusted sample estimator multiplies it by sqrt(n(n-1))/(n-2), which for n = 5 gives -1.5 x 1.4907 = -2.236, so the reported figure depends on which estimator the software uses.

Mathematical foundation

\gamma_1 = \frac{E\!\left[(X-\mu)^3\right]}{\sigma^3}, \qquad b_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^3}{\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2\right]^{3/2}}

Procedure

Compute the mean. Subtract it from each observation. Average the cubed deviations to get the third moment and the squared deviations to get the second. Divide the third by the second raised to the power one and a half. Report the sign first and the magnitude second, and state which estimator was used because the adjusted version gives a different number on the same data.

Worked example

Given
  • observations +1, +1, +1, +1, -4
  • n = 5
Steps
  1. mean = 0
  2. m2 = 20/5 = 4
  3. m3 = -60/5 = -12
  4. m2^1.5 = 4^1.5 = 8
  5. b1 = -12/8
b1 = -1.5 (method of moments); the bias-adjusted estimator gives -2.236 on the same data

Interpretation

The sign is the message. Negative skew says the rare observations are on the losing side, which converts directly into path risk once leverage is applied: the loss arrives before the mean does. Magnitude is harder to interpret, because it depends on the estimator and on the sample window.

Assumptions

  • The third moment must exist; for sufficiently heavy tails it does not, and the statistic then has no population value to estimate.
  • Several estimators are in common use — the plain moment ratio shown here and the bias-adjusted version multiplying by sqrt(n(n-1))/(n-2) — and they disagree materially in small samples, so the convention must be stated.
  • The measure assumes the observations come from one distribution; a regime change inside the window produces skewness that describes neither regime.
Limits of the formula
  • A single extreme observation can dominate the estimate, so the statistic is fragile and sensitive to the start and end dates of the sample.
  • Zero skewness does not mean symmetric risk: a distribution can be symmetric in its third moment while still carrying very heavy tails on both sides.

Commonly confused with

Kurtosis

Skewness says which side the extremes are on. Kurtosis says how heavy they are on both sides. A distribution can be symmetric and still have violent tails.
