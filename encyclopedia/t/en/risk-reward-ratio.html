Risk-Reward Ratio

Simple definition

How much a trade can make compared with how much it can lose, if both the target and the stop are respected. A ratio of three means three units of gain for one of risk.

Professional definition

The planned reward of a trade divided by its planned risk, both measured from entry: target distance over stop distance. It is a design property of the trade, fixed when the levels are chosen, and it sets the minimum win rate the strategy must achieve to avoid losing money.

Technical definition

A ratio of two planned price distances, not a measured outcome. Because it is defined on the plan, it is only meaningful alongside the frequency with which each leg is actually reached: a high ratio built from targets that rarely fill has a lower expected value than a modest ratio that fills often. Fees and slippage reduce the realised ratio on both legs.

Academic definition

The quotient of prospective favourable price excursion to prospective adverse excursion for a position, from which the break-even success frequency of a strategy follows directly.

Mechanism

The ratio works because it converts two prices into one comparable number, which can then be set against hit frequency. Its logic is arithmetic, not predictive: nothing about a wide target makes price more likely to reach it. Stretching the target raises the ratio and lowers the probability of the ratio ever being realised, and those two effects move against each other.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders planning entries and exits, strategy developers comparing rule sets, and risk reviewers reading a trade log.
Why it matters
It makes the break-even win rate explicit, so a strategy can be judged on arithmetic rather than on the feel of its last few trades.
Limitations
It is a plan, not a result. It ignores how often each leg is reached, and realised ratios are always worse than planned once costs are counted.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a planned ratio as if it were a realised one.
  • Widening the target to hit a target ratio.
  • Reading a high ratio as a high expected value on its own.

Examples

market

Two setups both plan a ratio of three. The first targets a level price has traded through repeatedly; the second targets an all-time high. Same ratio on paper, very different chance of the target ever filling.

numeric

Entry 100, stop 96, target 112. Risk is 4 and reward 12, so the ratio is 3 and the break-even win rate is one in four. At exactly 25 percent wins the arithmetic returns zero before costs.

Given
  • entry E = 100
  • stop S = 96
  • target T = 112
  • costs ignored
Steps
  1. risk per unit = E - S = 100 - 96 = 4
  2. reward per unit = T - E = 112 - 100 = 12
  3. R = 12 / 4 = 3
  4. break-even win rate = 1 / (1 + R) = 1 / 4 = 0.25
  5. check: 0.25 x 12 - 0.75 x 4 = 3 - 3 = 0
R = 3, break-even win rate = 25 percent

The check line is the whole point: at the break-even win rate the expected value per trade is exactly zero, so every cost takes the strategy below water.

Mathematical foundation

R = \frac{T - E}{E - S}\ \text{(long)}, \qquad w^{*} = \frac{1}{1 + R}

Procedure

Measure the target distance and the stop distance from the entry price in the same units. Divide reward distance by risk distance to get R. For a short, swap the numerator and denominator so both distances stay positive. The break-even win rate is one divided by one plus R.

Worked example

Given
  • E = 100
  • S = 96
  • T = 112
Steps
  1. risk = 100 - 96 = 4
  2. reward = 112 - 100 = 12
  3. R = 12 / 4 = 3
  4. w* = 1 / (1 + 3) = 1 / 4 = 0.25
  5. verification: 0.25 x 12 - 0.75 x 4 = 3.00 - 3.00 = 0
R = 3 and w* = 0.25, i.e. 25 percent of trades must reach target to break even before costs

Interpretation

R is the shape of the trade and the break-even win rate is its arithmetic consequence. Neither is a forecast. A strategy is viable only when its actual hit rate exceeds the break-even rate by enough to cover fees, slippage and funding.

Assumptions

  • Both exits are reached cleanly at their stated levels, with no partial fills and no gaps.
  • Costs are excluded. Including a round-trip cost c per unit gives R = (T - E - c) / (E - S + c), which is always smaller.
  • The break-even relation assumes every win is exactly the target and every loss exactly the stop; a trade log with scaled exits needs average win and average loss instead.
Limits of the formula
  • The ratio carries no information about the probability of either leg, so it cannot rank strategies on its own.
  • On instruments with funding or carry, holding time changes the realised ratio even when the price levels are hit exactly.

Commonly confused with

Win Rate

The ratio is the size of wins against losses; the win rate is how often wins happen. Either can be high while the strategy loses money, and only together do they give expected value.
