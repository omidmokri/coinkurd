Simple definition

The smallest price step a market allows. If the tick is one cent, prices can be 100.00 or 100.01 and nothing in between.

Professional definition

The minimum price increment for an instrument, set by the venue. It quantises the whole book: it is the floor on the spread, the unit of queue competition, and through the contract size it fixes the cash value of a one-step move.

Technical definition

A venue parameter rather than a property of the asset. A tick that is too large holds the spread artificially wide and pushes competition into time priority, since a quote cannot be improved by a fraction of a tick; a tick that is too small lets quotes be shaded by economically meaningless amounts and encourages queue-jumping. Tick regimes often vary by price band or by contract, and the cash value of one tick is the tick size multiplied by the contract size.

Academic definition

The minimum permissible price increment in a market, which discretises the price grid and thereby bounds the quoted spread from below.

Mechanism

Quantising price decides what traders can compete on. With a coarse grid they cannot undercut, so they compete on speed and on queue position; with a fine grid they can step in front for almost nothing, so displayed depth becomes less stable. The tick is therefore a lever on book quality, not a cosmetic detail.

In practice

Who uses it
Scalpers and market makers, risk teams converting a stop distance into money, and anyone sizing from a per-tick risk figure.
Why it matters
It converts a price distance into cash and sets the minimum possible spread, so it bounds how profitable very short-horizon trading can be.
Limitations
It is set and revised by the venue, regimes differ by price band and by contract, and inverse contracts break the simple tick-value identity.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming one tick size across contracts or price bands.
  • Converting ticks to money without the contract size.
  • Backtesting on prices finer than the real tick grid.

Examples

market

In an instrument whose tick is wide relative to its volatility, the spread sits at one tick almost always. Nobody can quote inside it, so the whole contest moves to who is earlier in the queue.

numeric

With a tick of 0.25 index points and a contract size of 50 currency units per point, one tick is worth 12.50 per contract. A twenty-tick stop on four contracts therefore risks 1,000.

Given
  • tick size 0.25 index points (illustrative)
  • contract size 50 currency units per index point (illustrative)
  • stop distance 20 ticks
  • 4 contracts
Steps
  1. tick value = 0.25 x 50 = 12.50 per contract
  2. risk per contract = 20 x 12.50 = 250
  3. total risk = 4 x 250 = 1000
12.50 per tick per contract, 1,000 of risk on the position

The parameters are illustrative. Each contract publishes its own tick size and contract size, and inverse contracts do not follow this identity.

Mathematical foundation

V_{tick} = \Delta P_{tick} \times M \times N

Variables

V_{tick}Cash value of a one-tick price move on the positionquote currency
\Delta P_{tick}Tick size: the minimum price incrementprice units
MContract size or multiplier: currency per one unit of pricecurrency per price unit
NNumber of contracts or units heldcount

Procedure

Read the tick size and the contract size from the instrument's specification. Multiply them to get the cash value of one tick for one contract, then multiply by the number of contracts. For spot instruments where one unit of quantity is one unit of the asset, the contract size is one.

Worked example

Given
  • tick size 0.25
  • contract size 50 currency units per price point
  • N = 4 contracts
Steps
  1. 0.25 x 50 = 12.50 per contract per tick
  2. 12.50 x 4 = 50.00 per tick on the whole position
50.00 of profit or loss per tick on four contracts

Interpretation

The output translates price movement into money for the specific position held. It is the bridge between a stop expressed in ticks and a risk budget expressed in currency.

Assumptions

  • For spot and most crypto pairs the contract size is one unit of the asset, so the tick value equals the tick size times quantity.
  • Inverse and quanto contracts do not satisfy this identity: in an inverse contract the cash value of a tick depends on the price level itself.
  • Tick size can vary by price band on the same instrument, so the applicable increment must be read for the current band.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity is linear and therefore does not describe instruments whose payoff is non-linear in price.
  • Venues revise tick regimes, so a tick value computed once is not a durable parameter of a strategy.

Commonly confused with

Lot Size

Tick size quantises price; lot size quantises quantity. A venue sets both independently, and an order can be rejected for violating either.
