Simple definition

When contracts for later delivery cost more than the price today, and more the further out you look. The futures curve slopes upward.

Professional definition

A term structure in which futures prices sit above spot and rise with time to delivery. It is the normal shape when holding the asset costs more than it yields: financing, storage and insurance are embedded in the later price.

Technical definition

An upward-sloping forward curve, so that the futures price exceeds spot and distant contracts exceed near ones. Under the cost-of-carry relation it occurs when financing plus storage exceeds the convenience yield of holding the physical asset. The shape is a statement about carry, not a forecast: it does not imply the market expects a higher spot price, and the curve flattens into expiry because settlement forces convergence.

Academic definition

A configuration of the forward curve in which futures prices are increasing in time to maturity and exceed the prevailing spot price, consistent with a net positive cost of carry.

Mechanism

Someone who buys the asset today and sells it forward must be paid for the money tied up and the space it occupies. That cost is what lifts the far contract above the near one. If the gap grew beyond the real cost of carrying, the trade would become free money and arbitrageurs would sell the forward until it did not.

In practice

Who uses it
Commodity and crypto futures traders, index and commodity fund managers who must roll, and arbitrageurs running cash-and-carry trades.
Why it matters
The curve's slope tells a holder what maintaining exposure through time will cost, which is separate from whether the price itself goes up.
Limitations
It describes carry today, not direction tomorrow. The shape can flip within weeks, and inferring expected spot prices from it is a theory claim, not an observation.

Common mistakes

  • Reading an upward curve as a bullish forecast.
  • Holding a long rolled position in contango and blaming the tracking gap on fees.
  • Assuming the slope is stable enough to plan a year of rolls.

Examples

market

A storable commodity in ample supply usually trades in contango: the warehouse is full, nobody needs the goods urgently, and whoever holds them wants paying for the finance and the space. The curve rises with delivery date.

numeric

Spot is 100, the three-month contract 101.5 and the six-month 103.0. Each three months of carry costs about 1.5% of spot, which annualises near 6% — a cost that must be paid again at every roll if the shape persists.

Given
  • S = 100
  • F(3m) = 101.5
  • F(6m) = 103.0
Steps
  1. Three-month carry = (101.5 - 100) / 100 = 1.50%
  2. Annualised = 1.50% x 4 = 6.0% simple
  3. Three-to-six-month segment = (103.0 - 101.5) / 101.5 = 1.48%
An upward curve with roughly 1.5% of carry per quarter, about 6% a year on a simple basis

Simple annualisation is used for transparency. Compounding and exact day counts shift the figure slightly and conventions differ by desk.

Mathematical foundation

F_{t,T} = S_t e^{(r + u - y)(T-t)}, \qquad \text{contango} \iff r + u > y

Variables

F_{t,T}Futures or forward price at time t for delivery at Tprice units
S_tSpot price at time tprice units
rFinancing rate over the remaining termper year
uStorage, insurance and handling cost expressed as a rateper year

T-tTime remaining to deliveryyears

Procedure

Compare the net cost of carrying the asset — financing plus storage, less the convenience of having it on hand — with zero. If the net is positive, deferred contracts must price above nearer ones and the curve is in contango; its slope is that net carry per unit of time.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 100
  • r = 4% a year
  • u = 2% a year
  • y = 0
  • T - t = 0.25 years
Steps
  1. r + u - y = 0.06
  2. 0.06 x 0.25 = 0.015
  3. e^0.015 = 1.01511
  4. F = 100 x 1.01511 = 101.51
F(3m) = 101.51, above spot: contango of about 1.5% for the quarter

Interpretation

The relation prices carry, not expectation. A steep contango says it is expensive to hold the asset, which is why a long position that must keep rolling bleeds even when spot is unchanged. Reading it as the market's forecast of spot confuses a cost with a prediction.

Assumptions

  • Cost-of-carry arbitrage is available: the asset can be bought, stored, financed and shorted without binding constraints.
  • Rates and storage costs are treated as known over the term; for non-storable or hard-to-borrow underlyings the relation becomes an inequality rather than an identity.
  • Convenience yield y is not directly observable and is usually backed out of the curve, which makes the identity harder to falsify than it looks.
  • Some authors use contango to mean futures above the expected future spot price rather than above today's spot; this entry uses the observable definition, against today's spot and across maturities.
Limits of the formula
  • Crypto perpetual markets have no delivery and no storage, so a persistent premium there is sustained by funding rather than by carry; the vocabulary transfers but the mechanism differs.
  • The relation says nothing about how long a shape will last, and curve shapes change with inventory, rates and borrowing conditions.

Commonly confused with

Basis

Basis is a level: the gap between one futures price and spot. Contango is a shape: the whole curve rising with maturity. A single positive basis does not establish contango across the curve.

Both the Persian and Kurdish headwords retain the English word, which is what practitioners use. Two definitions of contango circulate: futures above today's spot, and futures above the expected future spot. This entry uses the first, observable one and flags the second in the assumptions rather than treating them as interchangeable. Convenience yield is inferred from the curve, not measured, which limits how strongly the identity can be tested.
