Long and Short

Foundation

Simple definition

The two directions a position can take. Long profits if the price rises; short profits if it falls. Short means selling something you do not own and buying it back later.

Professional definition

The sign of exposure to an asset. A long gains one unit of currency per unit of price rise and loses on a fall; a short is the mirror image, created by borrowing the asset or by selling a derivative contract. The two are not symmetric in risk, cost or mechanics.

Technical definition

A long's loss is bounded, since price cannot fall below zero, while a short's loss has no upper bound because price has no ceiling. A short also carries obligations a long does not: borrow availability and borrow fees in cash markets, funding payments on perpetuals, and exposure to recalls or squeezes when the borrow becomes scarce.

Academic definition

The directional sign of an exposure, where a positive position profits from price appreciation and a negative position from depreciation, with asymmetric loss bounds and differing financing obligations.

Mechanism

Direction decides which way the payoff slopes, and the asymmetry follows from the number line: price is bounded below by zero and unbounded above. That single fact gives a long a worst case it can name and a short one it cannot, which is why short exposure is normally carried with a hard exit rather than patience.

In practice

Who uses it
Every market participant, since holding cash against an asset is itself a directional choice, plus hedgers and market makers who run both sides deliberately.
Why it matters
It is the first decision in any trade and it determines which risks apply: borrow and squeeze risk on one side, opportunity cost on the other.
Limitations
Shorting is not available in every market or to every client, and its cost is not just the price move: borrow fees and funding accrue for as long as the position is held.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a short as a long with the sign flipped.
  • Holding a short through a funding or borrow-cost regime that quietly exceeds the expected move.
  • Assuming a falling market makes a short easy to exit.

Examples

market

A long in a token that goes to zero loses everything put into it, and no more. A short in the same token during a sudden squeeze can lose several times the collateral posted, which is why venues liquidate shorts rather than wait.

numeric

Two units short from 100 gain 20 if price falls to 90, and lose 60 if it rises to 130. The long side of the same two units would gain 60 at 130 and lose at most 200, the whole value, if price reached zero.

Given
  • Q = 2 units
  • entry price = 100
  • exit scenarios: 90 and 130
  • fees, borrow costs and funding excluded
Steps
  1. short at 90: 2 x (100 - 90) = +20
  2. short at 130: 2 x (100 - 130) = -60
  3. long at 130: 2 x (130 - 100) = +60
  4. long at 0: 2 x (0 - 100) = -200, the floor on a long's loss
  5. short at any higher price: loss grows without limit, since price has no ceiling
the long's worst case is -200; the short's worst case is unbounded

This is the asymmetry, stated plainly. It is a property of the payoff, not a claim that either direction is more likely to be profitable.

Mathematical foundation

\text{PnL}_{\text{long}} = Q\,(P_{\text{exit}} - P_{\text{entry}}), \qquad \text{PnL}_{\text{short}} = Q\,(P_{\text{entry}} - P_{\text{exit}})

Variables

QPosition size in units of the instrumentunits
P_{\text{entry}}Price at which the position was openedprice
P_{\text{exit}}Price at which the position was closedprice
\text{PnL}Realised profit or loss before financing and feesaccount currency

Procedure

Take the price difference in the direction the position profits and multiply by size. For a long that is exit minus entry; for a short, entry minus exit. Then subtract financing: borrow fees and interest in cash markets, funding payments on perpetuals, and commissions on both sides.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 2 units
  • entry = 100
  • exit = 90 for the short, then 130
Steps
  1. short closed at 90: 2 x (100 - 90) = 2 x 10 = +20
  2. short closed at 130: 2 x (100 - 130) = 2 x (-30) = -60
  3. long closed at 130: 2 x (130 - 100) = +60
  4. long floor: at a price of 0, 2 x (0 - 100) = -200
the identity is symmetric in form, but the long's loss stops at -200 while the short's does not stop at all

Interpretation

The two formulas are mirror images and the two risks are not. A long can be sized against a known worst case; a short cannot, which is why its sizing has to come from a stop rather than from the notional.

Assumptions

  • A linear instrument quoted in the account currency, with the quantity unchanged between entry and exit.
  • Financing is excluded from the identity and must be subtracted separately: borrow fees, interest, funding payments and commissions.
  • Inverse contracts, where the position is denominated in the base asset, produce a non-linear payoff in quote-currency terms and need a different expression.
  • No dividends, coupons, staking rewards or airdrops, all of which accrue to one side and not the other.
Limits of the formula
  • The expression says nothing about financing, which on a long-held short can dominate the price move entirely.
  • The unbounded loss on a short is a real property of the payoff, but in practice forced liquidation intervenes first, so the realised loss is capped by the venue rather than by the formula.

Commonly confused with

Order Types

Direction and order type are independent choices. A sell order can open a short or close a long, and the ticket looks the same either way; only the resulting net exposure says which happened.
