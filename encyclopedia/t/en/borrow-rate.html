Borrow Rate

Simple definition

What a borrower pays for a loan from a lending pool. It is not negotiated: the more of the pool that is already borrowed, the higher the rate climbs.

Professional definition

The interest charged on outstanding debt, recomputed every block from a published function of utilisation. Most protocols use a kinked curve: a gentle slope up to a target utilisation, then a much steeper one above it, so the rate itself is the mechanism that keeps some of the pool withdrawable.

Technical definition

The rate is a control variable, not a price discovered by bargaining. Below the kink the slope reflects the normal cost of credit; above it the steep segment is a penalty designed to force repayment and attract supply. Because the input is utilisation and not credit quality, the rate carries no information about borrower risk, and it rises in a withdrawal squeeze exactly as it would in a borrowing boom. Each protocol sets base, kink and both slopes separately per asset, so quoted rates are not comparable across venues.

Academic definition

An algorithmically determined cost of funds in a pooled credit market, specified as a piecewise-linear function of pool utilisation and serving as the equilibrating mechanism for reserve availability rather than as a credit-risk price.

Mechanism

The curve does two jobs with one number. In the gentle segment it allocates credit at something like a market cost. Past the kink it stops being a price and becomes a lever: a rate that doubles in a few percentage points of utilisation makes holding the debt painful and supplying very attractive, which is how the pool pulls itself back from being fully drawn.

In practice

Who uses it
Borrowers costing a leveraged position, suppliers reading the other side of the same curve, and risk teams choosing the kink and the slopes.
Why it matters
It is the whole cost of carrying an on-chain loan, and its shape tells a borrower how quickly that cost can change.
Limitations
The rate is variable with no cap and no notice. The same quoted number means different things across protocols because the curve parameters differ by asset.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing a position on today's rate as if it were fixed for the holding period.
  • Comparing one protocol's quoted rate with another's without the curves.
  • Ignoring that a rate spike can come from suppliers leaving, not borrowers arriving.

Examples

numeric

With a base of zero, a slope reaching 7 percent at a kink of 80 percent utilisation and a steep slope of 100 percent above it, the borrow rate is 7 percent at the kink and about 57 percent at 90 percent utilisation.

Given
  • base rate = 0
  • slope 1 = 7% reached at the kink
  • kink U* = 80%
  • slope 2 = 100% applied over the remaining 20% of utilisation
  • all four parameters are illustrative, not any particular protocol's
Steps
  1. at U = 0.80: r = 0 + (0.80 / 0.80) x 0.07 = 7.0%
  2. at U = 0.90: excess = (0.90 - 0.80) / (1 - 0.80) = 0.50
  3. r = 0.07 + 0.50 x 1.00 = 0.57, i.e. 57%
A ten-point rise in utilisation past the kink takes the rate from 7% to 57%.

Real protocols use steeper or gentler second slopes; the point is the discontinuity in sensitivity, not these numbers.

market

A borrower opens a position at a comfortable rate. Large suppliers then withdraw, utilisation crosses the kink, and the cost of the same unchanged debt multiplies within a day — with no change in the borrower's own risk.

Mathematical foundation

U = \frac{B}{C + B}, \qquad r_b = \begin{cases} r_0 + \dfrac{U}{U^{*}}\,s_1, & U \le U^{*} \\[6pt] r_0 + s_1 + \dfrac{U - U^{*}}{1 - U^{*}}\,s_2, & U > U^{*} \end{cases}

Procedure

Compute utilisation from borrowed and available balances. If it sits at or below the kink, add the first slope in proportion to how far along that segment utilisation has reached. If it is above, add the whole first slope plus the second in proportion to progress through the remaining range. The result is an annualised rate that the contract accrues per block.

Worked example

Given
  • C = 800,000
  • B = 7,200,000
  • r_0 = 0
  • U* = 0.80
  • s_1 = 0.07
  • s_2 = 1.00
Steps
  1. U = 7,200,000 / 8,000,000 = 0.90
  2. U > U*, so use the upper branch
  3. (U - U*) / (1 - U*) = 0.10 / 0.20 = 0.50
  4. r_b = 0 + 0.07 + 0.50 x 1.00 = 0.57
Borrow rate 57% annualised at 90% utilisation.

Interpretation

The number is the current cost of debt, not a forecast of it: it is recomputed from pool balances every block and can move far without any action by the borrower. Reading the slope above the kink matters more than reading today's level, because that slope is the risk.

Assumptions

  • A two-segment kinked model, which is the common shape but not universal: some protocols use three segments, a smooth curve, or rates set by governance or an external market.
  • All four parameters are per-asset protocol settings. The values used in the worked example are illustrative and not drawn from any live market.
  • Rates are quoted annualised; the contract accrues per block, so the realised cost compounds within the year.
Limits of the formula
  • Utilisation conflates credit demand with supply withdrawal, so the rate is a poor signal of borrower quality or of market stress direction.
  • The model is a design choice, not an equilibrium result; there is no guarantee the chosen slopes keep the pool liquid in a severe run.
  • Quoted figures are not comparable across protocols or across assets within one protocol.
