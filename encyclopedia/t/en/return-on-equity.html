Return on Equity

Professional

Simple definition

Profit expressed as a percentage of the owners' recorded capital. It asks how much the company earned on the money the shareholders have left in it.

Professional definition

Net income attributable to ordinary shareholders divided by average ordinary shareholders' equity. The DuPont decomposition splits it into profit margin, asset turnover and the equity multiplier, which separates operating performance from the effect of borrowing.

Technical definition

A ratio of a flow to a stock, so the denominator convention matters: opening, closing or average equity give different figures. Because the equity multiplier is one of its three factors, leverage raises it arithmetically without any improvement in operations, and buybacks raise it by shrinking the denominator. A high value is informative only once its source among the three factors is identified.

Academic definition

The ratio of net income available to ordinary shareholders to the book value of ordinary equity, decomposable as the product of net profit margin, total asset turnover and the ratio of assets to equity.

Mechanism

The three DuPont factors multiply, so the same return can be reached by selling at a good margin, by turning assets over quickly, or simply by financing the assets with debt instead of equity. The first two are operating achievements; the third is a choice about risk. Reading the headline number without the split mistakes borrowed leverage for business quality.

In practice

Who uses it
Equity analysts, boards setting performance targets, banking supervisors, and anyone comparing a firm's return with its cost of equity.
Why it matters
It connects the income statement to the balance sheet in one figure and, through DuPont, shows exactly where that figure came from.
Limitations
It is computed on book equity, which understates intangible-heavy firms and can be made small or even negative by buybacks, so the ratio can be flattered or undefined.

Common mistakes

  • Praising a high return that comes entirely from the equity multiplier.
  • Using closing equity in a year with a large issue or buyback.
  • Comparing the ratio across firms with different intangible accounting.

Examples

numeric

Net income 120, revenue 1,500, assets 1,900 and equity 700 give a return on equity of 17.1 per cent. DuPont splits it into an 8 per cent margin, 0.79 asset turnover and an equity multiplier of 2.71 — more than half the return comes from the last factor.

Given
  • Net income = 120
  • Revenue = 1,500
  • Total assets = 1,900
  • Shareholders' equity = 700
Steps
  1. Net profit margin = 120 / 1,500 = 0.0800
  2. Asset turnover = 1,500 / 1,900 = 0.7895
  3. Equity multiplier = 1,900 / 700 = 2.7143
  4. Product = 0.0800 x 0.7895 x 2.7143 = 0.1714
  5. Direct check: 120 / 700 = 0.1714
ROE = 17.1%

Unlevered — that is, with assets equal to equity — the same margin and turnover would have produced only 6.3 per cent.

market

Two banks report the same return on equity. One earns it on a thin margin with heavy leverage, the other on a wide margin with much more capital. A supervisor reads these as opposite risk profiles, although the headline ratio is identical.

Mathematical foundation

ROE = \frac{NI}{E_{avg}} = \frac{NI}{S} \times \frac{S}{A} \times \frac{A}{E}

Variables

Procedure

Divide net income attributable to ordinary shareholders by average ordinary equity. To decompose, compute net profit margin, then revenue over total assets, then total assets over equity, and multiply the three — the revenue and asset terms cancel, so the product must equal the direct ratio.

Worked example

Given
  • NI = 120
  • Revenue = 1,500
  • Total assets = 1,900
  • Equity = 700
Steps
  1. 120 / 1,500 = 0.0800
  2. 1,500 / 1,900 = 0.7895
  3. 1,900 / 700 = 2.7143
  4. 0.0800 x 0.7895 x 2.7143 = 0.1714
ROE = 17.14%, of which the leverage factor contributes a multiple of 2.71 on an unlevered 6.3%

Interpretation

The figure is an accounting return on recorded owner capital for one period. It creates value only when it exceeds the cost of equity, and it is comparable only against that cost or against the same firm's own history.

Assumptions

  • The DuPont identity is true by construction, not an empirical finding: the revenue and asset terms cancel, so it explains the composition of the ratio and proves nothing about causation.
  • Numerator and denominator must be defined consistently: income after preferred claims against equity excluding preferred capital, and averaged equity against a flow measured over the same period.
  • Five-factor variants that separate tax and interest burden are also standard; which decomposition is used must be stated.
Limits of the formula
  • Rises with leverage and with buybacks, so a higher ratio is not by itself evidence of better operations.
  • Unreliable or undefined when book equity is small, negative, or dominated by goodwill from past acquisitions.

Commonly confused with

Profit Margin

Profit margin measures profit per unit of sales. Return on equity measures profit per unit of owners' capital. A firm can have a thin margin and a high return on equity if it turns its assets over fast or borrows heavily.
