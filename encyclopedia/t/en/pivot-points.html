Pivot Points

Simple definition

A set of levels calculated from the previous session's high, low and close, used as reference prices for the next session.

Professional definition

The floor-trader formula set. The pivot is the mean of the prior session's high, low and close; the first resistance and support reflect the low and the high about it; the second pair adds the prior range on each side; the third pair extends it once more. All seven levels are fixed before the session opens.

Technical definition

Pivot points are arithmetic, not inference: given three prior prices the levels are determined, which is exactly what makes them usable as a shared reference. Their meaning depends entirely on the session definition, so in 24/7 crypto the choice of daily boundary — UTC midnight, exchange-local, or a futures session — changes every level while the underlying price data is identical. Competing families exist, including Woodie, Camarilla, Fibonacci and DeMark, and they give different numbers.

Academic definition

A deterministic set of reference levels derived from the preceding period's high, low and close by a fixed arithmetic rule.

Mechanism

The levels work as coordination devices rather than as forces. Because many participants compute them from the same public prices with the same formula, orders cluster at the same numbers, and a level that many people watch is where resting liquidity ends up. That mechanism is self-referential: it works to the extent the convention is shared, which is why it is strongest where one session definition is agreed.

In practice

Who uses it
Intraday futures and equity traders, desks that need shared reference prices, and anyone automating session-based levels.
Why it matters
The levels are fixed before the open and identical for everyone using the same session, so they are unambiguous prices to plan around.
Limitations
Nothing in the arithmetic predicts anything, the levels change with the session boundary chosen, and the competing formula families disagree, so a result means little without both stated.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing pivot results computed on different session boundaries.
  • Mixing classic levels with Camarilla or Woodie values in one rule.
  • Assuming a crypto daily pivot is as widely watched as a futures one.

Examples

market

A futures contract has one settlement and one agreed session, so every desk computes the same pivot. A crypto pair trades continuously, so one desk uses the UTC day, another the exchange day, and the two sets of levels never coincide.

numeric

A session prints a high of 120, a low of 100 and a close of 112. The pivot is 110.67, the first levels 121.33 and 101.33, the second 130.67 and 90.67, the third 141.33 and 81.33.

Given
  • prior session high H = 120
  • prior session low L = 100
  • prior session close C = 112
Steps
  1. P = (120 + 100 + 112) / 3 = 110.67
  2. R1 = 2 x 110.67 - 100 = 121.33; S1 = 2 x 110.67 - 120 = 101.33
  3. R2 = 110.67 + (120 - 100) = 130.67; S2 = 110.67 - 20 = 90.67
  4. R3 = 120 + 2 x (110.67 - 100) = 141.33; S3 = 100 - 2 x (120 - 110.67) = 81.33
Seven levels, all determined before the next session begins.

Change the session boundary and H, L and C change, so all seven numbers change. The formula is exact; the inputs are a convention.

Mathematical foundation

\begin{aligned} P &= \frac{H + L + C}{3} \\ R1 &= 2P - L, \qquad S1 = 2P - H \\ R2 &= P + (H - L), \qquad S2 = P - (H - L) \\ R3 &= H + 2(P - L), \qquad S3 = L - 2(H - P) \end{aligned}

Procedure

Take the high, low and close of the previous completed session and average them to get the pivot. Double the pivot and subtract the low for the first resistance, and subtract the high for the first support. Add the prior range to the pivot for the second resistance and subtract it for the second support. For the third pair, add twice the distance from low to pivot above the high, and subtract twice the distance from pivot to high below the low.

Worked example

Given
  • H = 120
  • L = 100
  • C = 112
Steps
  1. P = 332 / 3 = 110.67
  2. R1 = 221.33 - 100 = 121.33
  3. S1 = 221.33 - 120 = 101.33
  4. R2 = 110.67 + 20 = 130.67
  5. S2 = 110.67 - 20 = 90.67
  6. R3 = 120 + 21.33 = 141.33
  7. S3 = 100 - 18.67 = 81.33
P 110.67, R1 121.33, R2 130.67, R3 141.33, S1 101.33, S2 90.67, S3 81.33.

Interpretation

The output is a set of reference prices, not a signal. Their only content is that a known formula applied to yesterday's three prices produces these numbers, and that other participants using the same session will have the same ones on their screens.

Assumptions

  • H, L and C come from the previous completed session. What counts as a session is a convention, and in continuously traded markets it is a free choice that changes every level.
  • The formulas above are the classic floor-trader set. Woodie weights the close and uses the current open, Camarilla applies different multipliers to the range, and Fibonacci pivots scale the range by retracement ratios. None of these agree with each other.
  • Some implementations publish only P, R1, S1, R2 and S2; the third pair is an extension of the same range and is reached rarely.
Limits of the formula
  • The calculation is deterministic and carries no predictive content of its own. Whatever effect the levels have comes from how many people are watching the same numbers.
  • In 24/7 crypto markets there is no single agreed session, so the coordination mechanism that gives floor pivots their force is weaker than in a futures market with one settlement.
  • Comparing a published pivot statistic with your own requires matching both the formula family and the session boundary; otherwise the two are measuring different levels.

Commonly confused with

Support and Resistance

Support and resistance are levels read from how price has behaved. Pivot points are computed from a formula before the session starts, whether or not price has ever reacted there.
