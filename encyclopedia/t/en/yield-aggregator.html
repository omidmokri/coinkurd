Yield Aggregator

Simple definition

A contract that takes your deposit, puts it to work in other protocols, harvests the rewards and reinvests them for you. You hold one position instead of managing several.

Professional definition

A vault that pools deposits, allocates them to underlying yield sources by a defined strategy, and compounds the proceeds automatically. It converts a manual routine of claiming and redepositing into a single position, charging a management or performance fee for doing so.

Technical definition

The aggregator earns its fee from two things: gas amortisation, because one harvest serves every depositor, and frequency, because compounding more often raises the realised annual figure. Neither changes the underlying return. What it does change is the risk surface, which becomes the union of the vault contract, every protocol it routes into, and the strategist's permission to reallocate. A depositor who cannot enumerate the current strategy cannot enumerate the risk.

Academic definition

An intermediating contract that aggregates deposits and reallocates them across yield-bearing protocols under a programmatic strategy, converting gas and monitoring economies into compounding frequency while concentrating the risks of every downstream dependency.

Mechanism

Compounding frequency is the honest part of the proposition: the same periodic return earns more when it is reinvested more often, and a shared vault can harvest frequently because one gas cost is split across all depositors. The dishonest part is when that arithmetic is presented as outperformance. The vault delegates allocation, so the depositor exchanges a known set of risks for one they can no longer see directly.

In practice

Who uses it
Depositors too small for gas-efficient manual harvesting, treasuries wanting one position instead of ten, and strategists running the vaults.
Why it matters
It automates compounding and routing, spreads gas across depositors, and removes a routine that is easy to neglect.
Limitations
Fees reduce the net return, the strategy can change without the depositor acting, and the vault adds its own contract risk on top of every protocol it uses.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a vault's annual figure with an underlying simple rate as if the difference were skill.
  • Not checking which protocols the strategy currently routes into.
  • Assuming an exit is always available when the underlying positions may not be liquid.

Examples

numeric

A 10 percent simple annual rate compounded daily reaches about 10.52 percent, and weekly about 10.51 percent. Compounding adds roughly half a point here; a vault charging two percent would leave less than the uncompounded rate.

Given
  • nominal annual rate r = 10%
  • n = 365 compounding periods
  • vault fee = 2% of assets per year
Steps
  1. APY = (1 + 0.10/365)^365 - 1 = 0.10516
  2. compounding gain = 10.52% - 10.00% = 0.52 points
  3. net of a 2% fee = 10.52% - 2.00% = 8.52%, below the 10% uncompounded rate
Compounding adds 0.52 points; a 2% fee removes four times that.

Fee structures differ and some are charged on profits rather than assets; the comparison is arithmetic, not a recommendation.

market

A vault's published strategy moves from a lending pool to an incentivised liquidity pool. The headline yield rises, but the position now carries divergence loss and a reward token it did not hold the week before.

Mathematical foundation

APY = \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n} - 1

Variables

APYEffective annual return after compoundingfraction or %
rNominal annual rate before compoundingfraction or %
nNumber of compounding periods per yearperiods

Procedure

Divide the nominal rate by the number of compounding periods, add one, raise to the power of that number of periods and subtract one. Then subtract the vault's fees on the same basis they are charged. Compare the result with the underlying source's own quoted figure, not with a different protocol's.

Worked example

Given
  • r = 0.10
  • n = 365
Steps
  1. r / n = 0.10 / 365 = 0.000274
  2. 1 + 0.000274 = 1.000274
  3. 1.000274^365 = 1.10516
  4. APY = 0.10516
APY = 10.52% from a 10% nominal rate compounded daily.

Interpretation

The gap between the nominal rate and the annual figure is arithmetic, not performance. It is largest when rates are high and frequency low, and it is routinely smaller than the fee charged to capture it, which is the comparison worth making.

Assumptions

  • A constant nominal rate over the year. On-chain rates are variable, so any annual figure is a projection of a current rate, not an earned return.
  • Rewards are reinvested in full at each period and no gas or swap cost is deducted inside the compounding.
  • Fees are stated separately; management fees on assets and performance fees on profits are not interchangeable.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity describes compounding only. It cannot capture divergence loss, reward-token price falls, or a strategy change mid-period.
  • Quoted annual figures from vaults are not comparable with each other unless the fee basis and compounding frequency are the same.
  • No annualised figure from a variable source should be read as an expected return.
