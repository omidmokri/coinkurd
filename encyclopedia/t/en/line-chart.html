Foundation

Simple definition

A chart that plots one price per period, almost always the close, and joins the points with a line.

Professional definition

The minimal price rendering: one chosen value per interval, nearly always the close, connected into a single curve. It carries no open, high or low, so intrabar range, wicks and gaps are invisible — and the series it shows is exactly the close series most indicators are computed from.

Technical definition

Because it plots only the close, a line chart is the visual form of the series that RSI, MACD and most moving averages actually consume. That makes it useful for drawing levels without wick noise and for long histories where bars overlap, and useless for anything defined on open-to-close relations or on range. Its smoothness is an artefact of linear interpolation between two points, not evidence that price travelled that path.

Academic definition

A rendering of a price series as an ordered sequence of one representative value per interval, linearly interpolated between observations, discarding intrabar extremes.

Mechanism

Joining closes removes the excursions that were rejected inside each interval, so what remains is the settled result of each period. That is why levels drawn on closes often look cleaner than the same levels drawn on wicks: the chart has already filtered out the attempts that failed.

In practice

Who uses it
Long-horizon analysts, anyone comparing several instruments on one panel, and traders who draw levels on closing prices only.
Why it matters
It shows the close series directly, which is the input most indicators take and the cleanest basis for drawing a level.
Limitations
It hides range, gaps and single-bar rejections, so volatility looks lower than it was and candlestick patterns cannot be seen at all.

Common mistakes

  • Judging volatility from how smooth a line chart looks.
  • Placing a stop on a line chart, which cannot show the wick that would trigger it.
  • Reading the interpolation between two closes as a real price path.

Examples

chart

On a weekly line chart covering a decade the trend is legible at a glance. The same window in candles shows hundreds of overlapping wicks and direction is harder to read — but the line chart cannot show a single-bar capitulation at all.

numeric

A bar closes at 101 after reaching 112. On the line chart nothing rises above 101, so a stop at 105 looks untouched. In the real series that stop was reached intrabar, seven points before the close printed.

Given
  • close = 101
  • high of the same bar = 112
  • stop placed at 105 after reading a line chart
Steps
  1. line chart plots 101 only, so 105 appears untouched
  2. the bar's true high was 112
  3. 112 > 105, therefore the stop was reached inside the bar
The stop triggers even though the line chart never rises above 101.

Any backtest that fills stops from close-only data understates how often they trigger. This is a data-resolution error, not a strategy result.

Commonly confused with

Candlestick

A candle reports four prices for the interval; a line chart reports one. The line is not a simplified candle, it is a different dataset.
