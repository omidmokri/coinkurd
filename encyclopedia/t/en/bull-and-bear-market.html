Bull and Bear Market

Simple definition

A bull market is a long stretch of rising prices with confident buyers. A bear market is the opposite: a long slide in which sellers set the pace.

Professional definition

Labels for the direction of a sustained price regime rather than for a single move: a bull market makes higher highs and higher lows over months, a bear market the reverse. A commonly quoted rule of thumb puts the boundary at a 20 percent move from the last extreme, but no official body defines it.

Technical definition

The labels are regime descriptions with no canonical definition. Practical identification uses one of three rules: a threshold move from the last extreme, commonly 20 percent; the sign of a long moving average's slope; or a sequence of higher highs and higher lows. Each produces different dates for the same history, and each dates a turn only after its threshold is met, so the current regime is always provisional. Crypto regimes are faster and deeper than equity regimes, which makes an equity threshold a poor import.

Academic definition

Conventional labels for persistent directional regimes in asset prices, operationalised variously by threshold moves from extrema, by trend-filter sign, or by swing-sequence rules, with no canonical definition.

Mechanism

Regimes persist because their inputs persist: funding conditions, flows into and out of the asset class, and the willingness of holders to realise losses all change slowly. Trends also feed themselves, because collateral values rise with prices and support more risk-taking, then fall with them and force selling. That loop is why a regime outlasts any single piece of news.

In practice

Who uses it
Allocators, risk managers, commentators, and systematic developers whose rules switch on a regime filter.
Why it matters
Position size, holding period and the base rate for any setup all change with the regime, so naming it keeps expectations honest.
Limitations
No definition is canonical, every rule dates a turn late, and the 20 percent figure was never more than a convention. Backtests that label regimes with hindsight overstate every result.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a bear market over after one strong week.
  • Importing the equity 20 percent threshold into crypto unchanged.
  • Labelling regimes in a backtest with hindsight.

Examples

market

A market falls for a year in a series of lower highs, then rallies for three months without exceeding the previous year's high. Under a threshold rule the bull market has begun; under a structure rule the downtrend is intact. Both are defensible.

numeric

An asset falls from 100 to 78, a drop of 22 percent, and then rises to 95, a gain of 21.8 percent from the low. Under one 20 percent rule the same series is in a bear market and a bull market at the same time.

Given
  • high = 100
  • low = 78
  • subsequent price = 95
  • convention: a 20 percent move from the reference extreme
Steps
  1. fall from the high: (78 - 100) / 100 = -22%, past the threshold
  2. rise from the low: (95 - 78) / 78 = +21.8%, also past the threshold
  3. 95 is still 5 percent below the high
Both labels are satisfied at once under the same threshold.

A threshold rule needs a stated reference point and a stated minimum duration, or the regime is not well defined.

Commonly confused with

Drawdown

A drawdown measures the decline from a peak for a series or a portfolio, in any regime. A bear market is a claim about the regime itself, and needs a threshold and a duration before it means anything.
