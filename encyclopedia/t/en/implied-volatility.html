Implied Volatility

Simple definition

The volatility number that makes an option-pricing model produce the price the option is actually trading at. It is read out of the market price, not forecast.

Professional definition

The volatility input that, substituted into an agreed pricing model, reproduces an option's observed market premium. It is therefore a quote in volatility units: a restatement of the premium conditional on the model, not an independent estimate of future variability.

Technical definition

The volatility parameter that solves the inversion of a pricing model for a given market premium, found numerically because the model cannot be inverted in closed form. Inverting one model at many strikes and maturities yields not a single number but a surface, and the smile or skew that appears is direct evidence that the model's constant-volatility assumption is wrong. Change the model and the implied figure changes with it.

Academic definition

The value of the volatility parameter that equates a specified option-pricing model's theoretical value to the observed market premium, obtained by numerical inversion.

Mechanism

Traders quote premiums, not volatilities. Implied volatility appears because inverting a model turns a premium into a number comparable across strikes, maturities and underlyings. It moves with demand for optionality, so it usually rises when markets fall and protection is bid, which is why it reads as a price of insurance rather than a forecast.

In practice

Who uses it
Options market makers, volatility traders, risk managers mapping option exposure, and anyone comparing option prices across strikes or assets.
Why it matters
It normalises premiums onto one comparable scale, and the shape of the surface shows where the market is paying for protection.
Limitations
It is model-dependent and backed out of a price, so it inherits every flaw of the model and every distortion of the quote, including a wide spread or a stale mark.

Common mistakes

  • Calling implied volatility a forecast of realised volatility.
  • Comparing figures computed under different models or day counts.
  • Calling a high IV expensive without checking realised volatility.

Examples

market

In a sharp sell-off, put premiums rise faster than the underlying falls, so implied volatility jumps and the downside strikes imply more volatility than the upside ones. The surface has steepened because protection is being bid, not because a model learned anything.

numeric

At a 60% volatility input the model prices one call at 5.10 and at 70% it prices it at 5.90. The option trades at 5.50, so the implied volatility lies between them, near 65%. No forecast was made: a price was translated.

Given
  • one call option with fixed strike, time to expiry, underlying price and interest rate
  • model price at a 60% volatility input = 5.10
  • model price at a 70% volatility input = 5.90
  • observed market premium = 5.50
Steps
  1. the model price of a vanilla option rises monotonically with the volatility input, so the solution is bracketed by 60% and 70%
  2. (5.50 - 5.10) / (5.90 - 5.10) = 0.50
  3. implied volatility is approximately 60% + 0.50 x 10% = 65%
implied volatility about 65%

Illustrative figures. A real solver iterates numerically rather than interpolating linearly, and the answer is valid only for the model, the inputs and the day-count convention used to produce it.

Procedure

Fix every input except volatility at its observed value, then search numerically for the volatility that makes the model value equal the traded premium. The model value of a vanilla option rises monotonically with volatility, so the solution is unique and a bisection or Newton search converges quickly. Repeat at each strike and maturity to build the surface.

Worked example

Given
  • model price at sigma = 60% is 5.10
  • model price at sigma = 70% is 5.90
  • market premium = 5.50
  • all other inputs held fixed
Steps
  1. the solution is bracketed because 5.10 < 5.50 < 5.90
  2. (5.50 - 5.10) / (5.90 - 5.10) = 0.50
  3. sigma_imp = 60% + 0.50 x (70% - 60%)
sigma_imp is approximately 65%

Interpretation

The output is a quoting convention: the premium expressed in volatility units under a stated model. A change in implied volatility is a change in the price of the option and nothing more.

Assumptions

  • A model must be named before the number means anything. Black and Scholes (1973) is the usual reference, and its assumption of a single constant volatility is contradicted by the smile its own inversion produces.
  • The remaining inputs -- the risk-free rate, any dividend or carry, the exact time to expiry and the day-count convention -- must be the same ones the quoting desk used, or the figures are not comparable.
  • The market premium used must be a tradeable price. Inverting a stale mark or the midpoint of a very wide spread produces a number that carries no information.
  • Monotonicity in volatility, which guarantees a unique solution, holds for vanilla European options. It is not a general property of all option structures.
Limits of the formula
  • It is not a forecast. It is a price, and the relationship between implied volatility and subsequently realised volatility is a research question, not an identity.
  • Model-dependence means the same premium implies different volatilities under different models, so implied volatilities are only comparable within a single stated convention.
  • Because the whole premium of a far out-of-the-money option is time value, implied volatility there is extremely sensitive to small pricing errors and to the bid-ask spread.

Commonly confused with

Volatility

Realised volatility is measured from past price data. Implied volatility is extracted from option prices under a model. They are different objects and routinely differ in value.
