Simple definition

Rules for tokens that promise to hold a steady value. They focus on what backs the token, who may issue it, and whether a holder can actually redeem it.

Professional definition

The regulatory treatment of tokens that reference a currency or other assets, centred on issuer authorisation, composition and segregation of reserves, redemption rights at par, disclosure, and in some regimes limits on use at scale. The emphasis is prudential because the instrument is a liability presented as money.

Technical definition

The regulatory problem is a run, not a price. A token that promises par convertibility carries a maturity and liquidity mismatch whenever its reserves are not immediately realisable in full, so rules concentrate on reserve quality, custody, same-day redeemability and the enforceability of the holder's claim. Analysts also distinguish tokens by backing model, because an algorithmic or collateral-in-kind design has no reserve to regulate in the same way. Regimes diverge sharply, including on whether issuance is permitted at all.

Academic definition

The prudential and conduct framework applied to tokens denominated in or referencing other assets, directed at reserve adequacy, redemption enforceability and the systemic consequences of a liability instrument circulating as a means of payment.

Mechanism

Anything that circulates at par is treated as money, and money is only as good as the promise behind it. If reserves are high quality and immediately redeemable, the promise holds; if they are longer-dated or illiquid, the first wave of redemptions is met and later ones are not. Regulation targets reserve composition and the redemption right, which decide whether lost confidence stays a discount or becomes a run.

In practice

Who uses it
Issuers and their reserve managers, banks and custodians holding reserves, payment supervisors and central banks, and holders relying on par value.
Why it matters
It determines whether a holder has an enforceable claim at par, what the reserves may consist of, and who may issue at scale in a given jurisdiction.
Limitations
Regimes diverge sharply and are still being built; some permit issuance under conditions, others restrict it. Disclosure about reserves is not the same as a verified, enforceable right.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a published reserve report as evidence of an enforceable redemption right.
  • Assuming all stablecoin designs present the same regulatory problem.
  • Reading a stable market price as proof the backing could meet mass redemption.

Examples

regulatory

Two tokens trade at the same price. One carries an authorised issuer, segregated reserves and a redemption right; the other carries a design document. The market price says nothing about the difference between them.

numeric

Tokens outstanding of 100 are backed by reserves of 100, but only 70 are realisable same-day. Redemption requests of 85 in one day leave 15 unmet without selling longer assets at a discount. Full backing is not the same as same-day liquidity.

Given
  • Tokens outstanding = 100
  • Total reserves at book value = 100
  • Reserves realisable same-day = 70
  • Same-day redemption requests = 85
Steps
  1. Same-day capacity = 70
  2. Unmet same-day demand = 85 - 70 = 15
  3. Meeting it requires selling longer-dated reserves, at a discount if markets are stressed
15 of 85 unmet from liquid reserves despite full nominal backing

Illustrative figures describing no real issuer. The point is the mismatch that reserve-composition rules address: a solvent reserve can still fail a same-day redemption test.

Commonly confused with

Stablecoin Depeg

A depeg is a market outcome. This entry is the rulebook aimed at making a depeg less likely and the holder's claim enforceable when one happens.

Commonly confused with

Central Bank Digital Currency

A central-bank digital currency is a public liability. A stablecoin is a private liability that regulation tries to make safe enough to circulate.

JURISDICTION WARNING: rules differ by country and change, and this is among the fastest-moving areas of financial regulation. Regimes diverge on fundamentals: whether issuance is permitted, who may issue, what reserves must consist of, whether redemption at par is a legal right and whether any volume limits apply. No reserve-composition rule, redemption deadline, volume cap or capital requirement is stated here, because such detail is jurisdiction-specific and dates quickly; the European regimes are cited as one example. No issuer, token or reserve report is assessed or endorsed, and the figures in the numeric example describe no real issuer.
