Simple definition

Financial services built as programs on a blockchain: trading, lending and interest-like returns, run by code instead of a bank, and open to anyone with a wallet.

Professional definition

A set of protocols that reproduce exchange, credit and derivatives functions in public smart contracts. Positions are held by the user's keys, collateral is posted on-chain, and rules execute automatically, which removes account-opening and settlement intermediaries while adding contract, oracle and governance risk.

Technical definition

The distinctive features are composability and overcollateralisation. Contracts call each other freely, so a position can span several protocols and inherit the weakest assumption among them; and because no protocol can assess a borrower, credit is extended against collateral worth more than the loan, with liquidation triggered by a price feed rather than by default. Both features concentrate risk in shared dependencies: a handful of oracles, bridges, stablecoins and governance keys.

Academic definition

A set of financial arrangements implemented as interoperable smart contracts on public ledgers, substituting collateral and automated execution for intermediated credit assessment and settlement.

Mechanism

Without identity or enforcement, a protocol cannot price a promise, so it prices collateral instead and liquidates on a feed. That makes positions safe for the protocol and fragile for the user: a fall in collateral value, a stale oracle or congestion at the wrong moment can force a sale. Because contracts compose, stress in one widely used protocol propagates to everything built on top of it.

In practice

Who uses it
On-chain traders, liquidity providers, borrowers against crypto collateral, and analysts tracking protocol exposures.
Why it matters
It offers settlement without an account, composable building blocks, and positions that can be audited on-chain by anyone.
Limitations
Advertised returns are not risk-free: they compensate contract, oracle, liquidation and token-price risk, and decentralisation is often partial, with upgrade keys, front-ends and oracle operators forming real control points.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a quoted yield as an interest rate on a deposit.
  • Ignoring that one oracle or stablecoin sits under several positions.
  • Assuming no one can change a protocol's rules after you enter.

Examples

numeric

A borrower posts 10,000 of collateral and draws 5,000, where the protocol liquidates below a 150% ratio. Liquidation arrives when the collateral is worth 7,500 — a 25% fall. The loan never became unaffordable; the price feed simply crossed a line.

Given
  • Collateral posted = 10,000
  • Loan drawn = 5,000
  • Minimum collateral ratio = 150%
  • Fees and interest ignored
Steps
  1. Required collateral = 5,000 x 1.50 = 7,500
  2. Buffer = 10,000 - 7,500 = 2,500
  3. Tolerable fall = 2,500 / 10,000 = 25%
A 25% fall in collateral value triggers liquidation

Thresholds, penalties and feeds differ by protocol. In congestion the realised liquidation price can be worse than the threshold implies.

market

A protocol described as fully decentralised keeps an upgrade key, a single price feed and a hosted front-end. Each is a point at which one party can change what users experience, whatever the branding says.
