Protective Put

ENGLISHProtective Putreference

Simple definition

Owning an asset and buying a put on the same amount of it, so that losses below the strike stop. The protection costs the put's premium.

Professional definition

A long underlying position combined with a purchased put on the same quantity. Position value at expiry is max(S,K) less the premium paid, which puts a floor at the strike while leaving the upside open above the breakeven of purchase price plus premium.

Technical definition

Equivalent by put-call parity to holding a call at the same strike plus the discounted strike in cash. The position is long gamma and long vega, so it gains in a disorderly fall and bleeds premium in a calm market. The floor applies at that expiry only: maintaining it requires buying the next put at whatever the market then charges, which is highest after volatility has already risen.

Academic definition

A long position in an underlying combined with a long put on the same notional, producing a convex payoff bounded below by the strike net of the premium paid.

Mechanism

The put transfers the distribution's left tail to someone else for a fee. Because the fee is charged per expiry, protection behaves like an insurance premium rather than an asset: it is consumed whether or not it is claimed. The writer prices it from the volatility input, so protection costs most once a fall already looks likely, and least when it feels unnecessary.

In practice

Who uses it
Holders who must stay invested but cannot carry a large drawdown, funds with mandated loss limits, and anyone holding through a scheduled event.
Why it matters
It sets a known floor without selling the asset, so a position can survive an event the holder could not otherwise sit through.
Limitations
The floor is temporary and repriced each expiry. Over many rolls the cumulative premium becomes the dominant cost, and in a calm market it is pure drag.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a single put's cost with an annual return as if it were an annual cost.
  • Buying protection after volatility has repriced and the premium already reflects it.
  • Choosing a strike so far below spot that the floor never binds.

Examples

market

A holder who cannot sell before a scheduled event buys a put expiring after it. If the event passes quietly the premium is gone and nothing else changes; if the asset falls hard the loss stops at the strike.

numeric

Asset at 100, 95 put bought for 4. Maximum loss is 9, whatever happens below 95. Breakeven on the upside is 104. At 80 the position loses 9 instead of 20; at 120 it gains 16 instead of 20.

Given
  • asset purchase price S_0 = 100
  • put strike K = 95
  • premium paid p = 4
  • one unit, European exercise, fees ignored
Steps
  1. maximum loss = (100 - 95) + 4 = 9, for any S_T <= 95
  2. upside breakeven = 100 + 4 = 104
  3. at S_T = 80: max(80, 95) - 100 - 4 = -9
  4. at S_T = 120: 120 - 100 - 4 = 16
loss floored at 9; upside reduced by the 4 premium at every level

If the same put is bought four times a year at the same cost, the protection consumes roughly 16 of notional per year before any compounding. Illustrative arithmetic, not a market quote.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Take the higher of the expiry price and the strike, because below the strike the put makes up the difference. Subtract the purchase price and the premium paid. The floor is the gap between purchase price and strike plus the premium; the upside breakeven is purchase price plus premium.

Worked example

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • K = 95
  • p = 4
  • S_T = 80
Steps
  1. max(80, 95) = 95
  2. 95 - 100 = -5
  3. -5 - 4 = -9
loss 9, against 20 for holding the asset alone

Interpretation

The arithmetic shows what is bought and what it costs: a fixed worst case, paid for with a certain reduction of every outcome. It is insurance, and like insurance it is expected to be a cost most of the time.

Assumptions

  • Stated per unit, for a European-style purchased put held to expiry, with the underlying quantity matching the contract size.
  • Fees, the carry or financing of the asset and any dividend or staking yield are excluded, as is the cost of rolling into the next expiry.
  • The equivalence to a call plus cash follows from put-call parity and holds under the same frictionless assumptions.
  • The floor binds at expiry. Before expiry the position's mark depends on the put's remaining time value, so an intra-life valuation can sit below the floor arithmetic.
Limits of the formula
  • The cost is per expiry, so the annual cost of a maintained floor is the sum of the rolls, not the price of one contract. That total is the dominant number over long horizons and depends on the volatility input at each roll.
  • Protection is priced by the same market it protects against, so its cost is highest after a fall has begun.
  • This entry describes mechanics and costs. It is not a recommendation to hedge, nor a claim that hedging improves long-run outcomes.

Commonly confused with

Stop-Loss

A stop-loss sells the asset at the market when a level trades, with no cost upfront and no guarantee of the fill. A protective put is paid for in advance and the floor holds regardless of how the market gaps.
