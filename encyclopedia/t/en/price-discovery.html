Simple definition

The process by which trading turns scattered information and opinions into one public price.

Professional definition

The process through which order flow incorporates information into price. It happens where informed traders choose to trade, which is why one venue or contract often leads and the others follow, and why turnover alone does not confer price leadership.

Technical definition

The dynamic by which dispersed private information is impounded into the observable price through informed participants trading against liquidity suppliers. It is a property of a market rather than of a chart: which venue leads is testable, and leadership tends to settle where informed flow finds the lowest cost of trading, often a liquid derivative rather than the underlying. Call auctions and continuous books discover price differently, and the measured leader can change with the time of day.

Academic definition

The process by which heterogeneous private information is aggregated into a public transaction price through the interaction of informed order flow and liquidity supply.

Mechanism

An informed trader can only profit by trading, and trading moves the price. The liquidity supplier, unable to tell informed from uninformed flow, widens the quote and revises it after each trade. Price therefore drifts toward the information precisely because somebody paid to act on it.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone choosing which venue or contract to watch, index and benchmark designers, and regulators assessing market quality.
Why it matters
It tells you where the price you are reacting to is actually being made, which decides which feed is a signal and which is only an echo.
Limitations
Leadership is an empirical question answered with specific statistical methods, and the answer is period-dependent rather than a permanent property.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the venue with the highest turnover leads.
  • Reading a lagging venue's print as independent confirmation.
  • Treating discovery as something that happens at a level rather than through trading.

Examples

market

A liquid futures contract moves first and the cash market follows within seconds. Both show the same number afterwards, but only one of them made it; the other reported it.

numeric

If the value is 110 or 90 with equal prior odds and 30% of buys are informed, one buy order raises the rational expected value from 100 to 103. The trade itself carried the information.

Given
  • value V is 110 or 90, each with prior probability 0.5, so the prior expected value is 100
  • a fraction 0.3 of orders come from informed traders, who buy only when V = 110
  • the remaining 0.7 are uninformed and buy or sell with probability 0.5 each
Steps
  1. P(buy | V = 110) = 0.3 + 0.7 x 0.5 = 0.65
  2. P(buy | V = 90) = 0.7 x 0.5 = 0.35
  3. P(V = 110 | buy) = 0.65 / (0.65 + 0.35) = 0.65
  4. E[V | buy] = 0.65 x 110 + 0.35 x 90 = 71.5 + 31.5 = 103
the expected value revises from 100 to 103 on a single buy order

A deliberately simple version of the sequential-trade reasoning in the cited model, with assumed parameters. It shows the mechanism, not any real market's magnitudes.

Commonly confused with

Efficient Market Hypothesis

The hypothesis is a claim about the end state: prices already reflect available information. Price discovery is the mechanism that would have to do the reflecting, and it has a cost and a speed.
