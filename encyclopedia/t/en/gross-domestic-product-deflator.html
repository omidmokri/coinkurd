GDP deflator

Simple definition

The price measure built into GDP: divide output valued in today's money by the same output at constant prices, and you see how much of growth was price rather than quantity.

Professional definition

Nominal GDP divided by real GDP, multiplied by 100. Because it covers everything the economy produces — consumption, investment, government, exports — its basket shifts with output itself, so it is not a fixed consumer basket and does not match CPI.

Technical definition

An implicit price index obtained by dividing nominal GDP by a constant-price real GDP estimate. Implicit because agencies deflate components separately and the aggregate falls out of the division. Its weights are current-period output shares, making it Paasche-type rather than Laspeyres-type, and it is revised whenever real GDP is revised. It prices domestic production, so imported consumer goods enter only through the components they affect.

Academic definition

An implicit deflator defined as the ratio of a nominal output aggregate to its constant-price counterpart, whose weights are endogenous to the composition of current production.

Mechanism

Nominal GDP moves for two reasons: more was produced, or the same was sold dearer. Real GDP removes the second by valuing quantities at constant prices. Whatever gap remains between the two series is, by construction, the price change — which makes the deflator a residual of the accounts rather than a survey of prices.

In practice

Who uses it
National accountants, macro analysts converting nominal series to real ones, and anyone comparing growth across periods or countries.
Why it matters
It is the correct deflator for output aggregates, and the gap between it and CPI shows where price pressure sits — domestic production or the consumer basket.
Limitations
It is a by-product of GDP estimation, published quarterly with long revision tails, so it is late and unstable compared with monthly consumer price data.

Common mistakes

  • Deflating a consumption series with the GDP deflator instead of a consumer index.
  • Reading a deflator-CPI gap as an error in one of them.
  • Treating an early quarterly deflator as final.

Examples

numeric

An economy reports nominal output of 1,250 and real output of 1,000 at base-year prices. The deflator is 125, meaning the price level of everything produced is 25 percent above the base year.

Given
  • nominal GDP = 1,250 (current prices)
  • real GDP = 1,000 (base-year prices)
  • base year index = 100
Steps
  1. ratio = 1,250 / 1,000 = 1.25
  2. deflator = 1.25 x 100
GDP deflator = 125

Figures are invented for the arithmetic. A level of 125 is cumulative price change since the base year, not an annual inflation rate.

market

A quarter where import prices fall sharply can show softer CPI inflation while the deflator stays firm, because imported consumer goods leave the consumer basket cheaper but are not part of domestic production.

Mathematical foundation

P^{GDP}_{t} = \frac{Y^{nom}_{t}}{Y^{real}_{t}} \times 100

Variables

P^{GDP}_{t}GDP deflator in period t, indexed to the base periodindex (base = 100)
Y^{nom}_{t}Nominal GDP: output valued at the prices of period tcurrency
Y^{real}_{t}Real GDP: the same output valued at constant (base or chained) pricescurrency, constant prices

Procedure

Take nominal and real GDP for the same period and the same vintage of the accounts. Divide the first by the second and multiply by 100 to get the level. For an inflation rate, take the percentage change of that level between two periods, not the level itself.

Worked example

Given
  • nominal GDP = 1,250
  • real GDP = 1,000
  • previous-period deflator = 120
Steps
  1. current deflator = 1,250 / 1,000 x 100 = 125
  2. inflation = (125 - 120) / 120
  3. = 0.041666...
deflator = 125; implied period inflation = 4.17%

Interpretation

The level says how much the price of all domestic output has moved since the base period. Its change over time is an inflation rate for production as a whole, which is a different question from the cost of a household's basket.

Assumptions

  • Nominal and real GDP must come from the same published vintage; mixing vintages produces a meaningless ratio.
  • Modern national accounts use chain-weighted real GDP, so the implied deflator is not a clean fixed-weight index and its weights move with output composition.
  • The 100 scaling is a convention of the base period chosen by the compiling agency, which differs by country.
Limits of the formula
  • Because weights are current-period output shares, the deflator mixes price change with changes in what the economy produces.
  • It is revised with GDP, sometimes materially, so early estimates of deflator inflation are provisional.

Commonly confused with

Consumer Price Index

CPI prices a largely fixed consumer basket, including imports. The deflator prices all domestic production with weights that change every period. Different question, different answer, both correct. The same point extends further: CPI, the euro area's HICP and the PCE price index are three different measures with different baskets, weights and methods, so one economy yields different inflation numbers depending on which is quoted.
