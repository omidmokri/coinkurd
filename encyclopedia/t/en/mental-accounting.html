Simple definition

Keeping money in separate mental pots. The same amount is treated differently depending on which pot it sits in, even though money itself does not know the difference.

Professional definition

Thaler's account of how people code, categorise and evaluate financial outcomes. Transactions are assigned to accounts, each with its own reference point, and gains and losses are evaluated within the account rather than across total wealth, which makes choices depend on how the books are kept.

Technical definition

Three decisions do the work: how outcomes are grouped into accounts, whether multiple outcomes are evaluated jointly or separately, and how often each account is closed. Segregating a gain from a loss, or closing an account early, changes the value computed under a reference-dependent value function without changing any cash flow. House money, separate pots for separate goals and reluctance to borrow against one pot to fund another all follow from the same structure.

Academic definition

The set of cognitive operations by which individuals code, categorise and evaluate financial activities into distinct accounts, producing choices that depend on the partition rather than on aggregate wealth.

Mechanism

Each account carries its own reference point, so the same outcome is coded as a gain in one book and a loss in another. Because value is curved around that point, the partition changes the computed value: two gains split into separate accounts are valued above their sum, while a loss merged into a larger gain is softened. Closing frequency matters for the same reason.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone running several sub-portfolios, labelling some capital as profit and some as principal, or reporting results per strategy.
Why it matters
It explains why risk taken on recent winnings feels different from risk taken on starting capital, although the exchange charges the same for both.
Limitations
Separating accounts is not always an error. Explicit budgets and strategy-level books are deliberate controls; the bias is in letting the labels, rather than total risk, decide the next position.

Common mistakes

  • Treating recent profit as free money that needs less risk control.
  • Running one position at a reckless size because another is in profit.
  • Reporting per-strategy results without a total-portfolio exposure figure.

Examples

numeric

Two positions: one up 300, one down 200. Net result is plus 100 either way, but closing both on the same day books one outcome, while closing them weeks apart books a win and, separately, a loss.

Given
  • position A closes at +300, position B closes at -200
  • value function concave over gains, convex over losses, steeper over losses
  • two bookkeeping choices: one joint account, or two separate accounts
Steps
  1. joint: a single outcome of +100 is evaluated once, in the gain region
  2. separate: v(+300) is evaluated in the gain region and v(-200) in the loss region
  3. because the loss limb is steeper, the separated pair is valued below the joint +100
identical cash, different experienced value, depending only on how the trades were booked

The cash outcome is unchanged in both cases. Only the coding changes, which is the content of the claim.

everyday

A household keeps a holiday fund untouched while carrying a credit-card balance at a higher rate. Paying the card from the fund is better arithmetic; the two pots are kept separate because the fund has a name.

Commonly confused with

Core-Satellite Portfolio

A core-satellite structure is a deliberate allocation design with a stated total risk budget. Mental accounting is the unplanned version: separate books that are never added up.
