Present value

ENGLISHPresent valuereference

Category
Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

What a future payment is worth today, once you allow for the fact that money arriving later is worth less than money in hand.

Professional definition

The value today of one or more dated future cash flows, each divided by a compounding factor built from the chosen discount rate. Adding present values is legitimate because they are all expressed at the same date.

Technical definition

The sum of each future cash flow divided by (1+r) raised to the number of periods until it arrives. The result is linear in the cash flows but strictly convex in the rate, so long-dated flows dominate the sensitivity. With a term structure, each flow should be discounted at its own maturity's rate rather than at one average rate.

Academic definition

The date-zero equivalent of a stream of dated cash flows under a specified discount rate, obtained by dividing each flow by its corresponding compounding factor and summing.

Mechanism

Dividing by the compounding factor undoes the growth that an equivalent sum invested today would have produced, so the result is the stake you would need now to replicate the future flow. Because the divisor grows geometrically with time, distant cash flows contribute little, which is why valuations hinge on the first years and on the rate.

In practice

Who uses it
Analysts valuing bonds, projects or businesses; lenders sizing instalments; anyone choosing between a lump sum and a schedule.
Why it matters
It puts every option on the same date so the comparison is arithmetic rather than rhetorical.
Limitations
Highly sensitive to the rate and to the terminal assumptions; a small change in either can move a long-horizon valuation materially.

Common mistakes

  • Discounting nominal cash flows with a real rate.
  • Using one rate for every maturity when a curve is available.
  • Treating a precise output as a precise truth.

Examples

everyday

A court award of 50,000 payable in three years is not worth 50,000 today. Discounted at 7%, it is worth about 40,800 now — which is the figure a buyer of that claim would start from.

numeric

Three annual receipts of 1,000 discounted at 8% give 925.93, 857.34 and 793.83. Their present value is 2,577.10, well under the 3,000 headline, and the third year contributes least.

Given
  • CF = 1,000 at t = 1, 2, 3
  • r = 8% per year, flat
  • annual compounding
Steps
  1. 1,000 / 1.08 = 925.93
  2. 1,000 / 1.08^2 = 857.34
  3. 1,000 / 1.08^3 = 793.83
  4. sum
PV = 2,577.10

A flat 8% is assumed for every maturity; with a real curve each flow would take its own rate.

Mathematical foundation

PV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_{t}}{(1+r)^{t}}

Variables

PVPresent value at date zerocurrency
CF_{t}Cash flow arriving at the end of period tcurrency

Procedure

Date every cash flow. Divide each by (1+r) raised to its own period count, using a rate expressed for the same period length. Add the results. If a curve is available, replace the single r with the rate for each maturity.

Worked example

Given
  • CF = 1,000 for t = 1..3
  • r = 8%
Steps
  1. 925.93 + 857.34 + 793.83
PV = 2,577.10

Interpretation

The output is the stake that, invested at r, would reproduce the stream. It is a statement about equivalence at one rate, not a prediction that the flows will arrive.

Assumptions

  • Cash flows are treated as certain; uncertainty is handled either by adjusting the flows to expectations or by raising r, and the two are not interchangeable.
  • A single flat r assumes no term structure. Where a yield curve exists, maturity-specific rates are the correct input.
  • End-of-period timing. Flows received at the start of each period need an extra factor of (1+r).
Limits of the formula
  • Convexity in r means the sensitivity of PV to the rate is itself rate-dependent, so two analysts with slightly different rates can reach very different long-horizon values.
  • Perpetual or terminal-value components often dominate the total, and those are the least verifiable part of the input.

Commonly confused with

Net Present Value

Present value discounts the inflows. Net present value subtracts the initial outlay from that figure, so NPV answers whether to proceed while PV only measures.
