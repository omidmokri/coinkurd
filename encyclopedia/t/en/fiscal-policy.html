Fiscal policy

Simple definition

A government using its budget — what it taxes and what it spends — to influence the economy. It is the other half of macro policy, alongside what the central bank does with rates.

Professional definition

The use of taxation, transfers and public spending to affect demand, income distribution and the supply side. Decided by the legislature and treasury rather than the central bank, implemented through budgets, and financed by taxes or borrowing.

Technical definition

Decomposed into automatic stabilisers, which move with the cycle without any decision, and discretionary measures, which require legislation and arrive with recognition, decision and implementation lags. The sign and size of the effect on output depend on how the measure is financed, how much of a transfer is spent rather than saved, and the monetary stance at the time. Institutional rules and fiscal frameworks differ by country.

Academic definition

The deliberate use of the public budget constraint — taxation, transfers and expenditure — as an instrument of macroeconomic stabilisation and allocation, distinct from monetary policy in both instrument and decision authority.

Mechanism

Spending and transfers add to demand directly; taxes subtract from the income available to spend. The size of the net effect depends on how much of each currency unit is spent again rather than saved or used to repay debt, and on whether the central bank offsets the impulse. Because measures must be legislated, the help often arrives after the need.

In practice

Who uses it
Treasuries and legislatures, sovereign credit analysts, rates desks pricing issuance supply, and macro investors reading the policy mix.
Why it matters
It sets the quantity of government paper to be issued and the path of demand, both of which feed directly into yields, inflation pressure and the policy mix.
Limitations
Lags are long, multipliers are estimated with wide uncertainty, and the same measure can have very different effects depending on the cycle phase and monetary stance.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a headline spending number as its net effect on demand.
  • Ignoring that the central bank may offset the impulse.
  • Reading fiscal rules as binding in all circumstances.

Examples

numeric

A transfer of 100 is paid out. If 60 is spent and the rest saved, the first-round addition to demand is 60, not 100 — and later rounds depend on what each recipient does in turn.

Given
  • transfer = 100
  • assumed share of the transfer spent = 0.60
  • remainder saved or used to repay debt
Steps
  1. first-round demand effect = 0.60 x 100 = 60
  2. further rounds depend on the spending share at each stage
first-round effect = 60, total effect unknown without an estimated multiplier

The spending share is assumed, not measured. Estimated multipliers vary widely across studies, households and cycle phases; this registry contains no estimate, so none is quoted.

market

An unexpected increase in planned borrowing lands on the bond market as extra supply. Yields can rise on the announcement itself, before any of the spending has reached the economy.

Commonly confused with

Interest Rates and the Federal Reserve

Fiscal policy is the budget, decided by the government. Monetary policy is the price and quantity of money, decided by the central bank. They interact, and can pull in opposite directions.

Budget institutions, fiscal rules, the division of authority between treasury and legislature and the tax mix all differ by country and change with each government, so the entry describes mechanisms rather than any national framework, and cites no deficit, spending or tax figure. The size of fiscal multipliers is genuinely uncertain and no estimate is quoted because this registry contains none.
