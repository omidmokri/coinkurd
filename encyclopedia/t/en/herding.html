Simple definition

Buying or selling mainly because others are. The crowd's action is treated as information, and at scale the crowd becomes the thing everyone is reading.

Professional definition

Correlated trading that arises from imitation rather than from independent assessment. A participant who treats others' orders as evidence may act rationally in isolation, yet the aggregate suppresses the private information that prices are supposed to collect.

Technical definition

The hard part is identification. Correlated trading is observable; its cause is not. Participants responding independently to the same public release produce the same footprint as imitation, and a cascade in which later actors rationally discard their own signal produces it too. Empirical work therefore separates intentional herding from spurious herding, and the distinction is not settled from price and volume alone.

Academic definition

Convergent trading behaviour attributable to the observation of others' actions rather than to independent information, distinguished in the literature from spurious correlation arising from common information shocks.

Mechanism

Other people's orders are cheap information and your own analysis is expensive, so copying is often the better trade individually. Each copy removes one independent signal from the price, so the information the price carries thins as participation grows. Narrative, social feeds and visible leaderboards speed the process by making the crowd's position easier to observe than its reasoning.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone trading a crowded position, and anyone studying why liquidity and correlation move together in a stressed market.
Why it matters
It explains why positioning itself becomes a risk: a crowded trade has a crowded exit, and the exit is where the cost shows up.
Limitations
Correlated trading is not proof of imitation, and imitation is not always irrational. Neither the presence nor the size of herding can be read off a price chart.

Common mistakes

  • Reading agreement among commentators as independent confirmation.
  • Assuming a crowded trade must be wrong.
  • Sizing a position as though your exit were the only one.

Examples

crypto

A token trends on social feeds for a weekend. Spot volume and open interest rise together, almost all of it long. The first move against the position meets an order book in which everyone needs the same side.

market

Two desks sell the same asset within minutes. One acted on a published figure, the other on seeing the first sale. The tape shows one wave; only the second desk was herding, and the tape cannot tell them apart.

Commonly confused with

Market Manipulation

Manipulation is a deliberate attempt to create a misleading impression, and it is a legal category. Herding needs no coordination and no intent; it is what a crowd does when each member watches the others.
