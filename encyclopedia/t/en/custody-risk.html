Custody Risk

Simple definition

The risk attached to who actually controls your assets. If someone else holds them for you, you depend on them; if you hold them yourself, you depend on your own key management.

Professional definition

The exposure created by the location and control of assets rather than by their price. Under custodial arrangements the holder has a claim against the custodian and cannot move the asset without it; under self-custody the holder has direct control and carries the full operational burden of keys, backups and signing.

Technical definition

On a public blockchain, control follows the private key that can sign a spending transaction. A custodial balance is a database entry representing a claim; the coins typically sit in the custodian's addresses, often pooled across clients in omnibus wallets, so the client's balance is an obligation of the operator rather than an on-chain position. The practical consequences are: withdrawals require the custodian to act, pooling makes per-client holdings unverifiable from the chain, and in insolvency recovery depends on whether assets were legally segregated under the applicable law.

Academic definition

The risk arising from the arrangements governing control and safekeeping of assets, encompassing the legal character of the holder's claim, the operational security of key management, and the verifiability of client-level balances.

Mechanism

On-chain, the key that can sign is the asset. A custodian holding the keys can therefore pause withdrawals, be hacked, lend the assets out or fail, and the client's balance is an entry in its ledger rather than coins the client can move. Self-custody removes that dependency and replaces it: a lost seed phrase or a signed malicious transaction is an unrecoverable loss with no operator to appeal to.

In practice

Who uses it
Long-term holders deciding where assets sit, operations teams designing signing procedures, and anyone choosing between an exchange balance and a wallet they control.
Why it matters
It separates two risks that are easy to confuse: being wrong about price, and being unable to access an asset you were right about.
Limitations
The exposure is largely unmeasurable from outside: reserve attestations are point-in-time and do not establish solvency, and self-custody risk depends on procedures no third party audits.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping long-term holdings in a trading account for convenience.
  • Reading a reserve attestation as proof of solvency.
  • Moving to self-custody with one unverified backup of the seed phrase.

Examples

crypto

Two holders own the same amount of the same asset. One holds it at an exchange and can trade instantly but cannot move it if withdrawals are suspended. The other holds the keys and can always move it, but a lost backup is a total loss.

numeric

A holder keeps 80 percent of a portfolio in self-custody and 20 percent at a venue for trading. A total failure of that venue costs 20 percent of the portfolio; a lost seed with no working backup costs 80 percent. Both are custody risk, sized differently.

Given
  • portfolio = 100 units of value
  • self-custody share = 80 units
  • venue share = 20 units
  • two independent failure modes considered separately
Steps
  1. venue total failure: loss = 20 units, that is 20 percent
  2. irrecoverable key loss: loss = 80 units, that is 80 percent
  3. concentration index on the custody dimension = 0.80^2 + 0.20^2 = 0.68
  4. effective number of custody arrangements = 1 / 0.68 = 1.47
Two custody arrangements, but an effective diversification of about 1.5.

Splitting across custody arrangements reduces the size of any single failure. It does not reduce the probability of either, and it adds operational complexity of its own.

Commonly confused with

Counterparty Risk

Counterparty risk is about an obligation not being performed. Custody risk is about control of the asset itself. A custodian creates both at once; self-custody removes the counterparty and leaves custody risk entirely with the holder.
