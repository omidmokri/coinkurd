Loss Aversion

Simple definition

Losses of a given size weigh more heavily on a decision than gains of the same size. Losing 100 feels worse than gaining 100 feels good.

Professional definition

A documented property of choice under risk: outcomes are judged as gains or losses against a reference point, and the value function is steeper on the loss side. It is distinct from risk aversion, which concerns the curvature of value in wealth rather than an asymmetry at zero.

Technical definition

In prospect theory, outcomes enter as deviations from a reference point, value is concave over gains and convex over losses, and the loss limb is steeper, which produces the characteristic kink at the reference point. The asymmetry predicts risk-seeking in the loss domain: a sure small loss is often rejected in favour of a gamble with the same or worse expectation. Measured magnitudes of the asymmetry vary by study, task and population.

Academic definition

The empirical regularity that the disutility of a loss exceeds the utility of an equal-sized gain relative to a reference point, formalised in prospect theory as a value function with a steeper negative limb.

Mechanism

Everything follows from the reference point. Because outcomes are coded as deviations from it, the same position can be a gain or a loss depending on where the trader marks the start. The steeper loss limb then makes closing at a loss feel costlier than holding, and the convexity over losses makes an extra gamble feel cheaper than it is.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone making a decision with a stake in it, and especially anyone deciding whether to close a position already underwater.
Why it matters
It explains a specific, repeatable pattern: realising gains early, holding losers, and accepting worse odds to avoid booking a loss.
Limitations
It is a finding about choice, not a trading rule. Measured magnitudes vary by study and context, and holding a losing position can also be correct on its own merits.

Common mistakes

  • Using the entry price as the only reference point.
  • Treating loss aversion and risk aversion as the same thing.
  • Quoting one loss-aversion coefficient as a universal constant.

Examples

everyday

Most people decline a coin flip that pays 100 on heads and loses 100 on tails, even though its expected value is zero. The same flip becomes acceptable only when the winning side pays noticeably more.

numeric

With an illustrative loss-aversion coefficient of 2, a potential loss of 100 is weighted like 200. Indifference on a fifty-fifty bet then needs a gain near 200, not 100.

Given
  • reference point at current wealth
  • loss of 100 and gain of G, each with probability 0.5
  • illustrative loss-aversion coefficient lambda = 2
  • value treated as locally linear so that only the kink matters
Steps
  1. weighted loss = 2 x 100 = 200
  2. indifference requires 0.5 x G = 0.5 x 200
  3. G = 200
a gain of about 200 is needed to offset a possible loss of 100

The coefficient 2 is used only to make the asymmetry visible. The 1979 paper establishes that the loss limb is steeper; it does not fix a universal value for the ratio, and later estimates vary.

Mathematical foundation

v(x) = \begin{cases} u(x) & x \geq 0 \\ -\lambda \, u(-x) & x < 0 \end{cases}, \qquad \lambda > 1

Variables

Procedure

Fix the reference point, express each outcome as a deviation from it, then value gains and losses with the same magnitude function and multiply the loss side by lambda. Compare prospects by their weighted values rather than by expected wealth.

Worked example

Given
  • lambda = 2
  • u treated as the identity locally
  • prospect: gain G with probability 0.5, loss 100 with probability 0.5
Steps
  1. v(-100) = -2 x 100 = -200
  2. 0.5 x v(G) + 0.5 x (-200) = 0
  3. v(G) = 200, so G = 200
indifference at a gain of 200 against a loss of 100

Interpretation

Lambda above one is the whole content of loss aversion: the kink at the reference point, not the curvature away from it. A lambda of one would leave an ordinary risk-averse decision maker with no asymmetry at all.

Assumptions

  • Outcomes are evaluated against a reference point, which the theory treats as given rather than deriving.
  • The 1979 formulation establishes reference dependence, diminishing sensitivity and a steeper loss limb; it does not impose a specific functional form, and the piecewise form above is a generic representation of that shape.
  • Probability weighting is omitted here for clarity; in full prospect theory, probabilities are transformed as well, which changes choices independently of lambda.
Limits of the formula
  • Lambda is not a universal constant: reported estimates differ across studies, elicitation methods and populations, so no single number should be quoted as the coefficient.
  • The function describes choices in framed decision tasks. Carrying it over to a specific market decision is an extrapolation, not a result.

Commonly confused with

Expected Value

Expected value is a property of the bet. Loss aversion is a property of the chooser: the same expected value is accepted or rejected depending on how the outcomes sit around a reference point.
