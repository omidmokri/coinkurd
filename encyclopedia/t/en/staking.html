Simple definition

Locking coins to help run a blockchain and being paid for it. If the validator you back misbehaves or goes offline, part of the locked amount can be taken away.

Professional definition

Committing a network's own token as a bond so that a validator may propose and attest blocks. Rewards come from issuance and fees; misbehaviour is punished by slashing part of the bond. The stake is a security deposit, not a deposit in the banking sense.

Technical definition

An economic-security mechanism in proof-of-stake consensus. Because voting weight is tied to bonded value that the protocol itself can destroy, attacks have a cost denominated in the asset being attacked. The design implies lock-up or exit queues, a penalty schedule for equivocation and liveness failure, and delegation markets where the delegator keeps the price risk while a third party keeps the keys and the operational risk.

Academic definition

The posting of a protocol-native bond that confers block-production rights in proof-of-stake consensus and is subject to protocol-enforced penalties, aligning validator incentives with ledger integrity.

Mechanism

The bond works because it is denominated in the asset the validator could damage. Dishonest behaviour destroys value the validator already owns, which makes honest operation the cheaper strategy. Rewards are the other side of the same arrangement, paid for a service that carries duties — which is why uptime matters and why an exit cannot be immediate without weakening the guarantee.

In practice

Who uses it
Node operators, delegators, staking-service providers, and treasuries deciding what to do with idle protocol tokens.
Why it matters
It pays for securing the network and turns a held token into a position that earns issuance instead of sitting idle.
Limitations
Three distinct risks sit here: a lock-up or exit queue during which the position cannot be sold, slashing for the operator's faults, and price risk on the staked token, which can erase a positive reward.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a staking reward with a bank rate as if the principal were fixed.
  • Staking funds that may be needed before the exit queue clears.
  • Delegating without checking the operator's slashing and uptime history.

Examples

numeric

An illustrative stake of 100 tokens earning a 4% nominal reward becomes 104 tokens after a year. If the token price fell from 10 to 7 over that year, the position is worth 728 against 1,000 at the start — the reward was positive and the outcome was not.

Given
  • Stake = 100 tokens
  • Nominal annual reward = 4%, paid in the same token
  • Token price at start = 10
  • Token price after one year = 7
  • Illustrative figures; not a forecast and not a current rate
Steps
  1. Tokens after one year = 100 x 1.04 = 104
  2. Value at start = 100 x 10 = 1,000
  3. Value after one year = 104 x 7 = 728
  4. Change = 728 / 1,000 - 1 = -27.2%
+4% in tokens, -27.2% in the unit of account

Reward rates are set by protocol issuance and participation, vary over time, and are not comparable with a cash interest rate because the principal is not fixed in value.

crypto

A delegator picks an operator who then goes offline during an upgrade. Rewards stop and penalties accrue for the downtime, even though the delegator did nothing and kept no keys. Delegation moves the work, not the exposure.
