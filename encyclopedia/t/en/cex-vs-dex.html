Centralised Exchange versus Decentralised Exchange

Simple definition

Two ways to trade. A centralised exchange holds your coins and matches orders on its own books. A decentralised exchange trades straight from your wallet against code running on a blockchain.

Professional definition

A CEX is a custodial venue: a deposit becomes a claim on the operator, orders meet in an internal order book, and fiat rails, identity checks and a support desk exist. A DEX is non-custodial: keys stay with the user and settlement happens on-chain, usually against an automated market maker.

Technical definition

The real distinction is custody and matching, not branding. A CEX nets trades off-chain, which allows deep books, low latency and cross-margining while concentrating counterparty, operational and solvency risk in one firm. A DEX pushes matching into public code, removing deposit risk and replacing it with contract risk, transaction-ordering risk and gas cost. Front-ends, governance keys and relayers often stay centralised, so decentralisation is a spectrum rather than a label.

Academic definition

A comparison of two market-organisation models for crypto assets, distinguished by whether custody and order matching are performed by an identified intermediary or by publicly executed protocol code.

Mechanism

A centralised book can match without touching the chain, so it is fast and deep but requires trusting a balance sheet you cannot audit. A pool-based venue must settle every trade on the chain, so it inherits the chain's cost, latency and public ordering, and its guarantees are only as good as its contracts and the keys that can upgrade them.

In practice

Who uses it
Active traders choosing execution venues, treasurers deciding where balances sit, and anyone trading assets not listed on regulated venues.
Why it matters
The choice sets who can freeze or lose your balance, what it costs to trade, and which risks you are actually taking.
Limitations
Neither model removes risk; it relocates it. A CEX replaces contract risk with counterparty risk, and a DEX does the reverse.

Common mistakes

  • Treating an exchange balance as self-custody.
  • Judging a DEX by pool fee alone, ignoring gas and price impact.
  • Assuming a non-custodial venue has no governance keys.

Examples

market

A pair listed on a large centralised venue may quote a spread of a fraction of a basis point, while the same token on-chain trades only against a pool. The screen price is similar; the cost of a large order is not.

numeric

An illustrative 50,000 unit market order. On a CEX at a 0.10% taker fee the explicit cost is 50. On a DEX with a 0.30% pool fee, 0.40% price impact and 4 of gas, it is about 354 — seven times more, before any custody consideration.

Given
  • Order notional = 50,000 units
  • CEX taker fee = 0.10%
  • DEX pool fee = 0.30%
  • DEX price impact = 0.40%
  • Gas = 4 units
  • Illustrative figures, not quotes from any venue
Steps
  1. CEX cost = 50,000 x 0.0010 = 50
  2. DEX pool fee = 50,000 x 0.0030 = 150
  3. DEX price impact = 50,000 x 0.0040 = 200
  4. DEX total = 150 + 200 + 4 = 354
CEX 50 versus DEX 354 on these assumptions

Reverse the price-impact assumption and the ranking reverses. The comparison is sensitive to pool depth and order size, and it prices execution only, not custody or counterparty risk.
