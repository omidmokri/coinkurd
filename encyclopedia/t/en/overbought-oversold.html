Overbought and Oversold

Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Labels for an indicator reading that is unusually high or unusually low. They describe what has just happened; they are not a warning that a reversal is coming.

Professional definition

Conventional threshold zones on a bounded oscillator — classically above 70 and below 30 on RSI, above 80 and below 20 on a stochastic. The label states that recent one-period moves have been strongly one-sided, and nothing beyond that.

Technical definition

A statement about the distribution of recent changes, not about value and not about expected return. In a trend a bounded oscillator saturates: as average loss approaches zero the underlying ratio explodes while the mapped reading compresses into its last few points, so it can sit at an extreme for weeks while price continues. The thresholds are inherited conventions, not quantiles estimated per instrument, so the same number has different rarity on different assets, lookbacks and timeframes.

Academic definition

Conventional threshold regions of a bounded momentum oscillator, interpreted as describing one-sided recent price change rather than mispricing.

Mechanism

A bounded oscillator must compress everything above a certain strength into its top few points, because the scale cannot extend. Once a reading is there, further strength produces almost no further movement, so the indicator stops discriminating exactly when the trend is strongest. That is why an extreme reading persists instead of resolving.

In practice

Who uses it
Screeners sorting a universe by momentum state, mean-reversion traders in genuinely range-bound conditions, and risk managers watching for one-sided positioning.
Why it matters
A bounded scale makes momentum state comparable across instruments, so one threshold can filter a large universe in a single pass.
Limitations
An extreme reading can persist through an entire trend and carries no timing information. The thresholds are conventions, and selling strength because of one is a direct bet against the trend.

Common mistakes

  • Shorting purely because an oscillator printed above 70.
  • Assuming 70 is equally rare on every asset and every lookback.
  • Using the label in a strong trend instead of in a range.

Examples

market

During a strong advance an oscillator can stay above its upper threshold for six weeks while price rises another third. Every reading in that stretch was correctly labelled, and every reversal inferred from the label was wrong.

numeric

With average gain of two percent against average loss of one tenth of a percent, RSI reads about 95. Halving the average loss again lifts it only to about 98: twice the imbalance, three points of movement. That is saturation, in arithmetic.

Given
  • average gain = 2.0%
  • average loss = 0.1% (positive magnitude)
  • then average loss halved to 0.05%
Steps
  1. RS = 2.0 / 0.1 = 20, RSI = 100 - 100/21 = 95.24
  2. RS = 2.0 / 0.05 = 40, RSI = 100 - 100/41 = 97.56
RSI moves from 95.24 to 97.56 while the gain-to-loss ratio doubles

Above roughly 90 the scale has almost no room left, so the reading cannot report further strength. Thresholds set at 70 say little about what happens at 95.

Commonly confused with

Stochastic Oscillator

Each oscillator carries its own conventional thresholds and its own computation: 70 and 30 on RSI, 80 and 20 on the stochastic. The same label therefore means a different measurement on each.
