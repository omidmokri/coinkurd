Disposition Effect

Simple definition

The tendency to sell positions that are up too soon, and to keep positions that are down for too long. Winners are closed; losers are given more time.

Professional definition

A documented empirical regularity in the trading records of individual investors: realised gains are taken at a higher rate than realised losses, relative to the opportunities for each. Shefrin and Statman named it in 1985 and explained it by reference-dependent preferences rather than by any view about future returns.

Technical definition

The effect is measured as a gap between the proportion of gains realised and the proportion of losses realised, each computed against the paper gains and paper losses available on the same days. Because both rates use the available opportunity as the denominator, the measure is not an artefact of a rising market. Reported magnitudes and the strength of the effect vary with the sample, the account type and the treatment of tax-driven selling.

Academic definition

The empirical regularity that investors realise gains at a higher rate than losses relative to available opportunities, commonly attributed to reference-dependent preferences in the sense of prospect theory.

Mechanism

If outcomes are judged against the purchase price, a position above it sits in the gain region, where value is concave and taking the sure thing appeals. A position below it sits in the loss region, where the same curvature favours the gamble over the certain loss. The asymmetry in holding behaviour follows from the shape of the value function, not from a forecast.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone reviewing their own exit behaviour, and anyone reading a track record where average winners are small and average losers large.
Why it matters
It is measurable from your own records, which makes it one of the few biases you can confirm or rule out rather than argue about.
Limitations
The effect describes a population tendency, not an individual verdict, and a gap can have non-behavioural causes such as tax-loss selling or a mean-reverting strategy with asymmetric targets by design.

Common mistakes

  • Reading average win smaller than average loss as proof of the effect without checking opportunities.
  • Treating it as a prediction that losers recover.
  • Fixing it by widening targets rather than by defining exits in advance.

Examples

numeric

An account had 50 chances to sell at a gain and sold 20; it had 50 chances to sell at a loss and sold 5. The two rates differ by 30 percentage points, which is what the measure reports.

Given
  • gain-selling opportunities = 50, gains realised = 20
  • loss-selling opportunities = 50, losses realised = 5
  • opportunity counted as a holding showing a paper gain or loss on a day when any sale occurred
Steps
  1. proportion of gains realised = 20 / 50 = 0.40
  2. proportion of losses realised = 5 / 50 = 0.10
  3. difference = 0.40 - 0.10 = 0.30
a 30 percentage-point gap between the two realisation rates

Illustrative counts, not a measured sample. The exact definition of an opportunity differs across studies, so published magnitudes are not directly comparable.

market

A portfolio holds eight positions. The three in profit are closed within days of turning green; the five underwater are described as long-term holdings. The holding period became a function of the entry price rather than of the plan.

Commonly confused with

Take-Profit

A take-profit order is a plan stated before the trade. The disposition effect is the pattern that appears when the exit is chosen by whether the position is green, with no plan behind it.
