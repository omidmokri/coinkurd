Opportunity cost

ENGLISHOpportunity costreference

Category
Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

What you give up by choosing one thing over the next-best thing. Money sitting idle has a cost even though no bill arrives for it.

Professional definition

The value of the best foregone alternative when a resource is committed. It is the economic basis of required returns: capital is only worth deploying if it beats what the same capital could earn elsewhere at comparable risk.

Technical definition

A comparative, not an accounting, cost. It never appears in financial statements, which record outlays, so decisions based solely on reported costs systematically ignore it. Proper application requires the alternative to be genuinely available and matched in risk and horizon, otherwise the comparison inflates or deflates the cost arbitrarily. Sunk outlays are excluded by construction.

Academic definition

The value of the highest-valued alternative forgone when a scarce resource is allocated to a particular use.

Mechanism

Resources are finite, so every commitment closes other doors. The cost is the value of the best door closed, which is why a project earning 3% is a loss-maker when a comparable-risk alternative pays 7% — even if the accounts show a profit.

In practice

Who uses it
Capital allocation committees, traders choosing between positions, treasury teams holding cash, and anyone setting a hurdle rate.
Why it matters
It is the reason a required return exists at all, and the discipline that stops a portfolio filling up with positions that merely do not lose money.
Limitations
The alternative must be real, available and risk-comparable; invoking an imagined better use can justify almost any decision after the fact.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring the cost of idle cash in a high-rate environment.
  • Comparing a risky project with a risk-free alternative as if equivalent.
  • Letting sunk costs enter the comparison.

Examples

everyday

Keeping 50,000 in a current account for a year when a one-year deposit pays 5% costs 2,500 in foregone interest. No statement shows that cost, but it is as real as a fee.

numeric

A project returns 300,000 on 5,000,000 committed, a 6% return, while a comparable-risk alternative offers 9%. Accounting profit is 300,000; the economic result is 150,000 worse than the alternative.

Given
  • capital committed = 5,000,000
  • project return = 300,000 (6%)
  • alternative of comparable risk = 9%
Steps
  1. alternative return = 5,000,000 x 0.09 = 450,000
  2. shortfall = 450,000 - 300,000
opportunity cost of 150,000 for the year

Valid only if the 9% alternative was genuinely available at the same risk and for the same period.

Commonly confused with

Sunk Cost Fallacy

Opportunity cost looks forward at alternatives still open. The sunk-cost fallacy looks backward at money already spent, which should play no part in the decision.
