Know Your Customer and Anti-Money Laundering

Simple definition

The checks a regulated business must run on who its customers are, and the rules it must follow to spot and report money that may come from crime.

Professional definition

Two linked obligations. Know Your Customer is the identification and verification of a customer and, where relevant, their beneficial owner and source of funds. Anti-money laundering is the wider programme around it: risk assessment, ongoing monitoring, record keeping, suspicious-activity reporting and sanctions screening.

Technical definition

A supervisory framework imposed on obliged entities rather than on assets. Its scope is defined by what a firm does — custody, exchange, transfer, issuance — so the same activity can be in scope in one country and outside it in another, and on-chain settlement does not remove a firm from scope. Obligations are risk-based, meaning the depth of diligence must rise with assessed risk, and enforcement reaches the firm and its officers rather than the technology.

Academic definition

The body of supervisory requirements obliging financial intermediaries to identify customers, assess and monitor financial-crime risk, and report suspicious activity to the competent authority.

Mechanism

The framework works by placing the duty on the intermediary that can see the customer, because that is the only point where a pseudonymous ledger entry can be tied to a person. This is why obligations cluster at on-ramps, off-ramps and custodians, and why a platform may freeze a withdrawal it cannot evidence: the firm bears the penalty, so it manages its own risk first and the customer's convenience second.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchanges, brokers, custodians, payment firms, compliance and onboarding teams, and the customers subject to their checks.
Why it matters
It determines who may open an account, what documents are required, when a transfer can be held, and which activity must be reported.
Limitations
Requirements differ by jurisdiction and change, thresholds and definitions are not uniform, and a firm's internal policy is usually stricter than the statute. Nothing here is legal advice and no figure or threshold is stated.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming one country's rules apply to a platform incorporated elsewhere.
  • Expecting a self-custodial wallet to carry the same obligations as a platform.
  • Treating completed verification as permanent rather than ongoing.

Examples

regulatory

The same business — swapping one asset for another and holding customer balances — can be a licensed activity in one country, a registration in a second, and unregulated in a third. The activity did not change; the obligations did.

numeric

A platform's own tiers, as an illustration only: an unverified account may withdraw up to 1,000 a day and a verified one up to 100,000. A customer needing 5,000 must either verify or split the request across days — the limit is the firm's policy, not a published legal threshold.

Given
  • Unverified daily withdrawal limit = 1,000 (platform policy, illustrative)
  • Verified daily withdrawal limit = 100,000 (platform policy, illustrative)
  • Customer need = 5,000
Steps
  1. Days required while unverified = 5,000 / 1,000 = 5
  2. Days required once verified = 5,000 / 100,000, which is under one day
Five days unverified against the same day verified

These tiers are invented for the example. Statutory thresholds vary by jurisdiction, are revised, and are deliberately not quoted here; check the rules that apply where the firm and the customer are located.
