Purchasing Managers' Index

Simple definition

A survey score built from one question: is business better, the same, or worse than last month? Above 50 means more firms said better than worse.

Professional definition

A diffusion index compiled from monthly questionnaires sent to purchasing and supply managers, covering new orders, output, employment, supplier delivery times and inventories. The 50 line separates expansion from contraction, and composite indexes weight manufacturing and services sub-indexes together.

Technical definition

The score is the share replying 'higher' plus half the share replying 'unchanged', so it measures the breadth of change across firms, not its size. A reading of 55 says more firms improved than worsened; it does not say by how much, and a small number of large firms can expand strongly while the index falls. Panels, weights and seasonal adjustment are the compiler's own and are not standardised across providers or countries.

Academic definition

A qualitative business-survey diffusion index, defined as the proportion of respondents reporting improvement plus half the proportion reporting no change, bounded on the interval [0, 100] with a 50 neutral point.

Mechanism

Because the index counts how many firms improved rather than by how much, it turns qualitative answers into a fast, bounded number available before official statistics. The same construction is its weakness: it saturates near its bounds, cannot register the scale of a shock, and reflects whichever firms sit in the compiler's panel.

In practice

Who uses it
Macro analysts wanting a monthly read before official data, supply-chain managers, and traders using survey releases as scheduled volatility events.
Why it matters
It arrives early, is comparable month to month within one provider, and its sub-indexes separate orders, output and delivery times, which helps locate where a slowdown is forming.
Limitations
It measures breadth, not magnitude; panels are provider-specific; and series from different compilers for the same country can disagree.

Common mistakes

  • Converting a PMI level directly into a GDP growth number.
  • Comparing one provider's index with another's as one series.
  • Reading 49.8 as a recession signal.

Examples

numeric

Of the firms replying, 40 percent report higher activity, 35 percent unchanged and 25 percent lower. The diffusion score is 57.5: more firms improved than worsened, with no information about how much.

Given
  • share replying higher = 0.40
  • share replying unchanged = 0.35
  • share replying lower = 0.25
Steps
  1. D = 0.40 + 0.5 x 0.35
  2. = 0.40 + 0.175 = 0.575
  3. index = 0.575 x 100
PMI = 57.5

Response shares are invented for the arithmetic. The same 57.5 could come from many different distributions of actual output change.

market

A manufacturing survey slips just under 50 while the much larger services survey holds well above it. Headlines report contraction; the composite index, weighted by sector size, still shows expansion.

Mathematical foundation

D = \left( s_{up} + \tfrac{1}{2} s_{same} \right) \times 100

Variables

Procedure

Take the shares of 'higher', 'unchanged' and 'lower' answers to one question, which sum to one. Add the higher share to half the unchanged share and scale by 100. Repeat for each sub-question, then weight the sub-indexes to form a composite if the compiler publishes one.

Worked example

Given
  • s_up = 0.40
  • s_same = 0.35
  • s_down = 0.25
Steps
  1. check: 0.40 + 0.35 + 0.25 = 1.00
  2. D = (0.40 + 0.175) x 100
D = 57.5

Interpretation

Fifty means as many firms improved as worsened. Distance from 50 is breadth of change, not rate of growth, so a level cannot be read as a percentage and cannot be mapped to output without an estimated regression that the index itself does not supply.

Assumptions

  • The three response shares sum to one for the question being scored; partial responses must be excluded or reweighted first.
  • The half-weight on 'unchanged' is the standard diffusion convention, not an estimated parameter.
  • Panel composition, sector weights and seasonal adjustment are chosen by each compiler, so two providers can publish different indexes for the same economy and month.
Limits of the formula
  • Breadth and magnitude are different quantities: an index can rise while aggregate output falls, if many small firms improve slightly and a few large ones contract sharply.
  • Bounded at 0 and 100, so it compresses information in extreme months.
