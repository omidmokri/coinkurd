Trade Journal

Category
Risk Management
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A record of every trade you take, written down at the time: what you bought, why, where the stop was, and how it ended. It exists so your memory cannot edit the result.

Professional definition

A structured log of entries, exits, position sizes, stop and target levels, the rule that triggered the trade and the state of the account at the time. Its purpose is measurement: without it, win rate, expectancy and maximum adverse excursion cannot be computed from anything but recollection.

Technical definition

At minimum each row holds instrument, direction, timestamps, size, entry and exit prices, initial stop, realised costs, and the rule version in force. That last field is what makes the record analysable: results from different rule versions must not be pooled. Exchange statements supply the fills; the journal supplies the intent, which statements never contain.

Academic definition

A primary record of trading decisions and outcomes, maintained contemporaneously to permit estimation of performance statistics and post-hoc evaluation of decision quality independent of recall.

Mechanism

Memory of trading is systematically biased: losses are reinterpreted, wins are attributed to skill, and trades taken outside the rules disappear from the account the trader gives themselves. A contemporaneous record removes that editing, and it is the only way to turn a vague sense of doing well into a statistic a sizing formula can use.

In practice

Who uses it
Discretionary traders of every level, systematic developers logging live divergence from the backtest, and prop or fund risk desks reviewing a trader's book.
Why it matters
It is the data source for every other risk statistic, and it separates a rule that lost money from a trader who broke the rule.
Limitations
A journal measures what was recorded, not what was true: self-reported reasons are unreliable, and selective recording quietly removes the worst trades.

Common mistakes

  • Writing it up after the outcome is known.
  • Logging price and size but never the rule and the reason.
  • Pooling trades from different rule versions into one statistic.

Examples

everyday

A trader is sure they only trade their plan. Ninety logged trades show thirty-one entries with no rule recorded, and those thirty-one account for most of the quarter's losses. The belief was not wrong about the plan, only about adherence.

numeric

From 90 logged trades: 36 winners averaging 420, 54 losers averaging 180, all after costs. Win rate 0.40, expectancy 60 per trade. None of those three figures exists without the record.

Given
  • 90 trades
  • 36 winners, average 420 after costs
  • 54 losers, average 180 after costs
Steps
  1. win rate = 36 / 90 = 0.40
  2. loss rate = 0.60
  3. 0.40 x 420 = 168
  4. 0.60 x 180 = 108
  5. expectancy = 168 - 108
Expectancy = 60 per trade over 90 recorded trades.

If the unlogged trades were systematically the worst ones, this figure overstates the real result. Completeness of the record is the binding constraint.

Commonly confused with

Backtesting

A backtest simulates a rule on past data that nobody traded. A journal records what was actually executed, at real fills and real costs, including the trades that broke the rule. The gap between the two is the information neither produces alone.
