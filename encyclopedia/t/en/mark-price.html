Mark Price

ENGLISHMark Pricereference

Category
Trading Fundamentals
Level
Professional

Simple definition

The price a derivatives venue uses to value your position and to decide liquidation. It normally comes from an index of other markets, not from the venue's own last trade.

Professional definition

A venue-computed reference price for unrealised profit and loss, margin ratio and liquidation. It is normally built from an external index, sometimes with a funding-basis adjustment, precisely so that one large trade on that venue cannot trigger liquidations.

Technical definition

A fair-value estimate maintained by the venue and used as the input to maintenance-margin tests. Constructions differ: the index price alone, the index plus a decayed funding basis, or a median of index, last and impact bid and ask. Because it is not a traded price, mark and last routinely differ, and a position can be closed on a mark that no public trade ever printed. The formula and its update frequency are venue rules and should be read before choosing leverage.

Academic definition

An administratively determined reference valuation used for margining and forced closure, constructed to be robust to transient dislocation and to manipulation of the venue's own order book.

Mechanism

If liquidations were assessed on the venue's own last trade, anyone with enough capital could push a thin book through a cluster of liquidation levels and collect the resulting forced flow. Anchoring the mark to an index of independent venues makes that attack cost as much as moving the whole market.

In practice

Who uses it
Perpetual and futures traders, venue risk teams, auditors of a liquidation event, and anyone disputing one.
Why it matters
It decides unrealised profit and loss, the margin ratio and the exact level at which the venue closes you. It, not the chart, is the price your risk runs on.
Limitations
Formulas differ by venue and are revised. If the index feed breaks or its constituents dislocate together, the mark can be wrong in either direction.

Common mistakes

  • Setting leverage from chart distance instead of mark-price distance.
  • Assuming a wick on the venue chart is what liquidated you.
  • Never checking which index the mark is built from.

Examples

market

A trader disputes a liquidation because the chart never reached their liquidation level. The chart plotted last price; the margin test ran on mark price, which is derived from the index and had already fallen further.

numeric

Long one unit at 100 with 10 margin and a 0.5% maintenance requirement liquidates at a mark of 90.45. A last-price wick to 90.10 does not liquidate it if the mark only reached 90.60.

Given
  • long 1 unit, entry 100.00
  • posted margin 10.00 (nominally 10x)
  • maintenance requirement 0.5% of notional, computed on mark M
  • fees and funding excluded
Steps
  1. equity(M) = 10 + (M - 100)
  2. liquidation when equity <= 0.005 x M
  3. 10 + M - 100 <= 0.005M
  4. 0.995M <= 90
  5. M <= 90.4523
liquidation mark about 90.45, roughly 9.55% below entry

Simplified: it ignores fees, funding and tiered maintenance rates, each of which moves the level. Every venue publishes its own formula, so recompute on the venue you actually trade.

Commonly confused with

Index Price

The index price is the external composite. The mark price is what the venue actually applies, which may be the index, the index plus a basis adjustment, or a median including venue data.

No single mark-price formula is standard: the three constructions named in the technical definition all exist in production, so no math block is given. The worked liquidation level is simplified and omits fees, funding and tiered maintenance rates. Persian and Kurdish keep the English word, which is what practitioners say.
