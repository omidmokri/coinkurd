Transaction Cost Analysis

Simple definition

Measuring what trading actually cost — fees, spread, price impact and the part of the move you missed — instead of assuming the cost was negligible.

Professional definition

The post-trade discipline of decomposing the gap between a decision price and the realised outcome into explicit costs, meaning fees and taxes, and implicit costs, meaning spread, impact, delay and the opportunity cost of quantity never filled, reported in basis points against a stated benchmark.

Technical definition

TCA is only meaningful once the benchmark is fixed in advance: arrival price, interval VWAP, TWAP and previous close each answer a different question, and the same fills can look good against one and poor against another. The hardest term is opportunity cost, because the counterfactual of the unexecuted remainder is unobservable and must be modelled. For a retail account the same arithmetic decides survival: fees plus spread per round trip, set against expectancy per trade, is what tells you whether a positive-edge rule nets anything.

Academic definition

The systematic measurement and attribution of explicit and implicit trading costs relative to a pre-specified benchmark, used to evaluate execution performance separately from investment decisions.

Mechanism

Costs are paid in small amounts per trade and compound across a trade count, so they are invisible per order and decisive per year. Separating explicit from implicit matters because only the explicit part reaches a statement: spread, impact and delay never appear as line items, which is why a strategy can look profitable in the ledger of fills and still lose against the decision price.

In practice

Who uses it
Execution desks and the compliance functions reviewing them, fund managers paying brokers, and systematic traders deciding whether a signal survives its own cost.
Why it matters
It separates two different questions that are easily blamed on each other: was this the right trade, and was it executed badly?
Limitations
Opportunity cost is modelled, not measured, and benchmark choice can move the answer by more than the costs being measured.

Common mistakes

  • Picking the benchmark that flatters the fills already achieved.
  • Reporting fees only and calling the result total cost.
  • Comparing cost in bps across pairs with very different spreads.

Examples

numeric

A buy benchmarked at 100.00 fills at 100.12 and pays five basis points in fees. Total cost is 17 basis points: twelve implicit, five explicit. Only the five appear on the statement.

Given
  • side = buy
  • benchmark price = 100.00
  • average fill = 100.12
  • explicit fees = 5 bps
  • order fully filled, so no opportunity cost term
Steps
  1. implicit = (100.12 - 100.00) / 100.00 x 10000 = 12 bps
  2. explicit = 5 bps
  3. total = 12 + 5
total cost = 17 bps

A rule whose average gross edge is 15 bps per round trip is a losing rule at this cost level, even with a high win rate.

institutional

A quarterly review shows one broker consistently beating interval VWAP but losing against arrival price. The pattern suggests orders were worked slowly in drifting markets — good against the chosen benchmark, expensive for the fund.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Declare the benchmark and the timestamp that defines it before the order is sent. After completion, compute the quantity-weighted average execution price, take the signed deviation from the benchmark in basis points, add explicit fees, and report any unfilled quantity separately with the modelled price move over the period it was not held.

Worked example

Given
  • buy
  • benchmark 100.00
  • average fill 100.12
  • fees 5 bps
  • fully filled
Steps
  1. (100.12 - 100.00) / 100.00 = 0.0012
  2. 0.0012 x 10000 = 12 bps implicit
  3. 12 + 5
17 bps total cost

Interpretation

The figure is the all-in cost of turning a decision into a position, expressed so it can be compared with the strategy's expected gross edge per trade. If cost exceeds edge, the rule is not tradeable at that size.

Assumptions

  • The benchmark is declared in advance. Arrival price, interval VWAP, TWAP and previous close are all in industry use and are not comparable with each other.
  • Opportunity cost of unfilled quantity requires a counterfactual and is reported as a separate, explicitly modelled term.
  • Basis points are measured against the benchmark price, so cost in bps is not comparable across instruments with very different spreads unless that is stated.
Limits of the formula
  • Per-order figures are dominated by noise; attribution needs a large sample of comparable orders.
  • The decomposition into delay, impact and timing depends on which intermediate timestamps a venue actually provides.

Commonly confused with

Execution Quality

Execution quality is the property being judged — how well a given order was handled. TCA is the measurement framework that produces the number, including the choice of benchmark and the decomposition of costs.
