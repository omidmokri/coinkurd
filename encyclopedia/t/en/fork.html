Simple definition

Two different things share this name: a temporary split when two valid blocks appear at once, and a change to the rules that may split the network.

Professional definition

In consensus, a divergence of history where competing branches extend different blocks. In governance, a change to validation rules, classified as soft or hard by whether non-upgraded nodes still accept new blocks. Both senses are in daily use and should be kept apart.

Technical definition

Accidental forks are routine and self-resolving: the chain-selection rule picks one branch and the loser's blocks become orphans, returning their transactions to pending. Rule forks are deliberate, and whether one splits the chain depends on compatibility rather than intent — if non-upgraded nodes reject blocks the new rules permit, two chains can persist, each with users, balances and prices. Deep reorganisations are the dangerous case, which is why recipients wait for depth.

Academic definition

A divergence in a ledger's accepted history, arising either from concurrent valid block production or from a change in validation rules that partitions agreement among nodes.

Mechanism

Forks exist because agreement is eventual rather than instant. Propagation delay lets two valid blocks coexist, and the selection rule resolves the tie. A rule fork is the same phenomenon driven by software instead of latency: nodes disagree about what is valid, so they disagree about which chain exists.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchange and custody operations, node operators, participants in protocol governance, and analysts tracking chain splits.
Why it matters
It decides whether an upgrade is a coordination problem or an asset-creation event, and how long deposits and withdrawals must be paused.
Limitations
Which branch gets a ticker, a price or a name are market and exchange decisions, not protocol facts, and they can change after the split.

Common mistakes

  • Treating every fork as the creation of a new coin.
  • Assuming an upgrade cannot split a chain because its developers agree.
  • Processing deposits during a contested fork without checking replay protection.

Examples

crypto

Two miners publish valid blocks at nearly the same height. Half the network sees one first, half the other. The next block decides, and the losing block's transactions go back to the mempool.

numeric

Your node holds a branch of two blocks when a branch of three arrives at equal difficulty. The selection rule switches, orphaning two blocks, so a transaction credited at two confirmations becomes unconfirmed again.

Given
  • branch A = 2 blocks
  • branch B = 3 blocks
  • equal difficulty per block
Steps
  1. cumulative work A = 2 units, B = 3 units
  2. selection rule prefers the greater cumulative work: B
a 2-block reorganisation; A's transactions return to pending

Chains compare cumulative work or attested weight, not block count. Counting blocks is only valid while difficulty is equal across branches.

Commonly confused with

Finality

A fork is a disagreement about history. Finality is the point at which the protocol's rules make reversing that history infeasible or punishable.
