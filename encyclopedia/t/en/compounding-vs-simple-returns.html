Compounding versus Simple Returns

Simple definition

Simple returns are added up; compound returns are multiplied together. Over several periods the two give different answers, and only the compound one matches the money in the account.

Professional definition

A simple return measures one period in isolation. Compounding chains gross returns multiplicatively, so the realised multi-period figure is the product of one plus each return, minus one. The arithmetic mean of single-period returns always overstates realised growth whenever returns vary.

Technical definition

Over n periods the compound total is the product of gross returns minus one, and the geometric mean is that product to the power one over n, minus one. The arithmetic mean exceeds the geometric mean for any series that is not constant, by Jensen's inequality, and the gap widens with dispersion — the effect usually called volatility drag. The common approximation that the gap equals half the variance is an approximation only, exact for no finite series.

Academic definition

The distinction between additive, single-period rate measurement and multiplicative, multi-period wealth accumulation, formalised by the inequality between the arithmetic and geometric means of a return series.

Mechanism

Losses and gains are not symmetric in multiplicative arithmetic: a 50% fall needs a 100% rise to undo. Averaging the rates hides that asymmetry because addition treats the two as equal and opposite. The geometric mean restores it by working on the products the account actually experiences, so it is the only mean that reproduces terminal wealth.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone reporting a track record, comparing funds, projecting growth, or reading a backtest's headline return figure.
Why it matters
The geometric figure is what an investor actually received. The arithmetic figure answers a different question and is the one that flatters a volatile strategy.
Limitations
The geometric mean hides the path, so two strategies with identical compound growth can have very different drawdowns and very different survivability.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging monthly percentages and calling the result annual return.
  • Comparing one fund's arithmetic mean with another's geometric mean.
  • Assuming the arithmetic-geometric gap is exactly half the variance.

Examples

numeric

Up 50% then down 50%. The arithmetic mean is 0% per period; the geometric mean is -13.3975%. The account is at 0.75 of its start, a 25% loss, and the 13.40-point gap between the two means is volatility drag.

Given
  • r1 = +50%
  • r2 = -50%
  • n = 2 periods
Steps
  1. terminal multiple = 1.50 * 0.50 = 0.75
  2. compound total return = 0.75 - 1 = -25%
  3. arithmetic mean = (0.50 - 0.50) / 2 = 0%
  4. geometric mean = 0.75^(1/2) - 1 = 0.866025 - 1 = -13.3975%
  5. gap = 0% - (-13.3975%) = 13.3975 percentage points
arithmetic 0% per period, geometric -13.3975% per period, realised total -25%

The popular shortcut 'gap equals half the variance' would predict 12.5 points here against the exact 13.3975. The shortcut is an approximation, not an identity.

everyday

Ten percent a period for three periods. Simple arithmetic gives 30%. Compounding gives 1.1 cubed minus one, or 33.1%. The 3.1-point difference is interest earned on earlier interest, and it grows superlinearly with the number of periods.

Given
  • r = +10% per period
  • n = 3 periods
  • no withdrawals or additions
Steps
  1. simple: 3 * 10% = 30%
  2. compound: 1.10^3 = 1.331
  3. compound total = 1.331 - 1 = 33.1%
  4. difference = 33.1% - 30% = 3.1 percentage points
simple 30%, compound 33.1%

With constant returns the arithmetic and geometric means coincide at 10%; the gap between them only opens when returns vary.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Add one to each period return to get gross returns. Multiply them all together for the realised total, then subtract one. For a per-period figure, take the n-th root of that product and subtract one. Compute the arithmetic mean separately and report both, because the difference is itself information about dispersion.

Worked example

Given
  • r1 = +50%, r2 = -50%
  • and separately r = +10% for n = 3
Steps
  1. case A: (1.50)(0.50) = 0.75, so compound total = -25%
  2. case A geometric mean = 0.75^0.5 - 1 = -13.3975%; arithmetic mean = 0%
  3. case B: (1.10)^3 = 1.331, so compound total = 33.1%
  4. case B simple sum = 3 * 10% = 30%
case A: -25% realised against a 0% arithmetic mean; case B: 33.1% compound against 30% simple

Interpretation

The compound figure answers what happened to the capital. The arithmetic mean answers what the typical single period looked like. Quoting the second as though it were the first overstates growth by exactly the drag the dispersion creates.

Assumptions

  • Returns are measured on the same base with no cash flows in or out; a contribution or withdrawal makes a money-weighted measure the correct one instead.
  • Period returns are simple, not logarithmic. Log returns are already additive, so the product becomes a sum.
  • Annualising a geometric mean requires the period count to be exact; mixing calendar conventions silently changes the result.
Limits of the formula
  • The geometric mean says nothing about the order of returns or the depth of drawdown along the way.
  • With a total loss in any period the product is zero and the geometric mean is minus 100%, regardless of every other period.
  • The half-variance approximation to the arithmetic-geometric gap is accurate only for small, nearly normal returns.

Commonly confused with

Logarithmic Returns

Log returns are additive across time precisely because compounding is multiplicative. The log scale is the tool; compounding is the phenomenon it linearises.

Commonly confused with

Dollar-Weighted Return

A geometric mean assumes a single undisturbed pot of capital. Once money is added or withdrawn, the dollar-weighted measure answers the investor's question instead.
