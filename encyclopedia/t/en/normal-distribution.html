Normal Distribution

Simple definition

The bell-shaped curve where most values sit near the average and extremes are rare. Easy to work with, assumed everywhere, and a poor description of market returns.

Professional definition

The two-parameter continuous law fixed entirely by its mean and standard deviation. About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of its mass lies within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean, and its tails thin out faster than any power of the distance from the centre.

Technical definition

A continuous law with density proportional to exp(-(x-mu)^2 / 2 sigma^2), zero skewness and kurtosis exactly 3. Sums of many independent finite-variance terms converge to it, which is why it underpins so much finance theory. Daily asset returns violate it where it matters: excess kurtosis and negative skew make large losses far more frequent than the fitted curve permits.

Academic definition

The two-parameter continuous law with Gaussian density, closed under linear combination of independent terms and the limit law of the classical central limit theorem.

Mechanism

The exponential of a negative square decays extremely fast. Four standard deviations out, the density is already tiny; six out it is effectively nil. That decay is the model's convenience and its failure: it hard-codes the assumption that very large moves essentially cannot happen, so any risk number built on it understates them.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone using a standard-deviation-based risk figure: parametric value at risk, Sharpe-style ratios, option models and most off-the-shelf portfolio tools.
Why it matters
Two parameters give closed-form answers for quantiles, portfolio sums and option prices, which no heavy-tailed alternative matches for tractability.
Limitations
The assumption is wrong in the tail, and the tail is where accounts are lost. It also assumes constant volatility, which volatility clustering directly contradicts.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a 6-sigma day impossible rather than evidence against the model.
  • Reporting parametric value at risk without a fat-tailed cross-check.
  • Treating a normality test that passes on a short sample as settled.

Examples

market

Take a series with 1% daily standard deviation. Under normality, moves beyond 1% happen on about a third of days, beyond 2% on about one day in twenty-two, and beyond 3% on about one day in three hundred and seventy.

numeric

How often should a 6-standard-deviation daily move happen if returns were normal? About once in 507 million trading days — roughly one day in two million trading years. Financial series produce such days far more often, which is evidence against normality, not against arithmetic.

Given
  • returns assumed normal with constant mean and standard deviation
  • threshold = 6 standard deviations in either direction
  • 252 trading days per year
Steps
  1. Pr(|Z| >= 6) = erfc(6 / sqrt(2)) = 1.9732e-9
  2. 1 / 1.9732e-9 = 5.068e8 trading days between such events
  3. 5.068e8 / 252 = 2.01e6 years
about 1 day in 5.07 x 10^8 trading days, or once in roughly 2.0 million trading years

Verified numerically. The point of the figure is its absurdity: observed 6-sigma days are common enough that the normal model, not the observation, is what has to be revised.

Procedure

Estimate the mean and standard deviation of the series. Convert any value to a standardised score z = (x - mean) / standard deviation. Probabilities of ranges then come from the standard normal: about 68.3% of mass inside one standard deviation, 95.4% inside two, 99.7% inside three. Before using any of it, test whether the series is in fact normal, and inspect the tails separately rather than trusting an overall fit.

Worked example

Given
  • returns assumed normal
  • threshold 6 standard deviations, two-sided
  • 252 trading days per year
Steps
  1. Pr(|Z| >= 1) = 0.3173, Pr(|Z| >= 2) = 0.0455, Pr(|Z| >= 3) = 0.0027
  2. Pr(|Z| >= 6) = 1.9732e-9
  3. waiting time = 1 / 1.9732e-9 = 5.068e8 trading days
  4. 5.068e8 / 252 = 2.011e6 years
Inside 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations: 68.27%, 95.45%, 99.73%. A 6-sigma day: once in about 2.0 million trading years.

Interpretation

The 68/95/99.7 figures are exact properties of the curve, not of any market. Used as a yardstick they are useful: when observed frequencies beyond three standard deviations exceed the curve's prediction by orders of magnitude, you have measured the size of the model error.

Assumptions

  • Both parameters are constant over the sample; a time-varying standard deviation already breaks the model even if each sub-period looks normal.
  • Draws are independent and identically distributed, which volatility clustering in financial returns contradicts.
  • The coverage percentages are exact for the distribution, not for any finite sample drawn from it.
Limits of the formula
  • Tails decay far too fast for asset returns, so quantile-based risk figures built on it understate large losses systematically.
  • Skewness is forced to zero and kurtosis to 3 by construction, so the model cannot represent asymmetric or heavy-tailed return series at all.

Commonly confused with

Fat Tails

Normality is the model; fat tails are the observed departure from it. The two are not competing descriptions of the same data — one is what is assumed, the other what is measured.
