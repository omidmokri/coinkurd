Fifty-One Percent Attack

Simple definition

An attack in which one party controls most of a chain's block-production power and uses it to reorder or block transactions.

Professional definition

Control of a majority of a proof-of-work chain's hashrate, or of the equivalent weight under proof of stake, lets an attacker reorganise recent history, double-spend their own coins and censor transactions. It does not let them forge signatures, spend other people's coins, or mint outside the issuance rules.

Technical definition

Majority control breaks the economic premise of chain selection: the attacker can always out-produce the honest branch, so the probability of a successful reorganisation stops decaying with depth. Validation is untouched — full nodes still reject invalid blocks, so balances without keys cannot move, the issuance schedule holds and old rules stand. The damage is confined to ordering, inclusion and reversal of recent history, which is precisely what payment security depends on.

Academic definition

An adversarial condition in chain-selection consensus in which the attacker commands a majority of the resource the selection rule measures, removing the depth-based decay of reversal probability.

Mechanism

Chain selection measures one resource and nothing else. If a single party owns most of it, the rule that protects users — that the honest branch grows fastest — simply inverts. Proof of stake changes the economics rather than the logic: the same weight buys the same reordering power, but it can be destroyed by slashing, which makes repeating the attack expensive.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchanges listing smaller chains, custody risk teams, bridge operators, miners assessing rental markets, and chain researchers.
Why it matters
It sets the real security budget of a chain and therefore how many confirmations a given transaction value deserves.
Limitations
Production power can be rented, so a chain's defence is the cost of attacking it relative to the value it settles — not its age, its brand or its community size.

Common mistakes

  • Believing an attacker can take coins from addresses whose keys they lack.
  • Reusing a large chain's confirmation policy on a small chain.
  • Calling any deep reorganisation a majority attack without evidence.

Examples

numeric

At a thirty percent share, the catch-up bound from six blocks behind is about 0.6 percent. At fifty-one percent the ratio exceeds one, so depth no longer protects at all and waiting longer stops helping.

Given
  • z = 6 blocks behind
  • case A: q = 0.30, p = 0.70
  • case B: q = 0.51, p = 0.49
Steps
  1. A: (0.30/0.70)^6 = 0.0062
  2. B: 0.51/0.49 = 1.041, and 1.041^6 > 1
A: about 0.62 percent; B: the bound is no longer below one

Uses the catch-up bound from the entry on finality. Above a majority share the expression is not a probability bound at all: success becomes a question of time, not luck.

crypto

An exchange on a low-hashrate chain raises required confirmations and caps deposit size, because renting a majority of that chain's power for an hour may cost less than the deposits it could reverse.

Commonly confused with

Byzantine Fault Tolerance

A BFT protocol tolerates f faults out of n members with n at least 3f plus 1 — a one-third bound on voting membership. The fifty-one percent figure is a majority bound on production power in an open set. They come from different models and neither threshold transfers to the other; treating a third as the safety line for a mining chain, or a majority as the line for a voting protocol, is the standard error.
