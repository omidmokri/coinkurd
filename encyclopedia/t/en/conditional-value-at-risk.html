Conditional Value at Risk

Simple definition

The average size of your losses in the worst cases, rather than just the line those worst cases start at. It tells you how bad a bad day is, not only how often one happens.

Professional definition

The expected loss conditional on the loss exceeding the value-at-risk quantile at a stated confidence level and horizon. It answers precisely the question value at risk cannot: given that the threshold is breached, how large is the loss on average.

Technical definition

Defined as the conditional expectation of loss in the tail beyond the quantile, and estimated empirically as the mean of the worst one-minus-alpha fraction of observed losses. Unlike value at risk it is sub-additive and therefore coherent, so it aggregates across desks without penalising diversification, which is why it has replaced value at risk in several regulatory and internal frameworks. Under a normal distribution it equals the volatility times the standard normal density at the quantile, divided by the tail probability.

Academic definition

The conditional expectation of loss given exceedance of the alpha-quantile of the loss distribution; a coherent risk measure satisfying monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and sub-additivity.

Mechanism

A quantile reports where the tail begins and discards everything past it, so two portfolios with the same value at risk can have entirely different losses beyond the threshold. Taking the average of that region puts the shape of the tail back into the number. Sub-additivity follows: the measure of a combined book never exceeds the sum of its parts, so diversification can only help it.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk functions reporting tail exposure, portfolio managers optimising against a tail constraint, and anyone whose value-at-risk figure has been breached and wants to know by how much.
Why it matters
It is sensitive to the shape of the tail and it aggregates coherently, so a firm-wide figure is the sum of parts at worst rather than an arbitrary number.
Limitations
It depends on the least reliable part of the data. Estimating a mean from the worst few observations has a large standard error, and no estimate can describe a loss larger than anything in the sample.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting it without the confidence level and horizon.
  • Estimating it from a sample with too few tail observations.
  • Reading it as a maximum loss rather than a conditional average.

Examples

numeric

From 1,000 daily losses, the 99 percent value at risk is the tenth-worst, at 5.2 percent. The mean of those ten worst losses is 7.8 percent. Same data, same confidence level: the threshold is 5.2 and the conditional average beyond it is 7.8.

Given
  • n = 1,000 daily observations
  • alpha = 0.99, so the tail is the worst 1 percent, that is 10 observations
  • tenth-worst loss = 5.2 percent
  • mean of the worst 10 = 7.8 percent
Steps
  1. number of tail observations = (1 - 0.99) x 1,000 = 10
  2. VaR at 99 percent = the tenth-worst loss = 5.2 percent
  3. CVaR at 99 percent = mean of those 10 = 7.8 percent
VaR 5.2 percent, CVaR 7.8 percent, both at 99 percent over one day.

The estimate rests on ten numbers. Its standard error is large, and one new extreme observation moves it materially.

market

Two books report the same one-day 99 percent value at risk. One holds liquid index exposure, the other sells far out-of-the-money options. Their conditional averages beyond the threshold differ by a multiple, and only the second measure shows it.

Procedure

State the horizon and the confidence level first. Sort the sample of losses from worst to best. Count the tail as one minus alpha times the sample size, rounded up. The worst value in that set is the value at risk; the mean of the whole set is the conditional value at risk. Report both, with the sample size and the number of tail observations the estimate rests on.

Worked example

Given
  • n = 1,000 daily loss observations
  • alpha = 0.99
  • sorted worst 10 losses average 7.8 percent
  • the tenth-worst is 5.2 percent
Steps
  1. m = (1 - 0.99) x 1,000 = 10
  2. VaR = L_(10) = 5.2 percent
  3. CVaR = mean of L_(1) to L_(10) = 7.8 percent
  4. normal-distribution check: at alpha = 0.99 the quantile is 2.326 sigma while CVaR is 2.667 sigma, a ratio of about 1.15
CVaR = 7.8 percent, which is 1.5 times the VaR of 5.2 percent.

Interpretation

The figure is the average loss on the bad days, not the worst possible loss. A ratio to value at risk well above the normal-distribution benchmark of roughly 1.15 at 99 percent indicates a fat tail: the losses beyond the threshold are more extreme than a normal model would produce.

Assumptions

  • The horizon and confidence level are stated with the figure. Changing either changes the number and the two are not comparable.
  • Observations are drawn from a stable loss distribution. Volatility clustering and regime change break this, as they do for value at risk.
  • The empirical estimator uses enough tail observations to form a mean. At 99 percent, a 250-day sample leaves two or three observations, which is not an estimate.
  • The normal closed form, volatility times the standard normal density at the quantile divided by one minus alpha, applies only under normality and understates a fat-tailed loss distribution.
Limits of the formula
  • It is bounded above by the worst observation in a historical sample, so it can never report a loss larger than anything that has already happened.
  • Its estimation error is larger than that of value at risk at the same confidence level, because it uses fewer effective data points.
  • Being coherent makes it well behaved for aggregation, not accurate. A coherent measure of a badly estimated distribution is still a bad number.

Commonly confused with

Value at Risk

Value at risk is the threshold: the loss that is exceeded with a stated probability. Conditional value at risk is the average loss once it is exceeded. The first says how often, the second how much, and only the second is sub-additive.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The definition and the coherence property are mathematically settled. The normal-distribution ratio quoted in the interpretation, about 1.15 at 99 percent confidence, is computed from the standard normal density and holds only under normality. Naming is inconsistent in practice: conditional value at risk, expected shortfall and average value at risk coincide for continuous distributions but are defined slightly differently in some texts for discrete ones, so the estimator used should always be stated.
