Insurance Fund

Simple definition

A pot of money a venue keeps to cover positions that were closed too late to pay their own losses. When it empties, the shortfall has to come from other traders.

Professional definition

A venue-held buffer that absorbs the gap when a liquidation is filled worse than the position's bankruptcy price. It is funded by liquidations closed better than bankruptcy, which leave a surplus, and it exists so that a winner's profit does not have to be clawed back when a loser's collateral proves insufficient.

Technical definition

The second layer of a venue's loss waterfall. Equity reaches zero at the bankruptcy price; liquidation is triggered earlier, at the maintenance threshold, precisely to leave room to exit. Fills better than bankruptcy pay surplus into the fund, fills worse draw from it. If the fund cannot cover a shortfall the venue falls back on socialised mechanisms such as auto-deleveraging or clawback. Size, funding policy and disclosure are venue-specific and a published balance is a historical figure, not a guarantee.

Academic definition

A venue-maintained reserve absorbing the residual deficit arising when forced closures execute beyond the price at which a position's collateral is exhausted, interposed before loss-mutualisation mechanisms.

Mechanism

A derivatives venue stakes no capital of its own in a bilateral bet: every winner is paid out of a loser's collateral. When a gap leaves that collateral short, someone must absorb the difference. Charging it to the fund rather than to winning accounts keeps profits payable, and the fund refills from the ordinary case where closure leaves collateral over.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk teams assessing venue solvency, traders choosing where to carry leverage, and anyone comparing venues on more than fee schedules.
Why it matters
It is the main thing standing between a disorderly liquidation cascade and other traders' realised profits being touched.
Limitations
A published balance is historical and unaudited on many venues, says nothing about the size of the next gap, and a fund that grows steadily may simply mean liquidations are being triggered early and profitably for the venue.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a large fund as a guarantee against socialised losses.
  • Ignoring the venue's clawback and auto-deleveraging rules because the fund looks healthy.
  • Comparing fund sizes between venues without comparing open interest and leverage.

Examples

crypto

A fast move clears the book through a cluster of liquidation levels. Most positions are closed with collateral left over, which tops the fund up. A handful fill below bankruptcy, and the fund covers the difference so the traders on the other side are paid in full.

numeric

A one-unit long at 50,000 on 10% initial margin is bankrupt at 45,000. Liquidation triggers near 45,226 but fills at 44,800 in a gap, leaving a 200 shortfall that the fund absorbs. Had it filled at 45,100, the fund would have gained 100.

Given
  • Long 1 unit at P_0 = 50,000
  • Initial margin rate m_i = 10%, so collateral = 5,000
  • Maintenance rate m_m = 0.5%
  • Fill price in the gap = 44,800
Steps
  1. Bankruptcy price: equity zero at P_bank = P_0 (1 - m_i) = 45,000
  2. Liquidation trigger: P* = 50,000 x 0.90 / 0.995 = 45,226
  3. Shortfall = 1 x (45,000 - 44,800) = 200, drawn from the fund
  4. Counterfactual fill at 45,100: surplus = 1 x (45,100 - 45,000) = 100, paid into the fund
A 200 shortfall is met by the fund; a better fill would instead have contributed 100 to it

Fees are excluded. The gap between the trigger and bankruptcy prices is the venue's exit room, and it is what the fund is sized against.

Procedure

Find the price at which the position's collateral is exactly exhausted. Compare it with the price the forced closure actually achieved. For a long, a fill below bankruptcy leaves a shortfall the fund must pay; a fill above it leaves a surplus the fund keeps. Reverse the inequality for a short.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 1 unit, long
  • P_0 = 50,000
  • m_i = 0.10 so collateral = 5,000
  • P_fill = 44,800
Steps
  1. P_bank = 50,000 x (1 - 0.10) = 45,000
  2. S = 1 x (45,000 - 44,800) = 200
A 200 shortfall, met from the insurance fund

Interpretation

The shortfall measures how far the exit missed the point at which the trader's own money ran out. It is a transfer, not a loss created by the fund: every unit the fund pays is a unit a winning trader receives and a losing trader could not fund.

Assumptions

  • A linear contract with collateral in the quote currency and a single isolated position; an inverse contract's bankruptcy price follows from its reciprocal payoff instead.
  • Fees, funding and liquidation penalties are excluded, and each of them moves the bankruptcy price nearer.
  • The venue is assumed to liquidate the whole position at one fill. Partial liquidation and tiered maintenance rates change both the trigger and the shortfall.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity describes one liquidation. The fund's adequacy depends on the joint distribution of many simultaneous liquidations, which this says nothing about.
  • Fund size, funding policy, disclosure and the rules that apply once it is exhausted are venue-specific and change; a published balance is a historical figure and not an audited guarantee.

Commonly confused with

Auto-Deleveraging

The insurance fund absorbs a shortfall without touching other traders. Auto-deleveraging is what happens when it cannot: profitable opposing positions are closed by the venue to balance the book. The fund is the buffer; auto-deleveraging is the failure mode behind it.
