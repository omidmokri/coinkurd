Due Diligence

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Checking for yourself whether what a project claims is true, before committing anything. It is verification work, not a way of deciding what is a good investment.

Professional definition

A structured verification process: confirming identity and registration, reading the code and the audits, checking the token supply and distribution against the contract, and testing whether every documented claim can be independently corroborated.

Technical definition

A procedure whose output is a list of claims that were confirmed, claims that could not be confirmed, and claims that were contradicted. The third category is decisive and the second is the one most often skipped. Regulators document recurring failure patterns — unverifiable teams, promises of guaranteed returns, pressure to act quickly, withdrawal obstacles — and the process is designed to surface those patterns rather than to forecast performance.

Academic definition

A systematic pre-commitment investigation intended to establish the factual basis of representations made about an asset, counterparty or project, and to document what could not be verified.

Mechanism

Diligence works by replacing a claim with a check. Every statement a project makes about itself has a source that either exists or does not: a company register, a block explorer, a contract, a published audit, a named auditor who will confirm the engagement. Where the check cannot be completed, that is the finding. Fraud survives on claims that nobody tried to resolve.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone evaluating a project before committing funds, time or a professional endorsement: allocators, exchange listing teams, journalists, grant committees and individual participants.
Why it matters
Most documented losses in this sector trace to claims that were never checked, and the checks are cheap relative to the exposure.
Limitations
Verification establishes what is true today about disclosed facts. It cannot price an asset, cannot see undisclosed arrangements, and offers no protection against risks that were fully disclosed.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a completed checklist as a recommendation to invest.
  • Accepting an audit badge without opening the report and its scope.
  • Stopping at claims that were confirmed and never listing those that could not be.

Examples

procedure

A minimum verification pass: resolve the legal entity in a public register; confirm each named person independently; open the audit and read its scope and date; verify the contract address from two sources; read it for mint, pause and owner powers; check holder distribution on chain; test a small withdrawal.

numeric

A project states that forty per cent of supply is community-held. On chain, ten addresses hold 680 million of the 1,000 million circulating, and a vesting contract releases a further 250 million within ninety days. The documented claim is contradicted, which ends the review.

Given
  • Stated community share of supply = 40%
  • Circulating supply = 1,000 million tokens
  • Top ten addresses hold 680 million
  • Vesting release in the next 90 days = 250 million
Steps
  1. Top-ten share = 680 / 1,000 = 68%
  2. Maximum possible community share = 100% - 68% = 32%
  3. Upcoming release as a share of current circulating supply = 250 / 1,000 = 25%
The stated 40 per cent community share is impossible on these holdings; a 25 per cent supply increase is scheduled within ninety days

Illustrative figures. Addresses are not holders, so the 68 per cent is an upper bound on concentration by address, not a count of people.

Commonly confused with

Smart Contract Audit

An audit is one input to diligence: a third party's review of code at a point in time and within a stated scope. Diligence also covers identity, registration, supply, distribution and whether funds can actually be withdrawn.
