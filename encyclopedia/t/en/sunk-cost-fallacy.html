Sunk Cost Fallacy

Simple definition

Staying in something because of what you already put into it. The money spent is gone either way; only what happens from here can still be changed.

Professional definition

Letting unrecoverable past outlays influence a forward-looking decision. The standard economic rule is that only incremental costs and benefits from the present moment should enter; a sunk cost is identical under every available choice and so cannot discriminate between them.

Technical definition

The fallacy is a departure from a normative rule, which makes it unusually clean: the correct comparison is between the expected value of holding from today's price and the expected value of the alternative uses of that capital, with the entry price absent from both. In practice the outlay is not psychologically sunk, because it sets a reference point against which exit is coded as a realised loss. That coding, not the arithmetic, is what makes the decision hard.

Academic definition

A violation of the normative irrelevance of unrecoverable prior expenditure, in which prior investment increases the propensity to continue a course of action independently of its prospective expected value.

Mechanism

Closing a losing position converts a paper loss into a realised one, and under prospect theory that step crosses the reference point where value is steepest. Holding keeps the outcome open, and in the loss region the gamble is preferred to the certain loss. The past outlay has no effect on the arithmetic; it works by fixing where zero sits.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders deciding whether to add to a losing position, and anyone holding an asset mainly because of what it cost.
Why it matters
Separating the two questions makes the decision answerable: would you open this position today at this price, with this capital?
Limitations
The rule says ignore the outlay, not ignore the position. Averaging into a plan that specified staged entries in advance is a different act from averaging to avoid a realised loss.

Common mistakes

  • Adding size to lower the average entry rather than because the thesis improved.
  • Measuring a position against your entry instead of today's price.
  • Holding for recovery with no defined invalidation level.

Examples

numeric

A position is down 40 percent. The forward question is whether this asset is the best use of the remaining 60, not whether it can return to the entry. Those two questions have different answers.

Given
  • entry at 100, current price 60, capital remaining in the position = 60
  • candidate alternative with the same risk and an expected 12 percent over the horizon
  • recovery to 100 requires a 66.7 percent gain
Steps
  1. required gain to break even = (100 - 60) / 60 = 0.667
  2. the entry price of 100 appears in no comparison between holding and switching
  3. the only inputs are expected return on 60 here versus expected return on 60 elsewhere
the break-even figure is a fact about arithmetic, not a reason to hold

Figures are illustrative. The point is structural: the entry price cancels out of every forward comparison, which is why it carries no decision weight.

everyday

A non-refundable ticket to an event you no longer want to attend. The money is spent whether you go or stay home, so the only live question is which evening you would rather have.

Commonly confused with

Dollar-Cost Averaging

Dollar-cost averaging is a schedule fixed in advance and indifferent to the current level. Averaging down to avoid closing a loss is a decision made by the loss itself.
