Simple definition

A way to prove a statement is true without revealing why it is true: the verifier learns the claim holds and nothing else.

Professional definition

A protocol in which a prover convinces a verifier that a statement holds while revealing nothing beyond its truth. Three properties define it: completeness, soundness, and the zero-knowledge property itself.

Technical definition

Completeness: an honest prover holding a valid witness always convinces an honest verifier. Soundness: a prover without a valid witness cannot convince the verifier except with negligible probability, and in deployed systems soundness is computational, resting on hardness assumptions. Zero-knowledge: the verifier learns nothing beyond the statement's truth, formalised by the existence of a simulator that produces an indistinguishable transcript without the witness. What is hidden is the witness — not the statement, and not anything outside the circuit.

Academic definition

An interactive or non-interactive proof system satisfying completeness, computational soundness, and a simulation-based zero-knowledge property with respect to the prover's witness.

Mechanism

The trick is to prove a computation was carried out correctly rather than to hand over its inputs. A verifier re-checks a short proof instead of re-running the work, which is why the same tool serves two different purposes: hiding data, and compressing verification. The two are often confused because one system can do both.

In practice

Who uses it
Rollup and privacy-protocol engineers, auditors of proving systems, and compliance teams evaluating selective disclosure.
Why it matters
It separates verification from disclosure, letting a chain check heavy computation cheaply or accept a claim without receiving the underlying data.
Limitations
It hides the witness only. Statements, values outside the circuit, timing and network metadata can all leak, and some systems need a trusted setup whose compromise breaks soundness.

Common mistakes

  • Reading zero-knowledge as anonymity.
  • Assuming a proof shows the inputs are true in the world, not merely consistent with the circuit.
  • Ignoring trusted-setup and implementation-bug risk.

Examples

everyday

Proving you are above an age threshold without showing your date of birth: the checker learns only that the threshold is met. Your name on the queue, and the time you arrived, are still visible.

crypto

A validity rollup publishes a short proof that a batch of transactions was executed under the right rules. The base chain verifies the proof without re-executing anything, and the proof says nothing about whether the inputs were honestly sourced.

Commonly confused with

Digital Signature

A signature proves a specific key authorised a specific message. A zero-knowledge proof proves a statement holds while keeping the data behind it secret. Both are verifiable without secrets, but they prove different kinds of thing.
