Probability Distribution

Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A full account of which values a quantity can take and how likely each one is. It is the shape behind an average, not a single number.

Professional definition

The complete assignment of probability across the possible values of a random variable. Discrete cases use a mass function, continuous cases a density; both integrate or sum to one, and both are summarised by the distribution function.

Technical definition

The law induced on the real line by a random variable. Every summary statistic — mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, any quantile, value at risk — is a functional of this object, so two series sharing a mean and a variance can still carry very different loss profiles if their distributions differ in shape.

Academic definition

The induced measure of a random variable on the Borel sets of the real line, characterised equivalently by its distribution function, its density or mass function, or its characteristic function.

Mechanism

Probability is a fixed total of one, spread across outcomes. Where you put it determines everything downstream: concentrate it near the centre and extremes become almost impossible; leave a sliver in the tails and the same mean and variance now describe a series that occasionally destroys capital.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk managers, option pricers, strategy developers and anyone converting a return series into a loss estimate.
Why it matters
Position sizing, value at risk, option prices and stress tests are all read off an assumed distribution. Choosing it is the single largest modelling decision in the chain.
Limitations
A distribution fitted to history is an estimate from one sample. It can be wrong in shape, and it can be right about the past while the process shifts underneath it.

Common mistakes

  • Summarising a return series by mean and variance alone.
  • Fitting a distribution and then forgetting it was fitted.
  • Reading a histogram of past returns as a forecast.

Examples

market

Two strategies report the same average monthly return and the same standard deviation. One has a tight, symmetric distribution; the other hides a small cluster of very large losses. The summary numbers match, the survival odds do not.

numeric

A rule wins 40% of the time for twice its risk and loses 60% of the time for one unit of risk. That two-point distribution has expected value plus 0.2 units per trade, even though most individual trades lose.

Given
  • Pr(win) = 0.40 with payoff +2 units of risk
  • Pr(loss) = 0.60 with payoff -1 unit of risk
  • probabilities sum to 1, so this is a valid distribution
Steps
  1. 0.40 x (+2) = +0.80
  2. 0.60 x (-1) = -0.60
  3. E = 0.80 - 0.60
E = +0.20 units of risk per trade

The probabilities are assumed, not measured. If the real win rate is 35% the same payoffs give 0.35 x 2 - 0.65 x 1 = +0.05, and a small estimation error has eaten most of the edge.

Mathematical foundation

\sum_i p(x_i) = 1 \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1 \quad \text{(continuous)}, \qquad F(x) = \Pr(X \le x)

Variables

p(x_i)Probability mass at the value x_i, for a discrete variableprobability, 0 to 1
f(x)Probability density at x, for a continuous variable; a rate, not a probabilityprobability per unit of x
F(x)Distribution function: accumulated probability up to xprobability, 0 to 1

Procedure

Decide whether the quantity is discrete or continuous. For discrete, list each value with its probability and check the probabilities sum to one. For continuous, specify a non-negative density whose integral over the whole line is one. Probabilities of ranges then come from summing or integrating; the distribution function is the running total from the left.

Worked example

Given
  • Two outcomes: +2 units with probability 0.40, -1 unit with probability 0.60
Steps
  1. Check it is a distribution: 0.40 + 0.60 = 1.00
  2. E = 0.40 x 2 + 0.60 x (-1)
  3. E = 0.80 - 0.60
E = +0.20 units of risk per trade

Interpretation

The distribution is the whole statement; the mean is one squeeze of it. Reporting only mean and variance discards exactly the shape information — asymmetry and tail weight — that decides whether a strategy survives a bad run.

Assumptions

  • Probabilities are non-negative and total exactly one; a set of weights that does not normalise is not a distribution.
  • For a continuous variable a density value is not a probability, and the probability of any single exact value is zero.
  • A distribution estimated from data inherits all the sampling error of that sample.
Limits of the formula
  • Shape cannot be read reliably from a short sample, and tail shape is the hardest part to estimate because tail observations are by definition rare.
  • Many financial series are non-stationary, so a single distribution may not describe the whole history at all.

Commonly confused with

Normal Distribution

A probability distribution is the general object. The normal is one special case of it — the one most often assumed by default, and often the wrong one for returns.
