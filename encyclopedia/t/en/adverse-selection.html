Adverse Selection

Simple definition

The risk that whoever takes your offer knows something you do not. Those who accept a price are, on average, the people for whom it is a good deal and for you a bad one.

Professional definition

The cost borne by a liquidity supplier because trade is not random: the counterparties who hit a resting quote are disproportionately those with better information, so a quoted price gets filled mainly when it is wrong. It is one of the three classical components of the bid-ask spread.

Technical definition

A selection effect in the conditional distribution of trades. Because the decision to trade carries information, the expected value of the asset conditional on receiving a buy order exceeds its unconditional expectation, and conditional on a sell order falls below it. A competitive, risk-neutral, zero-cost market maker must therefore quote an ask above and a bid below the unconditional mean, producing a positive spread with no inventory cost, no processing cost and no market power at all.

Academic definition

The informational cost incurred by an uninformed party whose willingness to transact at a posted price is exploited preferentially by better-informed counterparties, yielding a positive equilibrium spread under competition.

Mechanism

Quoting a firm price is writing a free option. The holder exercises it when it pays, which is when the quote is stale. Since the market maker cannot tell informed from uninformed flow in advance, the only defence is to widen until the losses to informed traders are covered by the gains from uninformed ones. If the informed share rises far enough, no finite spread clears and quoting stops altogether.

In practice

Who uses it
Market makers and automated market maker liquidity providers, insurers, lenders, and anyone whose business is to accept the other side on request.
Why it matters
It is the component of the spread that cannot be competed away, so it sets a floor on the cost of liquidity in any market with informed participants.
Limitations
The informed share is not directly observable. It is inferred from price impact or spread decompositions, and those estimates are model-dependent and unstable.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a wide spread as greed rather than as priced information risk.
  • Providing passive liquidity into a scheduled data release.
  • Assuming an automated market maker pool escapes it because there is no quoting human.

Examples

crypto

A liquidity provider in an automated market maker pool quotes continuously by formula. When the reference price jumps on another venue, arbitrageurs trade against the stale pool price first. The provider's loss there is adverse selection, not a fee problem.

numeric

Value is 120 or 80, equally likely. Three trades in ten come from someone who knows which; the rest are uninformed and buy or sell at random. A zero-cost competitive maker must quote 106 bid 94 — a spread of 12 on a mid of 100, created purely by information.

Given
  • V = 120 or 80 with probability 0.5 each
  • unconditional mean E[V] = 100
  • informed fraction alpha = 0.3; informed buy if V = 120, sell if V = 80
  • uninformed (0.7) buy or sell with probability 0.5 each
Steps
  1. P(buy, V=120) = 0.5 x (0.3 + 0.7 x 0.5) = 0.5 x 0.65 = 0.325
  2. P(buy, V=80) = 0.5 x (0.7 x 0.5) = 0.175
  3. P(buy) = 0.325 + 0.175 = 0.5
  4. ask = E[V | buy] = (120 x 0.325 + 80 x 0.175) / 0.5 = 53 / 0.5 = 106
  5. bid = E[V | sell] = 94 by symmetry
  6. spread = 106 - 94 = 12
ask 106, bid 94, spread 12 (12% of the mid)

No inventory cost, no processing cost and no market power enter this calculation. The whole spread is the price of being picked off, and it widens as alpha rises.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Write down the prior distribution of value and the probability that an order comes from an informed trader. Apply Bayes' rule to update the expected value separately on a buy and on a sell. Set the ask to the first and the bid to the second. The difference is the spread a competitive, zero-cost maker must charge.

Worked example

Given
  • V in {120, 80}, each with probability 0.5
  • alpha = 0.3
  • informed trade in the right direction with certainty; uninformed trade either way with probability 0.5
Steps
  1. P(buy) = 0.5
  2. E[V | buy] = (120 x 0.325 + 80 x 0.175) / 0.5 = 106
  3. E[V | sell] = 94
  4. s = 106 - 94 = 12
a = 106, b = 94, s = 12

Interpretation

The spread is not a markup over a known value; it is the Bayesian revision caused by the act of trading. Each fill is also a piece of news, which is why quotes move after a trade even when nothing else happened.

Assumptions

  • A risk-neutral, competitive market maker earning zero expected profit, with no order-processing cost and no inventory constraint; relaxing any of these adds further spread components.
  • The informed trader's signal is perfect in the simplest version. With a noisy signal the spread narrows but stays positive.
  • Order size is fixed at one unit. Allowing size makes the quote schedule depend on quantity, which is how real quote ladders arise.
Limits of the formula
  • The model explains the existence and sign of the spread, not its observed level: measured spreads also reflect fees, tick size, inventory risk and competition.
  • Alpha is unobservable, so empirical work substitutes proxies derived from price impact; different proxies give materially different estimates of the same market.

Commonly confused with

Slippage

Slippage is the gap between expected and achieved price on your own order, mostly a depth effect. Adverse selection is the loss borne by the party who supplied the quote, caused by who chose to trade against it.
