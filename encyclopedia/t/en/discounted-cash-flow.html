Discounted Cash Flow

Simple definition

A way of valuing something by forecasting the cash it will produce and shrinking each future amount to what it is worth today, then adding those amounts up.

Professional definition

A valuation method that projects free cash flow over an explicit forecast horizon, discounts each period at a rate reflecting its risk, and adds a terminal value for everything beyond the horizon. The output is only as good as the forecast, the discount rate and the terminal assumption.

Technical definition

Formally the present value of a forecast cash flow stream plus a discounted terminal value, usually built as a perpetuity growing at a rate strictly below the discount rate or as an exit multiple. In practice the terminal value commonly contributes the majority of the answer, so the valuation is dominated by two numbers chosen by the analyst — long-run growth and the discount rate — rather than by the detailed forecast.

Academic definition

The valuation of an asset as the sum of its expected future cash flows discounted at a rate commensurate with their risk, with a terminal value capturing flows beyond the explicit forecast horizon.

Mechanism

Discounting works because money has a time price: a sum due later is worth less than the same sum now, by the return available on an equally risky alternative. Dividing by one plus that rate, compounded, converts every future amount onto today's scale. The further out a flow lies, the more the answer depends on the rate chosen.

In practice

Who uses it
Corporate finance teams, equity and credit analysts, project appraisal functions, and anyone pricing an asset with a forecastable cash stream.
Why it matters
It is the only mainstream method that states its assumptions explicitly, so a disagreement about value becomes a disagreement about growth, risk or reinvestment that can be argued over.
Limitations
The terminal value usually dominates, small changes in the discount rate or terminal growth move the answer enormously, and the method is unusable where cash flows cannot be forecast at all.

Common mistakes

  • Presenting a single point estimate instead of a range.
  • Choosing a terminal growth rate above the long-run growth of the economy.
  • Discounting levered cash flow at a weighted average cost of capital.

Examples

numeric

Cash flows of 100, 110 and 120 are discounted at 10 per cent, then a terminal value is added using 3 per cent perpetual growth. The explicit years are worth 272 and the terminal value 1,327, so 83 per cent of the valuation sits beyond the forecast.

Given
  • Forecast free cash flow: year 1 = 100, year 2 = 110, year 3 = 120
  • Discount rate r = 10%
  • Terminal growth g = 3%, applied from year 4
Steps
  1. PV of year 1 = 100 / 1.10 = 90.91
  2. PV of year 2 = 110 / 1.10^2 = 90.91
  3. PV of year 3 = 120 / 1.10^3 = 90.16
  4. Year 4 cash flow = 120 x 1.03 = 123.60
  5. Terminal value at end of year 3 = 123.60 / (0.10 - 0.03) = 1,765.71
  6. PV of terminal value = 1,765.71 / 1.10^3 = 1,326.61
  7. Total = 90.91 + 90.91 + 90.16 + 1,326.61
Enterprise value = 1,598.6, of which 1,326.6 (83%) is terminal value

The three forecast years that took most of the modelling effort account for 17 per cent of the answer.

case

Two analysts model the same company and reach values 38 per cent apart. Neither forecast is wrong: one used a 9 per cent discount rate with 4 per cent terminal growth, the other 10 and 3. The disagreement is entirely in two cells of the spreadsheet.

Mathematical foundation

V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{TV_n}{(1+r)^n}, \qquad TV_n = \frac{CF_n(1+g)}{r-g}

Variables

CF_tForecast free cash flow in period t, on a stated levered or unlevered basiscurrency
rDiscount rate matching the risk and the claim being valuedratio per period
nLength of the explicit forecast horizonperiods
TV_nTerminal value at the end of the horizon, by perpetuity growth or exit multiplecurrency
gPerpetual growth rate of cash flow after the horizon, strictly below rratio per period

Procedure

Forecast free cash flow for each explicit period on one consistent basis. Divide each by one plus the discount rate raised to its period number. Compute a terminal value from the last forecast flow grown at g, discount it over n periods, and sum everything. Then re-run the whole model across a grid of r and g values.

Worked example

Given
  • CF = 100, 110, 120 for years 1 to 3
  • r = 0.10
  • g = 0.03
Steps
  1. Sum of discounted explicit flows = 90.91 + 90.91 + 90.16 = 271.98
  2. TV_3 = 120 x 1.03 / (0.10 - 0.03) = 1,765.71
  3. PV(TV) = 1,765.71 / 1.331 = 1,326.61
  4. V_0 = 271.98 + 1,326.61
V_0 = 1,598.6

Interpretation

The output is the present value implied by one set of assumptions, not a price and not a fact. Its usefulness lies in the sensitivity table around it: the range of values the plausible assumptions produce.

Assumptions

  • The perpetuity form requires g strictly below r and a stable reinvestment rate; it is not valid for a firm expected to grow faster than its discount rate indefinitely.
  • The discount rate must match the cash flow: weighted average cost of capital with unlevered flows, cost of equity with levered flows. Mixing them is a definitional error, not an approximation.
  • An exit multiple terminal value imports the market's current pricing of comparable firms, so the model stops being independent of relative valuation at that point.
Limits of the formula
  • The terminal value typically supplies most of the total, so most of the answer rests on two assumptions rather than on the detailed forecast.
  • Unusable where there is no forecastable cash flow, and highly unstable for early-stage firms, cyclical businesses and assets whose cash flows are optional rather than contractual.

Commonly confused with

Net Present Value

A discounted cash flow model produces a value. Net present value is that value minus what you must pay for it. The arithmetic is shared; the question is not.
