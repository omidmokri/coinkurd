Simple definition

A token you receive for staking, which you can trade or use elsewhere while the original coins stay locked. It stands in for the stake rather than being it.

Professional definition

A transferable claim on staked assets plus their accrued rewards, issued by a staking provider. It restores liquidity to a bonded position, so the same capital can both secure a chain and serve as collateral in lending or pool positions.

Technical definition

Three risks are layered on the underlying stake. Redemption depends on the provider's validators behaving and on the chain's exit queue, so the claim is not instantly convertible. The secondary market price can diverge from redemption value, and that discount widens exactly when exits are crowded. And because the receipt is widely accepted as collateral, a discount propagates into liquidations in protocols that never staked anything. Concentration adds a fourth: a dominant provider becomes a systemic dependency for the base chain itself.

Academic definition

A transferable derivative claim on bonded stake and its accrued rewards, whose market price may deviate from redemption value as a function of exit queue latency, provider credit and secondary market depth.

Mechanism

The token exists to break the trade-off between securing a chain and keeping capital usable, and it does so by converting a locked position into a claim that someone else must honour. Everything that follows comes from that substitution: an exit queue becomes a price discount, a provider becomes a counterparty, and a receipt accepted as collateral turns a staking problem into a lending problem.

In practice

Who uses it
Stakers who want yield without locking capital, lending protocols that accept the receipt as collateral, and anyone measuring provider concentration on a proof-of-stake chain.
Why it matters
It frees bonded capital for other uses and lets small holders stake without running validator infrastructure.
Limitations
The market price is not the redemption value, redemption is queued, and the provider's performance and governance sit between the holder and the underlying stake.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the receipt and the underlying asset as one price.
  • Borrowing against the receipt without modelling a discount scenario.
  • Assuming an exit is immediate because the token trades continuously.

Examples

numeric

A receipt redeemable for 1.04 units of the underlying trades at 1.01 in the market, a discount of about 2.9 percent. A position borrowed against it at a 75 percent threshold loses most of its safety margin from that discount alone.

Given
  • redemption value = 1.04 units of the underlying
  • secondary market price = 1.01 units
  • lending protocol values the receipt at the market price
Steps
  1. discount = 1 - 1.01 / 1.04 = 2.88%
  2. a position at 70% loan-to-value moves to 70% / (1 - 0.0288) = 72.1% on the discount alone
  3. the gap to a 75% threshold narrows from 5.0 to 2.9 points
A 2.9% discount consumes over 40% of the remaining margin to liquidation.

Illustrative figures. Whether a protocol prices the receipt at market or at redemption value is a parameter choice that changes this entirely.

market

Exits queue up after a market shock. Holders who need cash sell the receipt instead of waiting, the discount widens, and lending positions collateralised by it are liquidated — none of which involved the staked assets failing.

Commonly confused with

Wrapped Token

A wrapped token represents the same asset on another chain or standard. A staking receipt represents a bonded position plus rewards, redeemable only through a queue.
