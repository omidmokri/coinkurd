Paper Trading

Simple definition

Trading a rule with pretend money in real time. It checks that your process works; it does not check that your fills, or your nerve, would have held up.

Professional definition

Live simulation against current prices with no capital at risk. It validates plumbing, signal timing and operational routine, and it reliably overstates results, because the simulator fills orders the market would not have filled and charges costs the market would not have charged so lightly.

Technical definition

Four things are systematically missing. Queue position: a simulated limit order fills when price touches it, while a real one fills only once the queue ahead of it clears, so passive strategies are flattered most. Partial fills: a simulator usually fills all or nothing. Own impact: the paper order never consumes depth or moves the price it trades against. Funding and borrow: carry on perpetual and margin positions is often omitted. The fifth is not technical at all — simulated loss does not produce the behaviour that real loss produces.

Academic definition

Real-time simulated execution of a strategy without capital at risk, used to validate operational and signal-timing correctness while providing a systematically biased estimate of realisable performance.

Mechanism

A simulator has to guess what a real counterparty would have done, and every guess defaults in the simulator's favour: price touching a limit becomes a fill, size is always available, and nothing the order does changes the market. The errors are therefore one-directional, and largest exactly where a strategy intends to earn its edge — on passive quotes and in thin books.

In practice

Who uses it
Developers testing a new deployment, traders learning an unfamiliar venue, and anyone verifying that live wiring matches the backtest.
Why it matters
It is the cheapest way to find operational faults — wrong sign, wrong size, wrong timezone — before any capital is exposed.
Limitations
It cannot measure execution quality, and its psychological realism is zero: a clean paper record is evidence about the code, not about the trader.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a profitable paper record as validation of the edge.
  • Paper-trading a passive quoting strategy and believing the fills.
  • Omitting funding and borrowing costs from the simulation.

Examples

numeric

A maker strategy records a hundred fills a day in simulation and thirty-five live on the same signals. At three basis points per fill, two thirds of the simulated edge was queue position that was never earned.

Given
  • simulated fills = 100 per day
  • live fills on identical signals = 35 per day
  • gross capture per fill = 3 bps
  • unfilled attempts still consume quota and attention
Steps
  1. simulated gross = 100 x 3 = 300 bps per day
  2. live gross = 35 x 3 = 105 bps per day
  3. shortfall = (300 - 105) / 300 = 65%
65% of the paper result came from fills that would not have happened

Illustrative, but the direction is structural: the simulator fills on touch, the market fills on queue priority.

practical

Two weeks of paper trading catches a timezone error that put every signal an hour late, and a sizing bug that doubled one leg. Neither would have been visible in a backtest, and both would have cost real money.

Commonly confused with

Live Forward Testing

Paper trading simulates the fills; live forward testing takes them. Only the second measures queue position, partial fills, the strategy's own impact and the trader's behaviour under real loss.
