Iceberg Order

Simple definition

A large order that shows only a small slice in the book and republishes it as the slice fills, so the full size stays out of sight.

Professional definition

A venue-side order whose displayed quantity is much smaller than its total. Each time the visible slice is taken another is published automatically until the order is exhausted. It conceals size from the public book while the order itself sits at the venue.

Technical definition

A reserve order with display size and total size as separate parameters, where only the display size occupies the visible queue. Replenishment is automatic, and in most implementations the refreshed slice loses time priority to orders already waiting at that level, so the hidden portion executes more slowly than its total would suggest. Because repeated refills at one price are detectable, iceberg presence is itself a signal; some venues flag hidden executions after the fact. Minimum display sizes and availability differ by venue.

Academic definition

A conditional display instruction separating total order quantity from publicly disclosed quantity, intended to reduce the information leakage associated with large resting orders.

Mechanism

A large displayed order tells everyone what you intend before you have done it, and the market prices that in. Hiding the size reduces the leak but cannot close it: the refills themselves leave a pattern, so concealment buys time rather than secrecy.

In practice

Who uses it
Institutional desks working large size, treasury flow, and execution algorithms that want depth at a price without a visible footprint.
Why it matters
It reduces the information leakage and the front-running risk of showing a large resting order, which lowers realised market impact.
Limitations
It is not real secrecy: repeated refills at one price are a readable signature. Priority handling of refills and minimum display size are venue-specific.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming an iceberg is undetectable.
  • Reading visible depth as total depth where icebergs are in use.
  • Ignoring that a refilled slice usually goes to the back of the queue.

Examples

market

A level displays five lots, yet five hundred trade there over an hour while the displayed quantity never changes. The price is being defended by an iceberg, and the visible book was never the real depth.

numeric

A 50,000-unit order with a 500-unit display shows one percent of itself. Completing it needs a hundred refills, each of which may queue behind orders already resting at that price.

Given
  • total quantity 50,000
  • display size 500
  • refill restores the display size each time it is exhausted
Steps
  1. displayed fraction = 500 / 50000 = 0.01
  2. number of slices = 50000 / 500 = 100
1% displayed, 100 slices to complete

Queue treatment of each refill is a venue rule. Where refills go to the back of the queue, the completion time depends on other participants' resting quantity, not only on traded volume.

Commonly confused with

Market Depth

Market depth is measured from displayed quantity. An iceberg exists precisely so that displayed quantity understates what is there, so depth readings and iceberg use are in direct tension.
