Trading Bot

Category
Algorithmic Trading
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Software that places orders for you according to rules you set. It removes hesitation and fatigue from execution; it does not decide whether the rules were any good.

Professional definition

A program connected to a venue that reads market data, evaluates a fully specified rule and submits orders with no human in the loop. Automation industrialises whatever rule it is handed: a bad rule executed flawlessly loses money faster, and far more consistently, than a distracted human would have.

Technical definition

A bot has three parts that fail in different ways: a data path that can go stale or disconnect, a decision layer that can be no better than the test it came from, and an order path that must survive rejections, partial fills, duplicate submissions and reconnection without doubling a position. The edge, if any exists, lives only in the decision layer; most live losses come from the other two, plus costs the simulation charged too lightly.

Academic definition

An autonomous order-generating system mapping a market-data stream onto position decisions through a pre-specified rule set, with no discretionary human intervention at the point of execution.

Mechanism

Automation changes the variance of execution, not the expected value of the rule. A human hesitates, sizes inconsistently and skips signals after a loss, which adds noise and sometimes removes the worst trades by accident. A bot strips that noise out in both directions: every signal is taken at the stated size, so the rule's true expectancy, positive or negative, is what the account receives.

In practice

Who uses it
Systematic traders, market makers who cannot quote by hand, and anyone running a rule across more pairs or more hours than a person can watch.
Why it matters
It enforces the rule exactly as written, at all hours, and makes results attributable to the rule rather than to the operator's mood.
Limitations
It cannot create an edge, cannot notice that the regime changed, and will keep trading a broken rule until something outside it intervenes.

Common mistakes

  • Deploying with no kill switch and no maximum-position check.
  • Treating an untested rule as safer because code runs it.
  • Rebuilding position state from local memory after a disconnect.

Examples

crypto

A grid bot buys every fixed step down and sells every step up. In a range it collects small profits reliably; in a trend it accumulates losing inventory just as reliably, because the rule has no notion of being wrong.

numeric

Fees and spread of five basis points per round trip, twenty round trips a day, 250 days: the rule must earn more than 25,000 basis points of gross edge on traded notional just to break even.

Given
  • cost per round trip = 5 bps of traded notional
  • 20 round trips per day
  • 250 trading days
Steps
  1. 5 x 20 = 100 bps per day
  2. 100 x 250 = 25000 bps per year
25,000 bps of cumulative cost on turnover

This is cost measured on turnover, not on equity: it is the gross edge the rule must produce across all its trades, which is why trade frequency and cost matter more than win rate for an automated rule.

Commonly confused with

Order Execution Algorithm

A bot decides what position to hold. An execution algorithm decides how to work an order that has already been decided. A bot may contain one; the two answer different questions and fail for different reasons.
