Simple definition

A wallet you can regain after losing your key, because people or devices you chose in advance can together approve a new one.

Professional definition

A recovery design for programmable accounts: one signing key for everyday use, plus a set of guardians — other devices, trusted people or services — of whom a threshold can authorise replacing that key, usually after a delay the owner can cancel within.

Technical definition

Because a smart-contract account defines its own validation rules, recovery becomes a contract function instead of a secret you must keep intact for decades. Typical designs pair a guardian threshold with a timelock, so an unauthorised attempt is visible and cancellable. The risk model shifts rather than disappears: guardian collusion, guardian unavailability, stale guardian sets, and bugs in the recovery contract itself. The account is only as sound as that code and as independent as the guardians really are.

Academic definition

A key-recovery mechanism in which a threshold of pre-designated guardians may authorise rotation of an account's signing key, substituting distributed social and temporal safeguards for a single stored secret.

Mechanism

A recovery phrase makes the owner the only backup, which is why most permanent losses are self-inflicted. A programmable account lets the authorisation rule change instead of the secret being copied, so a lost device becomes a recoverable event — paid for with trust in a quorum of guardians and in the contract that enforces them.

In practice

Who uses it
Users of smart-contract accounts, wallet designers, and holders who judge their own risk of losing a key higher than their risk of theft.
Why it matters
It makes device loss survivable without handing custody to a firm and without one phrase that must outlive decades of moves and devices.
Limitations
It exists only on chains and wallets that support programmable accounts, depends on contract code that may be young, and fails if the guardian set is collusive, stale or unreachable.

Common mistakes

  • Naming guardians who all share one household or one device.
  • Never testing a recovery before it is needed.
  • Leaving the guardian list unchanged for years.

Examples

numeric

An account sets five guardians with a threshold of three and a 48-hour timelock. Three guardians can start a key replacement; the owner, if still in control, can cancel it inside those 48 hours.

Given
  • Guardians n = 5
  • Recovery threshold m = 3
  • Timelock = 48 hours
  • Guardians assumed independent of each other and of the owner's device
Steps
  1. Guardians that may be unreachable = 5 - 3 = 2
  2. Guardians that must collude to seize the account = 3
  3. Owner's cancellation window = 48 hours from the request
Two guardians may be lost; three must collude; the owner holds a 48-hour veto

The timelock only protects an owner who still controls the account and notices the request. If the key is already lost, the delay is simply a wait.

everyday

Spare house keys left with three relatives, with the rule that two of them together may call a locksmith. No single relative can open the door, and you are not locked out if one moves away.

Commonly confused with

Seed Phrase

A seed phrase is a secret you must preserve. Social recovery is a rule you can exercise, and it does not reconstruct a lost phrase.
