Information Ratio

Simple definition

How much a manager beat the benchmark, divided by how erratically they did it. High means the outperformance was consistent, not lucky.

Professional definition

Mean active return divided by tracking error, where active return is portfolio minus benchmark and tracking error is the standard deviation of that difference. It is the Sharpe ratio of the active bet, with the benchmark replacing the risk-free rate.

Technical definition

Numerator and denominator must use the same benchmark, the same periodicity and the same fee treatment. Annualising multiplies the numerator by the number of periods per year and the denominator by its square root, so the annualised ratio equals the periodic one times the root of the period count. Using the risk-free rate as the benchmark turns the measure back into the Sharpe ratio, which is why the benchmark must be the mandate's, not a convenience.

Academic definition

The ratio of expected differential return over a benchmark to the standard deviation of that differential return, used to assess the consistency of benchmark-relative performance.

Mechanism

Active return alone cannot be judged, because a manager can produce it by taking a larger deviation from the benchmark rather than by choosing better. Dividing by tracking error removes the size of the bet and leaves the quality of it, so two managers with different risk budgets become comparable on a single scale.

In practice

Who uses it
Allocators selecting active managers, internal risk committees reviewing mandates, and strategy developers comparing an overlay against its base.
Why it matters
It is the standard measure of skill per unit of deviation from a mandate, and it makes managers with different risk budgets comparable.
Limitations
It is a sample statistic with a wide confidence interval: short records cannot separate a good ratio from a lucky one.

Common mistakes

  • Using the risk-free rate as the benchmark and calling the result an information ratio.
  • Comparing an annualised ratio with a monthly one.
  • Ranking managers on a two-year ratio as if the ordering were reliable.

Examples

numeric

A fund returns 12% against a benchmark's 10%, with tracking error 4%. Active return is 2% and the information ratio is 0.5 — two points of outperformance bought with four points of deviation.

Given
  • portfolio return = 12% per year
  • benchmark return = 10% per year
  • annualised tracking error = 4%
  • both measured net of the same fees
Steps
  1. active return = 12% - 10% = 2%
  2. IR = 2% / 4% = 0.5
information ratio = 0.5

The same fund with tracking error of 1% would show an information ratio of 2.0 on identical outperformance — the denominator carries most of the verdict.

worked-series

Quarterly active returns +3, -1, +3, -1, +1 percent give a mean of 1% and tracking error of exactly 2%, so the quarterly ratio is 0.5. Annualised it becomes 0.5 times the square root of four, or 1.0.

Given
  • active returns = [3, -1, 3, -1, 1] % per quarter
  • sample divisor n - 1
  • m = 4 quarters per year
Steps
  1. mean = 1% per quarter
  2. sample standard deviation = 2% per quarter
  3. quarterly IR = 1 / 2 = 0.5
  4. annualised numerator = 1% * 4 = 4%; annualised TE = 2% * sqrt(4) = 4%
  5. annualised IR = 4 / 4 = 1.0, which equals 0.5 * sqrt(4)
quarterly IR 0.5, annualised IR 1.0

Annualisation multiplies the ratio by the square root of the period count, so the periodicity must always be stated with the figure.

Mathematical foundation

\mathrm{IR} = \frac{\bar{R}_p - \bar{R}_b}{\mathrm{TE}} = \frac{\bar{a}}{\sigma_a}, \qquad \mathrm{IR}_{\text{ann}} = \mathrm{IR}\sqrt{m}

Variables

\bar{a}Mean active return: portfolio minus benchmark% per period
\sigma_aTracking error: standard deviation of active return% per period
\bar{R}_pMean portfolio return% per period
\bar{R}_bMean benchmark return over the same periods% per period
mPeriods per year used for annualisationcount

Procedure

Choose the mandate benchmark and keep it fixed. Form the active return series period by period. Average it for the numerator and take its standard deviation for the denominator. Divide. To annualise, multiply the ratio by the square root of the periods per year, and report the benchmark, the period and the fee basis with the number.

Worked example

Given
  • portfolio 12% per year, benchmark 10% per year
  • annualised tracking error 4%
  • second case: quarterly active returns [3, -1, 3, -1, 1] %
Steps
  1. case A: active = 2%, IR = 2 / 4 = 0.5
  2. case B: mean active = 1% per quarter, TE = 2% per quarter, quarterly IR = 0.5
  3. case B annualised: 0.5 * sqrt(4) = 1.0
IR = 0.5 in case A; 0.5 quarterly and 1.0 annualised in case B

Interpretation

The figure is active return per unit of active risk. Zero means the deviation from the benchmark bought nothing; a negative value means it cost money. Magnitude is only meaningful once the periodicity is known.

Assumptions

  • The benchmark is the mandate's benchmark. Substituting the risk-free rate produces the Sharpe ratio under a different name.
  • Arithmetic active return is standard; a geometric definition of active return exists and gives slightly different values.
  • Annualisation by the square root of m assumes serially independent active returns, which persistent active positions violate.
Limits of the formula
  • It has a wide sampling distribution, so short records cannot distinguish skill from chance.
  • It is blind to the shape of the active return distribution: a ratio built on one huge quarter scores the same as one built on steady increments.
  • Reporting the best ratio among many candidate managers or strategies inflates it by selection.

Related terminology

Commonly confused with

Sharpe Ratio

Sharpe measures excess return over the risk-free rate per unit of total volatility. The information ratio measures excess return over a benchmark per unit of tracking error.

Commonly confused with

Alpha

Alpha is a level of return a model cannot explain. The information ratio is a consistency score: the same alpha earned with less deviation scores higher.
