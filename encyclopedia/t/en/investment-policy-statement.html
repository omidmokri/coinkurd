Simple definition

A written document recording what a portfolio is for: its objectives, its constraints, how it may be invested, how it will be measured, and who is allowed to decide what.

Professional definition

The governing document of a portfolio. It states return and risk objectives, liquidity needs, time horizon, tax and legal constraints, the permitted asset classes and their ranges, the rebalancing rule, the benchmark, delegated authorities and the review schedule.

Technical definition

A governance artefact that converts objectives and constraints into executable policy: an opportunity set, policy weights with permitted ranges, a risk budget for deviations, a benchmark specified in advance, and an explicit map of decision rights. Its function is to make later action auditable, because any decision can be checked against a rule written before the outcome was known, and to move policy-setting away from the moment of stress in which it would otherwise happen.

Academic definition

A written mandate specifying an investor's objectives, constraints, permitted exposures and evaluation framework, serving as the ex-ante reference against which realised decisions are audited.

Mechanism

Decisions made inside a drawdown are made with incomplete information and under pressure. Writing the rules first moves the decision to a calmer moment and makes any departure from them visible rather than silent. The document has no enforcement power of its own: its value comes entirely from having been specified before the event and from actually being consulted during one.

In practice

Who uses it
Trustees, investment committees, advisers and their clients, and individuals who want their own rules written down before they are needed.
Why it matters
It fixes objectives, limits and the benchmark in advance, so both realised performance and departures from plan can later be judged honestly.
Limitations
A document cannot create discipline. If it is vague, never reviewed, or quietly overridden in stress, it records intentions rather than governing behaviour.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a return objective with no matching risk limit or liquidity schedule.
  • Naming no benchmark, which leaves the portfolio impossible to evaluate.
  • Leaving the rebalancing rule and decision rights undefined.

Examples

everyday

A household's written plan states that two years of expenses stay in instruments realisable within days, the rest follows fixed target weights with plus or minus 5 point ranges, and the whole document is reviewed each January and whenever income changes. None of it is a forecast.

numeric

A plan requires 18 months of a 120,000 annual spending need to be held in assets realisable within five business days. On a 1,500,000 portfolio that reserves 180,000, or 12% of the total, before any allocation decision is taken.

Given
  • annual spending requirement: 120,000
  • policy: 18 months of spending held in assets realisable within five business days
  • total portfolio: 1,500,000
Steps
  1. liquidity reserve = 120,000 x 1.5 = 180,000
  2. reserve as a share of portfolio = 180,000 / 1,500,000 = 0.12
12% of the portfolio is reserved by the liquidity constraint before any allocation decision

The constraint binds first: the remaining 88% is the opportunity set the allocation policy works on. The figures are illustrative, not a recommendation about reserve size.

Commonly confused with

Strategic Asset Allocation

The policy weights are one section of the statement. The statement also fixes the objectives those weights serve, the constraints they must respect, the benchmark, and who may change them.
