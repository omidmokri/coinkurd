Bond yields and prices

Simple definition

A bond's promised payments are fixed, so if buyers demand a higher return they can only get it by paying less. Yields up means prices down — always, by arithmetic.

Professional definition

Price is the present value of a fixed cash-flow schedule discounted at the yield demanded. The inverse relation is therefore an identity, not a behaviour. Its steepness is measured by duration, the first-order sensitivity of price to a small yield change, with convexity as the second-order correction.

Technical definition

Macaulay duration is the cash-flow-weighted average time to receipt; modified duration divides it by one plus the periodic yield and gives the percentage price change per unit yield change. The approximation is linear and therefore understates price for large moves in either direction, which convexity partly corrects. Duration rises with maturity, falls with coupon and falls with yield, so the same maturity carries different risk at different coupons.

Academic definition

The inverse mapping between a fixed-income instrument's required yield and its present value, whose local elasticity is modified duration and whose curvature is convexity.

Mechanism

The coupons and the face repayment do not change when market rates move, so the only adjustable quantity is the price. A higher required yield discounts the same promised cash more heavily, and distant payments shrink most, which is why long-maturity and low-coupon bonds move most for a given yield change.

In practice

Who uses it
Fixed-income managers, treasury and ALM teams, risk officers setting rate limits, and anyone holding bonds or bond funds through a rate cycle.
Why it matters
Duration converts an interest-rate view into an expected price move and lets positions of different maturities be compared and hedged on one scale.
Limitations
Duration is a local linear approximation valid for small parallel shifts; real curves twist, credit spreads move with yields, and callable or floating structures break the mapping.

Common mistakes

  • Applying duration to a large yield move without convexity.
  • Assuming a parallel curve shift when the curve twists.
  • Thinking a bond fund cannot lose money.

Examples

numeric

A bond has modified duration 7.2 and convexity 60. Yields rise 50 basis points. The linear estimate is a 3.6 percent price fall; adding convexity softens it to about 3.53 percent.

Given
  • modified duration = 7.2
  • convexity = 60
  • change in yield = +0.0050 (50 bp)
Steps
  1. first order = -7.2 x 0.0050 = -0.0360
  2. second order = 0.5 x 60 x 0.0050^2 = +0.00075
  3. total = -0.0360 + 0.00075
estimated price change = about -3.53%

Duration and convexity values are invented. Both are themselves functions of yield, so they must be recomputed after a large move.

market

Two bonds mature on the same day. The zero-coupon one has duration equal to its maturity; the high-coupon one has much less, because more of its value arrives early. The same yield move hits them very differently.

Procedure

Compute Macaulay duration as the present-value-weighted average time to each cash flow, then divide by one plus the periodic yield to get modified duration. Multiply by minus the yield change for the first-order price effect, and add half the convexity times the squared yield change for the curvature correction.

Worked example

Given
  • D_mod = 7.2
  • C = 60
  • delta y = +0.0050
Steps
  1. -7.2 x 0.0050 = -0.0360
  2. 0.5 x 60 x (0.0050)^2 = 0.5 x 60 x 0.000025 = 0.00075
  3. sum = -0.03525
price change of about -3.53% for a 50 bp rise in yield

Interpretation

Modified duration is the slope of the price-yield curve at the current yield, read as a percentage price change per 100 basis points. It is an approximation at a point, so it degrades as the yield move grows and must be recomputed after the move.

Assumptions

  • The yield change is small and the whole curve shifts in parallel; a twist in the curve is not captured by a single duration number.
  • Cash flows are fixed and certain. Callable, putable, floating-rate or defaultable bonds need effective-duration measures instead.
  • Duration and convexity are stated per the bond's own compounding convention; mixing annual and semi-annual conventions misstates the result.
Limits of the formula
  • The linear term alone always overstates the loss from a yield rise and understates the gain from a fall, because the true relation is convex.
  • It measures sensitivity to yield, not to credit spread, liquidity or inflation, each of which can move price independently.

Commonly confused with

Yield to maturity

Yield to maturity is the single discount rate that reproduces the current price. Duration is the sensitivity of that price to a change in the rate. One is a level, the other a slope.
