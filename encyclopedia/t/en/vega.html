Simple definition

How much an option's price changes when the volatility used to price it changes by one point. It is positive for anyone who owns options.

Professional definition

The partial derivative of option value with respect to the volatility input, ∂V/∂σ, quoted per one percentage point of volatility. Positive for a long call and a long put alike, negative for both when written. Vega is not a Greek letter, which is why some texts call the same quantity kappa.

Technical definition

Under Black-Scholes, vega equals the underlying price times the square root of time remaining times the standard normal density at d1, identical for a call and a put on the same strike and expiry. It grows with time to expiry and peaks near the money, so it is the long-dated mirror of gamma rather than its twin. It measures sensitivity to a single volatility number, which is exactly the object the volatility smile shows does not exist.

Academic definition

The first-order sensitivity of a contingent claim's model value to its volatility parameter, undefined outside a specified pricing model.

Mechanism

An option is a claim on movement, so raising the assumed amount of movement raises what the claim is worth, whichever direction the holder is positioned for. The effect is biggest where there is the most time for movement to accumulate and where the outcome is still open, which is why vega rises with maturity and concentrates near the strike.

In practice

Who uses it
Volatility desks whose profit is in the volatility input rather than direction, structurers pricing long-dated books, and risk teams stressing an options portfolio against a volatility shock.
Why it matters
It separates the part of a position's profit and loss that comes from the market repricing volatility from the part that comes from the underlying moving.
Limitations
A single vega assumes one volatility number shifts for every strike at once. The smile says that is not how a surface moves, so aggregate vega can understate real exposure.

Common mistakes

  • Adding vegas across maturities as if a one-point shift were equally likely at each.
  • Buying options on a volatility view without noticing the position is also long gamma and short theta.
  • Reading vega as a per-unit-of-variance figure rather than per volatility point.

Examples

market

After a quiet period, protection is bid and the volatility input used across a chain rises. A holder of long-dated options gains even with the underlying unchanged; a writer of the same contracts loses on a market that never moved.

numeric

An option with vega 0.12 and a premium of 4.50 is repriced from a 55% volatility input to 60%. All else equal the premium rises about 0.60, to roughly 5.10, with no change in the underlying at all.

Given
  • vega = 0.12 per volatility point
  • volatility input moves from 55% to 60%
  • underlying, strike, rate and time unchanged
Steps
  1. change in volatility = 5 points
  2. 0.12 x 5 = 0.60
  3. 4.50 + 0.60 = 5.10
premium about 5.10

First order only. Vega itself changes as volatility changes, so a five-point move is already outside the range where the linear estimate is reliable.

Procedure

Vega is defined as the derivative of value with respect to the volatility parameter, everything else fixed. In the Black-Scholes framework, multiply the underlying price by the square root of time remaining and by the standard normal density at d1. Divide by 100 to express the figure per one percentage point, which is how it is quoted.

Worked example

Given
  • vega = 0.12 per volatility point
  • premium = 4.50
  • volatility input 55% to 60%
Steps
  1. 0.12 x 5 = 0.60
  2. 4.50 + 0.60 = 5.10
premium about 5.10 at the higher volatility input

Interpretation

Vega measures exposure to a repricing of uncertainty itself. A long-vega book profits when the market pays more for optionality, regardless of direction; a short-vega book is selling that repricing risk.

Assumptions

  • Vega has no meaning without a named model. The closed form above is Black-Scholes for a European option on a non-dividend underlying, and the number changes under a different model.
  • The quoting convention is per one percentage point of annualised volatility. A figure expressed per unit of volatility is one hundred times larger, and a figure per unit of variance is a different quantity again.
  • Taking the derivative with respect to a single sigma presumes one volatility applies to all strikes. That assumption is what the volatility smile contradicts, so desks hedge the surface with several bucketed vegas rather than one.
  • The name is a convention, not a Greek letter: the same sensitivity appears as kappa in part of the literature, and no standard symbol is universal.
Limits of the formula
  • Vega falls as expiry approaches, so a short-dated book can be nearly vega-flat and still be dangerous through gamma. The two Greeks do not substitute for one another.
  • First order only: vega itself moves with volatility, with the underlying and with time, so large volatility shocks need a full revaluation rather than a vega multiplication.
  • Aggregating vega across strikes and maturities assumes a parallel shift of the surface, which is not how observed surfaces move.

Commonly confused with

Implied Volatility

Implied volatility is the level of the input. Vega is how much the premium moves when that level changes. One is a coordinate, the other a slope.
