Fama-French Factors

Simple definition

A way of explaining a portfolio's returns by three things rather than one: the market, how small the companies are, and how cheap they look on book value.

Professional definition

A cross-sectional return model that adds two long-short portfolios to the market factor: small minus big, and high minus low book-to-market. A fund's average return is then attributed to its loadings on the three, and what is left over is the intercept.

Technical definition

A time-series regression of excess returns on three constructed factor returns. SMB and HML are returns on zero-cost portfolios formed by sorting on market capitalisation and on book-to-market, and their inclusion absorbs much of the cross-sectional variation in average returns that the market factor alone leaves unexplained. Whether the loadings represent compensated risk or a pattern surviving from the sample used to find it remains disputed.

Academic definition

A three-factor empirical asset-pricing specification in which expected excess returns are linear in sensitivities to market, size and value factor portfolios.

Mechanism

Sorting firms on size and on book-to-market produces portfolios whose returns move together in ways the market index misses. Adding those two series gives the regression directions in which average returns actually differ, so loadings explain much of what once looked like skill. Whether they earn a premium for bearing risk, or because the sorts were chosen after seeing the data, is what the model cannot settle.

In practice

Who uses it
Performance analysts separating skill from exposure, fund allocators, factor-portfolio builders, and researchers reporting alpha.
Why it matters
It raises the bar for a claim of skill: a manager whose return is explained by size and value loadings has delivered exposure, which is cheap to buy.
Limitations
Factor returns are construction-dependent: breakpoints, rebalancing dates, exchange coverage and the treatment of financials all change the series and therefore the alpha.

Common mistakes

  • Reporting alpha without naming the factor data source and sample.
  • Applying equity-sorted factors to crypto assets that have no book value.
  • Treating a measured premium as a stable future expected return.

Examples

numeric

A fund has loadings of 1.1 on the market, 0.4 on size and minus 0.3 on value. With illustrative premia of 6%, 2% and 3%, the model attributes 6.5% of expected excess return to exposure alone — before any skill is counted.

Given
  • b = 1.1 (market), s = 0.4 (SMB), h = -0.3 (HML)
  • illustrative factor premia: market 6%, SMB 2%, HML 3%
  • these premia are assumptions for the arithmetic, not observed current values
Steps
  1. 1.1 x 6% = 6.6%
  2. 0.4 x 2% = 0.8%
  3. -0.3 x 3% = -0.9%
  4. sum = 6.6% + 0.8% - 0.9% = 6.5%
6.5% of expected excess return attributable to factor exposure

Any excess over this figure is the regression intercept. It is only alpha if the three factors are the right risk model, which is itself the open question.

research

Two analysts run the same regression on the same fund and report different alphas. Neither made an error: they used factor series built with different breakpoints and universes, and the intercept absorbs that difference entirely.

Procedure

Obtain the three factor return series for the same market and frequency as the asset. Regress the asset's excess return on them over a stated sample. Report the loadings, the intercept with its standard error, and the fit. State the factor data source: different constructions give different intercepts for identical holdings.

Worked example

Given
  • b = 1.1, s = 0.4, h = -0.3
  • illustrative premia: market 6%, SMB 2%, HML 3%
Steps
  1. 1.1 x 6 = 6.6
  2. 0.4 x 2 = 0.8
  3. -0.3 x 3 = -0.9
  4. total = 6.5
6.5% expected excess return from exposure

Interpretation

Loadings describe what the portfolio is exposed to; the intercept is what the three factors cannot account for. A positive intercept is evidence of either skill or a missing factor, and the regression cannot tell you which.

Assumptions

  • The factors are returns on investable zero-cost portfolios; the specification is an empirical description and is not derived from a utility-based equilibrium.
  • Loadings are treated as constant over the sample. Allowing them to vary with the business cycle changes both the loadings and the intercept.
  • Factor series are not unique. Breakpoints, the universe of firms, rebalancing frequency and currency all vary across providers, and the model is silent on which construction is correct.
Limits of the formula
  • The original evidence is from listed equities with book values. Assets without book equity, including crypto assets, have no HML analogue, so the model cannot simply be carried across.
  • The factors were identified in the same historical data later used to confirm them, so part of the measured premium may be a selection effect rather than compensation for risk.

Commonly confused with

Capital Asset Pricing Model

The capital asset pricing model derives expected returns from one factor under equilibrium assumptions. The Fama-French specification adds two empirically chosen factors and makes no equilibrium derivation, so a positive intercept means something different in each.
