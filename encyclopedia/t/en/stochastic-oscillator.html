Stochastic Oscillator

Simple definition

A 0 to 100 gauge of where the latest close sits inside the recent high-low range. Near 100 means it closed near the top of that range, near 0 near the bottom.

Professional definition

Lane's oscillator. %K locates the close within the highest high and lowest low of a lookback, usually 14 periods, on a 0 to 100 scale; %D is a short moving average of %K, conventionally 3 periods, and the crossover of the two is the classic signal. Above 80 and below 20 are the usual thresholds.

Technical definition

%K is 100 times the close minus the lowest low, divided by the highest high minus the lowest low over the lookback; %D is an m-period moving average of %K, and the slow variant smooths %K once before %D is taken. The denominator is an extreme-value range, so one spike governs it for the whole window, the reading is undefined when the range is zero, and it saturates at 0 or 100 whenever the close is the window extreme — which happens continuously in a trend.

Academic definition

A bounded oscillator expressing the current closing price as its relative position within the empirical high-low range of a fixed lookback window, together with a smoothed signal line derived from that series.

Mechanism

The reading is a ratio of two distances on the same price scale, so it is dimensionless and bounded by construction. Because the denominator is the window's full extreme range, a single outlier bar can hold it wide for n periods and flatten the oscillator; and in a trend the close keeps printing at the window extreme, pinning the line near 0 or 100 while price continues on its way.

In practice

Who uses it
Technical analysts and systematic developers screening for momentum extremes, crossovers and divergence, mostly in range-bound markets.
Why it matters
It puts position-in-range on one fixed scale, so a single threshold can be applied across instruments and the close is compared with the range that contained it.
Limitations
It saturates in trends, so 80 and 20 are not reversal levels. Fast, slow and full variants smooth differently and give different numbers from the same prices.

Common mistakes

  • Buying every cross back above 20 inside a downtrend.
  • Treating fast and slow stochastic readings as one scale.
  • Reading saturation at 100 as proof that a reversal is due.

Examples

market

In a steady uptrend the close prints near the window's high day after day, so %K sits in the 90s for weeks. Every cross back down looked like a sell signal and every one of them was inside a continuing trend.

numeric

Over the lookback the highest high is 120 and the lowest low 100, and the close is 115. %K is 75. With the two prior %K values 65 and 70, a three-period %D is 70.

Given
  • n = 14
  • highest high over n = 120
  • lowest low over n = 100
  • close = 115
  • previous %K values = 65 and 70
  • m = 3
Steps
  1. close - lowest low = 115 - 100 = 15
  2. highest high - lowest low = 120 - 100 = 20
  3. %K = 100 x 15 / 20 = 75
  4. %D = (65 + 70 + 75) / 3 = 70
%K = 75, %D = 70

A reading of 75 says the close sat three quarters of the way up the window's range. It is a position, not a probability.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Take the highest high and lowest low of the last n bars. Subtract the lowest low from the close, divide by the range, multiply by 100 to get %K. Average the last m values of %K to get %D. In the slow variant, smooth %K first and treat that smoothed series as %K.

Worked example

Given
  • n = 14
  • H_n = 120
  • L_n = 100
  • C_t = 115
  • m = 3 with previous %K of 65 and 70
Steps
  1. numerator = 115 - 100 = 15
  2. denominator = 120 - 100 = 20
  3. %K = 100 x 0.75 = 75
  4. %D = (65 + 70 + 75) / 3
%K = 75, %D = 70

Interpretation

A reading describes where the close fell inside the recent range: 50 is the middle of the range, not a balance of gains and losses. The crossover of %K and %D is a statement about the short-term direction of that position, nothing more.

Assumptions

  • n = 14 and m = 3 are common defaults, not constants.
  • The slow and full variants smooth %K before computing %D, so the same prices give different values under fast, slow and full settings; a level is only comparable within one fixed configuration.
  • If the highest high equals the lowest low the ratio is undefined; platforms substitute a fixed value, commonly 50 or the previous reading.
  • Highs and lows are taken from the bar extremes, which include wicks, so the measure depends on the data feed's handling of spikes.
Limits of the formula
  • Bounded and range-based, so it saturates in trends and stays at an extreme while price continues; level-based reversal signals fail there.
  • The denominator is an extreme-value statistic, so one spike dictates the scale for the whole lookback.
  • It carries no information about the size of the moves inside the range, only about where the close ended up.

Commonly confused with

Relative Strength Index

The stochastic asks where the close sits inside the high-low range. RSI asks how large average gains have been relative to average losses. Both run from 0 to 100 and answer different questions, so their extremes do not coincide.
