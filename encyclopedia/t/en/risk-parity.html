Simple definition

Sizing holdings so each one contributes about the same amount of risk, rather than the same amount of money. Quieter assets get larger weights and volatile ones get smaller.

Professional definition

A construction rule that sets weights inversely proportional to each asset's volatility, so that every position's standalone risk contribution is equal. It replaces the capital-allocation question with a risk-allocation question, and in its simple form uses no return forecasts at all.

Technical definition

The naive form sets each weight proportional to the reciprocal of that asset's volatility, normalised to sum to one, which equalises the products of weight and volatility. That equals true equal risk contribution only when all pairwise correlations are identical; the general case requires equalising marginal contributions from the covariance matrix and has no closed form for more than two assets. Because low-volatility assets dominate the weights, the resulting portfolio has low expected volatility and is commonly levered to a target.

Academic definition

A portfolio construction methodology that allocates weights to equalise constituents' contributions to total portfolio risk, with the inverse-volatility solution exact under equicorrelation and requiring numerical solution of marginal risk contributions otherwise.

Mechanism

Equal money weights hand the riskiest asset control of the portfolio: its larger swings dominate the total. Dividing each weight by its volatility removes that asymmetry, so no single holding determines the outcome. The price of the symmetry is that the construction is built on volatility estimates, and those are backward-looking, so the balance it achieves is the balance of the past window.

In practice

Who uses it
Multi-asset allocators, managed-futures and volatility-targeting programmes, and anyone splitting capital between instruments whose volatilities differ by a multiple.
Why it matters
It stops one volatile holding from driving the whole portfolio, and it requires no return forecast, only a volatility estimate.
Limitations
Volatility estimates are unstable, the inverse-volatility shortcut assumes equal correlations, and reaching a return target usually requires leverage with its own funding and margin risks.

Common mistakes

  • Treating inverse-volatility weights as true equal risk contribution regardless of correlations.
  • Levering up to a volatility target without sizing the margin-call risk.
  • Re-estimating volatility so often that the portfolio trades on noise.

Examples

numeric

Asset A has 10 percent volatility and asset B 40 percent. Inverse-volatility weights are 80 percent in A and 20 percent in B. Each then contributes 0.08 of standalone risk, and at zero correlation the portfolio volatility is 11.3 percent.

Given
  • sigma_A = 0.10, sigma_B = 0.40
  • weights proportional to 1 / sigma
  • correlation = 0 for the portfolio volatility step
Steps
  1. 1 / 0.10 = 10 and 1 / 0.40 = 2.5, sum = 12.5
  2. w_A = 10 / 12.5 = 0.80, w_B = 2.5 / 12.5 = 0.20
  3. risk contributions: 0.80 x 0.10 = 0.08 and 0.20 x 0.40 = 0.08
  4. portfolio variance at zero correlation = 0.08^2 + 0.08^2 = 0.0128
  5. portfolio volatility = sqrt(0.0128) = 0.1131
Weights 80 / 20, equal standalone risk contributions of 0.08, portfolio volatility 11.3 percent.

To reach a 20 percent volatility target this portfolio would need roughly 1.8 times leverage, which introduces funding cost and margin-call risk that the unlevered version does not have.

market

A two-asset book of a government bond and an equity index ends up mostly in bonds under this rule, because the bond's volatility is a fraction of the index's. The allocation looks conservative in money terms and is balanced only in risk terms.

Procedure

Estimate each asset's volatility over one stated window and return frequency. Take the reciprocal of each volatility, sum the reciprocals, and divide each by the sum to obtain weights that add to one. Verify that the products of weight and volatility are equal. If a volatility target is required, compute the unlevered portfolio volatility first and only then decide whether leverage is acceptable, and state its cost.

Worked example

Given
  • sigma_A = 0.10
  • sigma_B = 0.40
  • two assets, long only, weights sum to one
Steps
  1. reciprocals: 1 / 0.10 = 10, 1 / 0.40 = 2.5
  2. sum of reciprocals = 12.5
  3. w_A = 10 / 12.5 = 0.80
  4. w_B = 2.5 / 12.5 = 0.20
  5. check: 0.80 x 0.10 = 0.08 and 0.20 x 0.40 = 0.08
w_A = 80 percent, w_B = 20 percent, with equal risk contributions of 0.08.

Interpretation

Equal products of weight and volatility mean no single asset dominates the portfolio's variability. The weights will look lopsided in money terms, which is the point: capital and risk are different allocations, and this rule balances the second.

Assumptions

  • Volatilities are estimated over one consistent window and frequency, and are treated as stable over the holding period.
  • The inverse-volatility solution equalises true risk contributions only if all pairwise correlations are equal. Otherwise the correct construction equalises marginal contributions from the covariance matrix.
  • Long-only weights summing to one. Short or levered versions change the normalisation and the risk accounting.
  • Volatility is accepted as the measure of risk. Assets with similar volatility but very different tail behaviour are treated as equivalent by this rule.
Limits of the formula
  • It concentrates capital in low-volatility assets, which concentrates exposure to whatever drives those assets, such as interest rates for bonds.
  • Reaching a return or volatility target normally requires leverage, which adds funding cost, margin-call risk and the possibility of forced deleveraging in exactly the periods volatility rises.
  • Volatility estimates rise after a shock, so the rule mechanically sells what has just fallen, which can be procyclical across many funds following it at once.
  • It uses no return forecast at all, so it cannot distinguish a low-volatility asset with a positive expected return from one with a negative one.

Commonly confused with

Asset Allocation

Asset allocation is the general question of how capital is divided, usually with a view on expected returns. Risk parity is one specific answer that ignores return forecasts entirely and equalises risk contributions instead.
