Real interest rate

Simple definition

The interest rate after inflation has been taken out: what your money actually buys more of, rather than how many more currency units you end up with.

Professional definition

The nominal rate deflated by inflation over the same horizon. Ex ante it uses expected inflation and is the rate that matters for borrowing and investment decisions; ex post it uses realised inflation and is what lenders actually earned.

Technical definition

Defined exactly by the Fisher relation as a multiplicative link, not a subtraction: one plus the nominal rate equals the product of one plus the real rate and one plus inflation. Subtracting inflation drops the cross-term and is acceptable only at low rates. Expected real rates are unobservable, so they are proxied by index-linked bond yields or survey expectations, each with its own bias.

Academic definition

The rate of intertemporal exchange of goods, defined by the Fisher identity linking nominal returns to the expected or realised change in the price level over the same horizon.

Mechanism

Borrowers repay in money whose purchasing power has changed. Inflation therefore transfers value from lender to borrower on nominal contracts, and the real rate is what is left for the lender. Because investment decisions compare a project's real return with the real cost of funds, it is the real rate that governs behaviour, not the posted nominal one.

In practice

Who uses it
Central banks judging whether policy is restrictive, long-horizon savers, project finance teams, and anyone comparing yields across inflation regimes.
Why it matters
A nominal rate alone cannot say whether money is cheap or dear: the same nominal level is tight at low inflation and loose at high inflation.
Limitations
The forward-looking version depends on an inflation expectation you cannot observe, and the realised version is only known after the fact.

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting inflation from the nominal rate at high inflation.
  • Mixing an expected real rate with realised inflation.
  • Using one country's price index to deflate another's rate.

Examples

numeric

A deposit pays 6 percent over a year in which prices rise 4 percent. The exact real return is 1.92 percent, not the 2 percent the subtraction suggests. At 40 percent inflation the same shortcut fails badly.

Given
  • nominal rate i = 6% = 0.06
  • inflation pi = 4% = 0.04
  • same one-year horizon for both
Steps
  1. (1 + 0.06) / (1 + 0.04) = 1.06 / 1.04 = 1.019231
  2. r = 1.019231 - 1 = 0.019231
  3. approximation i - pi = 0.02
exact real rate = 1.92%; linear approximation = 2.00%

Rates are invented for the arithmetic. The approximation error is the product r x pi, which grows with both inputs.

market

Two economies post the same nominal policy rate. In the one with much higher inflation, real rates are deeply negative and borrowing is cheap in real terms, even though the posted number is identical.

Mathematical foundation

(1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + \pi) \;\Longrightarrow\; r = \frac{1 + i}{1 + \pi} - 1 \;\approx\; i - \pi

Variables

iNominal interest rate over the horizondecimal or %
rReal interest rate over the same horizondecimal or %
\piInflation over the same horizon: expected for ex ante, realised for ex postdecimal or %

Procedure

Fix one horizon and use the same horizon for both the rate and the inflation measure. Divide one plus the nominal rate by one plus inflation and subtract one. Keep ex ante and ex post versions separate and name which price index was used.

Worked example

Given
  • i = 0.06
  • pi = 0.04
  • one-year horizon
Steps
  1. 1 + i = 1.06
  2. 1 + pi = 1.04
  3. 1.06 / 1.04 = 1.0192308
  4. r = 0.0192308
r = 1.92% exactly; the subtraction shortcut gives 2.00%

Interpretation

The result is the increase in purchasing power earned, not the increase in currency units. A negative real rate means the lender ends the period able to buy less, even though the account balance grew.

Assumptions

  • The nominal rate and the inflation measure must cover identical periods and the same compounding convention.
  • Which price index is used matters: CPI, HICP and the PCE price index give different inflation numbers for the same economy, and therefore different real rates from the same nominal rate.
  • Ex ante real rates require an inflation expectation, which is unobservable and must be proxied; the choice of proxy changes the answer.
Limits of the formula
  • The linear approximation i minus pi omits the product r x pi and is unsafe at high inflation.
  • Realised real rates can only be computed after the inflation data are published and revised, so they are a historical measure, not a decision input.
