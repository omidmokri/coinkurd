Rebalancing

Simple definition

Trading a portfolio back toward its target weights after prices have moved them. It trims whatever has grown relatively large and tops up whatever has shrunk, to keep the intended risk profile.

Professional definition

The process of returning drifted weights to policy targets, triggered by calendar date, by a threshold breach, or by both. Its primary purpose is controlling risk drift rather than raising return; its side effect is that it mechanically reduces appreciated holdings and adds to depreciated ones.

Technical definition

Weights evolve multiplicatively with returns, so an untouched portfolio becomes progressively concentrated in whatever has risen most, carrying its volatility and factor exposures with it. A rebalancing rule bounds that drift. Calendar rules are simple to administer but ignore the size of the deviation; threshold rules act only when the deviation matters but require continuous monitoring. Whether the rule raises or lowers realised return depends on the return process: it tends to help when prices mean-revert and to hurt when they trend persistently.

Academic definition

A control policy that bounds the deviation of realised portfolio weights from target weights, with the trade-off between tracking accuracy and transaction cost governing the choice of trigger.

Mechanism

Left alone, each weight becomes the normalised product of its initial weight and its cumulative return, so the best performer's share grows without any decision being taken. Rebalancing is the only thing that keeps those weights a choice rather than an outcome. The cost is that every correction crystallises a trade, with spread, fee and, in a taxable account, a realised gain.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone running a portfolio to target weights: funds, advisers, treasury teams, and individuals following a written plan.
Why it matters
It keeps realised risk close to intended risk. Without it the allocation stops being a decision and becomes a record of what has gone up.
Limitations
There is no guaranteed return benefit. Frequent rules raise trading costs and realised tax; wide tolerances allow large risk drift between actions.

Common mistakes

  • Describing rebalancing as a source of return rather than a control on risk drift.
  • Choosing a frequency without costing spread, fees and realised tax.
  • Correcting a 1% drift while a 15% drift sits untouched elsewhere.

Examples

market

A fund rebalances on the first trading day of each quarter and also whenever any class is more than 5 points from target. In a quiet quarter only the calendar rule fires; in a violent one the threshold fires first and the calendar trade is already unnecessary.

numeric

A portfolio rebalanced monthly turns over on average 2% of its value each time, at a round-trip cost of 0.50% of the traded amount. The policy therefore costs about 0.12% of the portfolio a year in trading alone, before any tax.

Given
  • rebalancing frequency: 12 times a year
  • average turnover per rebalance: 2% of portfolio value
  • round-trip cost: 0.50% of traded value (spread plus fee)
Steps
  1. annual turnover = 2% x 12 = 24% of portfolio value
  2. annual trading cost = 24% x 0.50% = 0.12% of portfolio value
about 0.12% of portfolio value per year in trading cost

Quarterly rebalancing of the same portfolio would cost roughly a quarter of that, with correspondingly wider risk drift between dates. Realised tax, where it applies, is additional and can exceed the trading cost; see tax-loss-harvesting.

Mathematical foundation

w_{i,1} = \frac{w_{i,0}\,(1 + R_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{j,0}\,(1 + R_j)}, \qquad T_i = \left(w_i^{*} - w_{i,1}\right) V_1

Variables

w_{i,0}Weight of holding i at the start of the periodfraction of portfolio
R_iTotal return of holding i over the perioddecimal return
w_{i,1}Drifted weight of holding i at the end of the periodfraction of portfolio
w_i^{*}Target (policy) weight of holding ifraction of portfolio
V_1Portfolio value at the end of the periodcurrency
T_iTrade required in holding i, positive to buy and negative to sellcurrency

Procedure

Value each holding at the end of the period, sum to get portfolio value, and divide to get the drifted weights. Compare each drifted weight with its target and with the tolerance band. For every breach, the required trade is target weight minus drifted weight, multiplied by portfolio value. The trades net to zero if no cash enters or leaves.

Worked example

Given
  • target weights: equities 60%, bonds 40%
  • tolerance: plus or minus 5 percentage points
  • period returns: equities +30%, bonds 0%
  • starting values: equities 60, bonds 40
Steps
  1. equity value = 60 x 1.30 = 78; bond value = 40 x 1.00 = 40
  2. portfolio value V1 = 118
  3. drifted equity weight = 78 / 118 = 0.661
  4. drift = 66.1% - 60% = 6.1 points, which exceeds the 5-point tolerance
  5. target equity value = 0.60 x 118 = 70.8
  6. trade = 70.8 - 78 = -7.2 in equities, and +7.2 in bonds
sell 7.2 of equities and buy 7.2 of bonds, restoring 60/40 on a portfolio worth 118

Interpretation

The identity describes how far the portfolio has moved and what trade would undo it. It carries no view on which holding will do better next: the sell is triggered by the weight, not by a forecast.

Assumptions

  • Weights are measured on market values at a single instant; contributions or withdrawals during the period change the arithmetic and should be handled as cash flows.
  • Tolerances can be set in percentage points of the portfolio or as a percentage of the target weight. The two give different trigger levels and are not interchangeable.
  • Costs, taxes and the market impact of the restoring trade are excluded from the identity and must be modelled separately.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity describes drift, not whether correcting it is worthwhile.
  • Any 'rebalancing bonus' is a property of the return series, not of the rule: it appears when prices mean-revert around a common level and reverses when one holding trends persistently higher than the rest.

Commonly confused with

Dollar-Cost Averaging

Dollar-cost averaging is a rule for putting new money in over time. Rebalancing redistributes money already invested. One changes the amount at risk, the other changes only its composition.
