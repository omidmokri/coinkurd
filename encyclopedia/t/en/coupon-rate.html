Coupon rate

Category
Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The percentage of a bond's face amount that the issuer pays out each year. It is fixed when the bond is issued and does not move with the market.

Professional definition

The annual contractual payment expressed as a fraction of face value. It determines the cash the holder receives, not the return the holder earns: once the bond trades away from par, the return is the yield, and the coupon is only one input to it.

Technical definition

A fixed contractual parameter, typically paid in one, two or four instalments per year according to market convention. It is invariant to market rates, so the coupon equals the yield only at a price exactly equal to par. The relation is directional and exact: coupon above yield implies a premium price, coupon below yield implies a discount.

Academic definition

The contractually specified periodic interest payment on a debt security, expressed as an annual percentage of its face amount and invariant to subsequent market conditions.

Mechanism

The coupon is a number written once, in currency per year. Markets cannot change it, so when required yields move, the only thing that can adjust is the price paid for that unchanged stream. This is why a high coupon is not a high return, and why comparing bonds by coupon alone is meaningless.

In practice

Who uses it
Income-focused investors, treasury teams forecasting cash receipts, and anyone reading a bond's terms sheet.
Why it matters
It tells you exactly how much cash arrives and when, which matters for liability matching even when it says little about return.
Limitations
Says nothing about total return, credit quality or reinvestment: a 9% coupon from a weak issuer can return less than a 3% coupon from a strong one.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing bonds by the highest coupon.
  • Confusing the coupon rate with the yield to maturity.
  • Forgetting that coupons must be reinvested at unknown future rates.

Examples

market

Two bonds mature on the same day from the same issuer: one carries a 7% coupon, the other 2%. Their yields are nearly identical, because the 7% bond trades well above par and the 2% bond well below.

numeric

Face 1,000 with a 5% coupon paid semi-annually means 25 twice a year, 50 in total. If the bond is bought at 900, the current yield is 5.56% — higher than the coupon rate, purely because of the price paid.

Given
  • F = 1,000
  • coupon rate c = 5% per year
  • two payments per year
  • purchase price = 900
Steps
  1. annual coupon = 0.05 x 1,000 = 50
  2. each payment = 50 / 2 = 25
  3. current yield = 50 / 900
50 per year in cash; current yield 5.56%

Current yield ignores the pull to par at maturity, so it is not the yield to maturity.

Mathematical foundation

C_{annual} = c \times F, \qquad C_{period} = \frac{c \times F}{m}

Variables

cCoupon rate, an annual contractual percentagedecimal or %
FFace or par amount of the bondcurrency
C_{annual}Total coupon cash per yearcurrency
C_{period}Cash paid on each coupon datecurrency
mCoupon payments per year, by market conventiontimes per year

Procedure

Multiply the coupon rate by the face amount for the annual cash, then divide by the number of coupon dates per year. The coupon rate never changes; only the price and therefore the yield do.

Worked example

Given
  • c = 5%
  • F = 1,000
  • m = 2
Steps
  1. C_annual = 0.05 x 1,000 = 50
  2. C_period = 50 / 2
25 paid every six months

Interpretation

The output is a cash amount, not a return. To judge the investment, compare the price paid against all the cash to come, which is the yield-to-maturity calculation.

Assumptions

  • The coupon rate is fixed. Floating-rate notes reset their coupon to a reference rate and are not described by a single c.
  • m follows the market's convention; the same annual rate paid twice yearly produces a slightly different effective annual amount than paid once.
  • Face value is the contractual base, which may differ from the amount originally raised.
Limits of the formula
  • Carries no information about credit quality or about the price at which the bond can be bought.
  • Current yield, coupon divided by price, is still not the yield to maturity because it ignores the capital movement to par.

Commonly confused with

Yield to maturity

The coupon rate is what the contract pays on face value. Yield to maturity is what the buyer earns on the price actually paid, including the move back to face at maturity.
