Simple definition

How easy and cheap it is to raise money across borders at a given moment. When it is abundant, credit flows to risky places; when it dries up, money goes home.

Professional definition

The ease of financing in international markets, usually proxied by cross-border bank credit, international debt issuance, dollar funding spreads and measures of risk appetite. It is a condition of markets, not a counted quantity, and every proxy for it is partial.

Technical definition

There is no single series called global liquidity; the term covers several distinct things — central-bank balance sheets, the supply of cross-border credit and the price of dollar funding — which correlate in crises and diverge otherwise. Its contraction is transmitted to small open economies through funding spreads, currency depreciation and the retrenchment of cross-border lenders rather than through domestic policy.

Academic definition

A composite notion of international financing ease, operationalised through indicators of cross-border credit aggregates, funding spreads and intermediary risk-bearing capacity, with no canonical measure.

Mechanism

Cross-border credit is supplied by intermediaries whose willingness to take risk moves with their own funding costs and balance-sheet capacity. When funding tightens or volatility rises, they shrink exposures everywhere at once, which is why conditions in unrelated economies tighten together. The dominant funding currency concentrates that channel.

In practice

Who uses it
Emerging-market investors, sovereign and bank treasurers funding in foreign currency, and allocators whose correlations change with the global cycle.
Why it matters
It explains why assets with no economic link sell off together, and why local fundamentals can be overwhelmed by external funding conditions.
Limitations
There is no agreed measure, so any claim about its level depends on the chosen proxy, and the mapping from any proxy to asset prices is unstable.

Common mistakes

  • Equating central-bank balance sheets with global liquidity.
  • Reading one proxy as the quantity itself.
  • Assuming a stable lead time from funding conditions to risk assets.

Examples

market

Spreads on foreign-currency funding widen sharply. Within days, bonds, currencies and equities of unrelated small economies fall together, and local news explains almost none of it.

crypto

Crypto assets have often traded with global risk appetite rather than against it, falling in the same weeks as other risk assets. The relationship is a historical correlation, unstable and not a mechanism.

Commonly confused with

Liquidity

Market liquidity is about the cost of trading one asset now. Global liquidity is about the availability and price of funding across borders. One is microstructure, the other a financing condition.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

There is no canonical measure of global liquidity, and the entry says so rather than adopting one; central-bank balance sheets, cross-border credit aggregates and funding spreads are named as distinct proxies that can diverge. No index, level or quantitative relationship to asset prices is quoted, and the crypto correlation is presented strictly as an unstable historical association with no mechanism claimed. The Persian headword نقدینگی جهانی is the standard phrase in that language and is deliberately kept distinct from نقدشوندگی, the project lexicon's wording for market liquidity. The Kurdish headword is a proposal by this project and needs native-expert review.
