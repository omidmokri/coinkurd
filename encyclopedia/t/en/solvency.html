Solvency

Level
Professional

Simple definition

Being able to cover everything you owe, not just this month's bills. A business is solvent when its assets are worth more than its debts.

Professional definition

The condition of holding assets sufficient to meet total obligations as they fall due. Distinct from liquidity: a solvent firm can still fail if its assets cannot be turned into cash in time, and an illiquid firm may be perfectly solvent.

Technical definition

Assessed on two separate tests that can disagree. The balance-sheet test asks whether assets exceed liabilities at some measurement basis; the cash-flow test asks whether obligations can be paid as they come due. Both depend on valuation, and asset values are least reliable exactly when the question is being asked, which is why regulatory capital frameworks impose buffers rather than relying on the identity alone.

Academic definition

The state in which an entity's assets, appropriately valued, suffice to discharge its total liabilities, assessed by balance-sheet or cash-flow criteria.

Mechanism

Equity is the residual: assets minus liabilities. Losses consume it from the top, and when it reaches zero the claims of creditors exceed what the assets can cover. Because assets are marked using estimates, insolvency is often recognised long after it has economically occurred.

In practice

Who uses it
Credit committees, auditors forming going-concern judgements, directors with statutory duties, and bondholders assessing downside.
Why it matters
It separates a timing problem, which financing can bridge, from a value problem, which it cannot.
Limitations
Depends entirely on how assets are valued; book, market and liquidation values can give three different verdicts on the same entity on the same day.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a cash squeeze with insolvency.
  • Reading positive book equity as proof of solvency.
  • Ignoring off-balance-sheet and contingent obligations.

Examples

market

A lender with long-dated loans funded by short-term deposits can be solvent on paper and still fail when depositors withdraw faster than loans can be sold. The failure is liquidity first, and only then a solvency question.

numeric

Assets 5,000,000 and liabilities 4,600,000 leave equity of 400,000, an equity ratio of 8%. A 10% write-down of assets erases the equity entirely and leaves creditors short.

Given
  • assets A = 5,000,000
  • liabilities L = 4,600,000
  • asset write-down scenario = 10%
Steps
  1. E = 5,000,000 - 4,600,000 = 400,000
  2. equity ratio = 400,000 / 5,000,000 = 8%
  3. after write-down: 4,500,000 - 4,600,000 = -100,000
equity 400,000 becomes negative 100,000 after a 10% write-down

Illustrative. Thin equity means solvency depends on the accuracy of asset valuations.

Mathematical foundation

A = L + E \;\Longrightarrow\; E = A - L, \qquad \text{equity ratio} = \frac{E}{A}

Variables

ATotal assets at the chosen measurement basiscurrency
LTotal liabilities, including contingent ones where recognisedcurrency
EEquity: the residual claim after liabilitiescurrency

Procedure

State the measurement basis first, then subtract liabilities from assets. Express the residual as a share of assets to see how large a valuation error the entity can absorb before equity is gone.

Worked example

Given
  • A = 5,000,000
  • L = 4,600,000
Steps
  1. E = 5,000,000 - 4,600,000
  2. E / A = 400,000 / 5,000,000
E = 400,000; equity ratio 8%

Interpretation

The accounting identity always holds by construction; it is the valuation of A that decides whether the entity is really solvent. A thin equity ratio means small valuation errors flip the verdict.

Assumptions

  • The identity is definitional and cannot be violated; it carries no information on its own without a stated valuation basis.
  • Book, fair and liquidation values give different equity figures for the same entity, and the gap widens in stress.
  • Contingent liabilities, guarantees and off-balance-sheet exposures may not appear in L at all.
Limits of the formula
  • Balance-sheet solvency and cash-flow solvency can disagree, and most failures begin with the second.
  • Regulatory solvency for banks and insurers is defined by capital rules that differ from this general identity and vary by jurisdiction.

Commonly confused with

Liquidity

Liquidity is about being able to pay now; solvency is about having enough value overall. A solvent entity can be illiquid, and that is enough to end it.
