Schelling Point

Simple definition

The obvious choice that people converge on without being able to talk first, because each expects the others to think it is obvious too.

Professional definition

A solution that participants in a coordination problem select because it is salient to all of them, not because it is better. Salience can come from history, roundness, a published default or a shared story, and it does the work that communication would otherwise do.

Technical definition

A focal profile in a coordination game with multiple equilibria, selected by features outside the payoff structure. Nothing in the equilibrium condition can distinguish the candidates, so selection depends on common knowledge of what the parties regard as conspicuous. Salience is therefore a property of the participants and their context rather than of the game, which makes focal points real but not derivable.

Academic definition

A conspicuous equilibrium in a multiple-equilibrium coordination problem, selected through mutually recognised salience rather than through payoff dominance.

Mechanism

When several outcomes are equally stable, something must break the tie, and the only thing available without communication is what each party believes the others will find obvious. Beliefs about obviousness are self-reinforcing: expecting others to pick the salient option makes picking it correct. That is why defaults, round numbers and long-standing conventions carry far more weight than their payoffs justify.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol and standard designers, people writing defaults into systems, and anyone analysing why a market settles on one convention over equally workable alternatives.
Why it matters
It explains the stickiness of conventions and defaults: once a focal choice is established, changing it needs coordinated movement rather than a better argument.
Limitations
Salience is not derivable from the model and is not stable across cultures or communities, so a focal point in one group may not exist in another.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a focal choice as the optimal one.
  • Assuming salience transfers to a different community unchanged.
  • Expecting coordination to persist after the salient feature is removed.

Examples

everyday

Two people lose each other in a large station with no way to call. Both head for the main clock, not because it is the best place to wait but because each expects the other to pick the one spot everybody knows.

crypto

A public chain's rule that participants treat the longest valid chain as the correct one is focal: any other rule could in principle coordinate the network, but only one needs to be common knowledge for everyone to converge on the same history.

Commonly confused with

Support and Resistance

A focal point explains why participants coordinate on the same reference, such as a round number. It does not predict that price will stop there; a chart level is a claim about future behaviour, which the concept of salience does not supply.
