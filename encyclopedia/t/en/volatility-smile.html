Volatility Smile

Simple definition

Options on the same asset and the same expiry imply different volatilities at different strikes. Plotted against the strike, the line curves instead of running flat.

Professional definition

The observed pattern of implied volatility as a function of strike for one underlying and one maturity. A single constant volatility is what the Black-Scholes model assumes, so a non-flat line is a direct contradiction of the model being inverted to produce it.

Technical definition

Implied volatility read as a surface over strike and maturity. In index markets the shape has typically been a one-sided skew, with lower strikes implying higher volatility; in currency and crypto markets a more symmetric smile is common. The shape is attributed to return distributions with fatter tails than lognormal, to jumps, to volatility that is itself stochastic, and to persistent demand for downside protection. None of these is the model's own explanation, because the model admits no such variation.

Academic definition

The empirically observed dependence of Black-Scholes implied volatility on strike and maturity, inconsistent with the constant-volatility assumption of the model used to define it.

Mechanism

Market makers quote premiums for real distributions and then report those premiums in Black-Scholes volatility units, because the map between premium and implied volatility is one-to-one and convenient. If the real distribution has fatter tails or a skew, the single number needed to reproduce each premium must differ by strike. The curve is therefore the footprint of everything the model left out.

In practice

Who uses it
Volatility traders quoting and hedging a surface, model validation teams, and anyone pricing a strike away from the money.
Why it matters
It is the reason a single implied-volatility number cannot price a chain, and the reason desks interpolate a surface instead of using one input.
Limitations
The surface is built from quotes, so its wings inherit every wide spread and stale mark in the illiquid strikes where the shape is most pronounced.

Common mistakes

  • Using one at-the-money implied volatility to value every strike in a chain.
  • Reading the skew as a forecast of direction rather than a price of protection.
  • Comparing smiles across venues with different expiry and settlement conventions.

Examples

market

Three strikes, one expiry, one underlying: the low strike implies the highest volatility, the middle one the lowest, the high strike something in between. Under a model with one volatility this picture cannot happen, yet it is the normal state of an option chain.

numeric

Illustrative chain: the 90 strike implies 68%, the 100 strike 55% and the 110 strike 60%. The spread between wing and centre is 13 points. A single-volatility model fitted to the centre misprices both wings.

Given
  • one underlying, one expiry
  • implied volatility at K = 90: 68%
  • at K = 100: 55%
  • at K = 110: 60%
Steps
  1. 68 - 55 = 13 points of skew on the low side
  2. 60 - 55 = 5 points on the high side
  3. Black-Scholes requires all three to be equal
a non-flat curve, which the model that produced the numbers forbids

The volatility figures are illustrative and chosen to show the shape. They are not quotes from any market or date.

Procedure

Invert the pricing model separately at every listed strike for one maturity, then plot the resulting volatilities against strike or against log-moneyness. Repeat for each maturity to obtain a surface. The model used for the inversion must be named, because the shape is a property of the pair of model and market, not of the market alone.

Worked example

Given

  • same underlying and same expiry
Steps
  1. check whether the three values are equal
  2. they are not: the partial derivative with respect to strike is non-zero
the constant-volatility assumption is violated by the market's own quotes

Interpretation

The smile is best read as an admission. The market quotes in Black-Scholes units because the units are convenient and comparable, while the shape of the curve records precisely how far the market's view of the distribution departs from the model's. The model survives as a quoting language, not as a description of returns.

Assumptions

  • A smile only exists relative to a named model. Inverting a different model on the same premiums produces a different surface, and a model that already allows varying volatility produces no smile at all in these coordinates.
  • The shape depends on the coordinate chosen: strike, moneyness, log-moneyness and delta each stretch the picture differently, and a skew in one can look like a smile in another.
  • Observed shapes differ by market and over time. Index markets have typically shown a one-sided skew and currency markets a more symmetric smile, but no shape is a constant of nature and none is stated here as current.
  • Wing quotes come from the least liquid strikes, so part of any observed curvature is spread and marking practice rather than distributional belief.
Limits of the formula
  • The smile documents an inconsistency; it does not by itself say which extended model is right. Local-volatility, stochastic-volatility and jump models all reproduce smiles with different dynamics and different hedges.
  • The skew is a price, not a forecast. A steep downside skew records what protection costs, and inferring a directional prediction from it is not supported.
  • A surface fitted to quotes can be arbitrage-free or not; interpolation between strikes without constraints can create implied distributions with negative probabilities.

Commonly confused with

Volatility Clustering

Clustering is a time-series property of realised volatility: calm follows calm, storms follow storms. The smile is a cross-sectional property of option prices at a single instant.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The observation itself — that implied volatility varies by strike and so contradicts the constant-volatility assumption of the model used to compute it — is consensus and is recorded as such. Which extended model best accounts for the shape is not consensus and no choice is endorsed here. The Kurdish headword پێکەنینی لەرزۆکی is this project's coinage, built on the lexicon's proposed wording for volatility, and needs native-expert review; the Persian لبخند نوسان is in use but چولگی نوسان circulates for the one-sided case. All volatility figures in the entry are illustrative and carry no date or venue, in line with the rule against live market figures.
