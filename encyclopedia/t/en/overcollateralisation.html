Overcollateralisation

Simple definition

Locking up collateral worth more than you borrow. The extra value is the cushion that lets the lender get its money back even if the collateral falls in price.

Professional definition

Posting collateral whose market value exceeds the debt by a required margin. In DeFi it is the substitute for credit assessment: a protocol that cannot judge a borrower prices only the collateral, and the surplus is sized to survive a price fall plus the cost of selling into it.

Technical definition

The buffer must cover three things at once: the price move between the last oracle update and the sale, the market impact of selling the collateral, and the incentive paid to whoever executes the liquidation. That is why the required surplus rises with the collateral's volatility and thinness, and why the same debt needs much more of a volatile token than of a pegged one. The borrower is a residual claimant on the buffer, not a counterparty whose promise matters.

Academic definition

A credit arrangement in which pledged collateral value exceeds the obligation by a margin calibrated to price volatility, liquidation cost and feed latency, substituting asset coverage for borrower-specific credit information.

Mechanism

Without identity, enforcement or income data, a protocol has nothing to lend against but an asset it can seize and sell automatically. The surplus exists because seizure is not instant and not free: a feed updates at intervals, a sale moves the price, and a liquidator must be paid to act. Every one of those frictions is covered out of the borrower's buffer, never the lender's.

In practice

Who uses it
Borrowers choosing how much to deposit, protocol risk teams calibrating parameters, and stablecoin issuers backing a peg with volatile reserves.
Why it matters
It makes lending possible without knowing anything about the borrower, and it is what keeps the lender whole in an ordinary market fall.
Limitations
It is capital-inefficient by design and only protects against price risk. It does nothing about a wrong oracle, a frozen chain or a collateral asset that stops trading.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the minimum ratio as a place to sit rather than a line to stay away from.
  • Using a thinly traded token as collateral because the stated ratio allows it.
  • Assuming a buffer sized in calm conditions holds through a gap move.

Examples

numeric

A borrower deposits 10 units of collateral at a price of 2,000 and borrows 8,000. At a minimum ratio of 1.25 the debt needs only 10,000 of collateral, so 10,000 is surplus and the collateral can fall by half before the floor is reached.

Given
  • collateral quantity Q = 10 units
  • collateral price P = 2,000
  • debt D = 8,000
  • minimum collateral ratio m = 1.25 (equivalently a maximum loan-to-value of 80%)
Steps
  1. collateral value = 10 x 2,000 = 20,000
  2. required collateral = 1.25 x 8,000 = 10,000
  3. surplus = 20,000 - 10,000 = 10,000
  4. tolerated price fall = 1 - (m x D) / (Q x P) = 1 - 10,000 / 20,000 = 50%
A 50% fall in the collateral price takes the position to its minimum ratio.

Parameters m and the maximum loan-to-value are protocol choices and differ by collateral asset; 1.25 here is illustrative.

market

The same 8,000 of debt is treated very differently depending on what backs it: a pegged-asset collateral may be allowed a thin buffer, while a volatile small-cap token may be given a much larger one or refused as collateral entirely.

Mathematical foundation

C \;=\; Q\,P \;\ge\; m\,D, \qquad \text{surplus} = QP - mD, \qquad \text{tolerated fall} = 1 - \frac{mD}{QP}

Procedure

Value the collateral at the feed price, multiply the debt by the required minimum ratio, and compare. The difference is the surplus. To express the same buffer as resilience, divide the required collateral by the current collateral value and subtract from one: that is the fraction the collateral price can lose before the position sits exactly at the floor.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 10
  • P = 2,000
  • D = 8,000
  • m = 1.25
Steps
  1. C = 10 x 2,000 = 20,000
  2. m x D = 1.25 x 8,000 = 10,000
  3. surplus = 20,000 - 10,000 = 10,000
  4. tolerated fall = 1 - 10,000 / 20,000 = 0.50
Surplus 10,000; the collateral price can fall 50% before the minimum ratio binds.

Interpretation

The surplus is not idle capital in the lender's eyes; it is the whole credit decision. Read as a tolerated fall it becomes a risk statement the borrower can act on, and it shrinks as interest accrues even if the price never moves.

Assumptions

  • A single collateral asset and a single debt asset, both valued at the protocol's own feed. Multi-asset positions weight each collateral by its own parameter.
  • The minimum ratio m is the protocol's liquidation parameter, which is usually stricter than the ratio required to open the position.
  • Interest accrues to D over time, so the buffer erodes without any price move.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity is arithmetic: it cannot tell whether m is set high enough for the asset in question, which is a risk-modelling judgement and differs between protocols.
  • A buffer calibrated on historical volatility understates the cost of a gap move, when the sale happens far below the last feed price.
  • Overcollateralisation addresses price risk only, not contract, oracle, bridge or governance failure.
