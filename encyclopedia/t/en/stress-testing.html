Simple definition

Working out what a specific bad event would cost you, instead of asking how likely bad events are. You choose the shock, then measure the damage.

Professional definition

Evaluation of a portfolio under specified severe but plausible conditions. It complements probabilistic measures by dropping the probability question entirely: the scenario is imposed, and the output is the loss, the margin call or the funding gap it would produce.

Technical definition

Four standard forms. Historical replay applies the realised moves of a past episode to the current book. Hypothetical scenarios impose a constructed set of shocks across risk factors with explicit correlation assumptions. Sensitivity testing moves one factor at a time to locate the largest exposures. Reverse stress testing inverts the exercise: it starts from a defined failure, such as breaching a margin threshold, and solves for the smallest set of moves that would cause it.

Academic definition

The deterministic evaluation of portfolio outcomes under exogenously specified adverse states of the world, used as a complement to distribution-based risk measures that depend on estimated parameters.

Mechanism

Probabilistic measures inherit every weakness of their estimated parameters, and those parameters are estimated on data that by definition excludes the event in question. A stress test sidesteps the estimation problem by making the event an assumption. The cost of that is that the result is only as relevant as the scenario, and the scenario is written by someone.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk committees and regulators at institutional scale, and individually any trader who wants to know what a gap, an exchange outage or a funding spike would do to a levered book.
Why it matters
It produces an answer that does not depend on a volatility or correlation estimate, and reverse testing names the move that would actually break the account.
Limitations
Scenario selection is a judgement, and the scenario nobody wrote is the one that happens. Results are also sensitive to the correlation assumptions inside the scenario.

Common mistakes

  • Running only scenarios the book is already positioned for.
  • Shocking prices while leaving liquidity and funding unchanged.
  • Treating a passed stress test as a bound on possible loss.

Examples

numeric

A reverse test on a levered long: equity 10,000, position notional 50,000, maintenance requirement 5 percent. The adverse move that exhausts equity down to the requirement is about 15 percent, so the scenario to plan for is named rather than guessed.

Given
  • equity = 10,000
  • position notional = 50,000, so leverage is 5x
  • maintenance requirement = 5 percent of notional
Steps
  1. required equity at the threshold = 0.05 x 50,000 = 2,500
  2. equity available to lose = 10,000 - 2,500 = 7,500
  3. adverse price move that loses 7,500 on 50,000 notional = 7,500 / 50,000 = 0.15
  4. ignoring the reduction in notional as price falls, which makes the true threshold slightly closer
A move of roughly 15 percent against the position reaches the maintenance threshold.

This is the simplified case with notional held fixed. A venue computing the requirement on the falling mark price triggers slightly earlier, and funding accrual brings it closer again.

market

A historical replay applies a past crash episode's daily moves to today's positions. A hypothetical scenario instead assumes all risk assets fall together while the hedge instrument becomes untradeable, which no historical window may contain.

Commonly confused with

Monte Carlo Simulation

Monte Carlo draws many paths from an assumed distribution and reports a distribution of outcomes. A stress test imposes one specified state of the world and reports a single outcome. One estimates probability, the other deliberately refuses to.
