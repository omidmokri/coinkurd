Simple definition

Two single-candle shapes with a small body and one long wick: the hammer carries the wick below, the shooting star carries it above.

Professional definition

Single-candle shapes defined by proportion: a small body at one end of the range, a shadow on the other side conventionally at least twice the body, little shadow behind. The same shape is a hammer after a decline and a hanging man after an advance; inverted, a shooting star after an advance and an inverted hammer after a decline.

Technical definition

The geometry does not identify the pattern; location does. One candle is a hammer or a hanging man depending only on the preceding trend, so any code must define preceding trend before it can count either. The proportional rule — shadow at least twice the body, opposite shadow small — is convention, with no agreed number for small, which is why detection counts differ widely between implementations.

Academic definition

Single-interval candlestick shapes characterised by a short body at one extreme of the range and a dominant shadow at the other, classified by the direction of the preceding move.

Mechanism

A long lower shadow with the close back near the open says price was pushed down inside the interval and then bought back to where it started. The candle does not say who bought, how much size it took, or whether it continues. It records that an excursion was rejected within one interval, no more. The rejection is real; the inference drawn from it is the contested part.

In practice

Who uses it
Candlestick traders timing an entry at a level already drawn, and developers who want a one-bar rejection filter.
Why it matters
It isolates intervals in which an excursion was fully retraced, which pairs naturally with a level the trader had identified beforehand.
Limitations
The proportions are conventions, the name depends entirely on the prior trend, published out-of-sample evidence is weak, and measured performance follows the coded definition rather than the shape.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a shape a hammer without checking that a decline preceded it.
  • Using one fixed shadow-to-body ratio across very different volatility regimes.
  • Treating a hammer in mid-range as if it formed at support.

Examples

chart

Price falls to a level drawn weeks earlier, prints a candle with a long lower shadow and closes near its open. The shape is identical to one printed at the top of the previous advance, where it was named a hanging man instead.

numeric

A candle opens 100, closes 101, reaches 101.5 and falls to 94. The lower shadow is six times the body and the upper shadow is half of it, so the proportions qualify. Whether it is a hammer or a hanging man still depends on what came before.

Given
  • open = 100
  • close = 101
  • high = 101.5
  • low = 94
  • rule: lower shadow at least twice the body, upper shadow no larger than the body
Steps
  1. body = |101 - 100| = 1
  2. lower shadow = min(open, close) - low = 100 - 94 = 6
  3. upper shadow = high - max(open, close) = 101.5 - 101 = 0.5
  4. 6 >= 2 x 1 and 0.5 <= 1, so the proportions pass
The proportions qualify; the name still requires the preceding trend to be defined.

Tightening the rule to three times the body, or allowing a larger upper shadow, changes the number of candidates in any dataset substantially.

Commonly confused with

Doji

A doji has no real body and wicks on both sides. A hammer or shooting star has a definite small body at one end of the range and one dominant wick.
