Simple definition

Selling a holding that is below what was paid for it so the loss is recorded for tax, while keeping comparable exposure to the market through a different holding.

Professional definition

Realising an unrealised loss so it can offset realised gains or, where the rules permit, other income, then re-establishing exposure with an asset that is not substantially identical. It creates no value on its own: the replacement carries a lower cost basis, so part of the gain is deferral rather than reduction.

Technical definition

The benefit has two components: the time value of the tax deferred, and any gap between the rate at which the loss is used now and the rate at which the enlarged future gain is taxed. Because the replacement resets a lower basis, a later sale produces a bigger taxable gain, converting a current deduction into a future liability. Rules on what counts as repurchasing the same asset, on offsetting and carrying forward losses, and on whether digital assets fall inside those rules differ by jurisdiction.

Academic definition

The deliberate realisation of capital losses to offset taxable gains, whose value derives from deferral and from inter-temporal rate differences, net of transaction costs and of the basis reduction on the replacement holding.

Mechanism

Tax is charged on realised amounts, not on paper ones, so the timing of a sale is itself a decision with a cash consequence. Harvesting moves a deduction forward and a liability back, and keeps market exposure by substituting a similar but not identical holding. If the relevant rules treat the substitute as the same asset, the loss is disallowed and only the transaction costs remain.

In practice

Who uses it
Taxable investors and their advisers, in jurisdictions where a realised loss can be set against a realised gain.
Why it matters
It turns an unrealised loss into a usable deduction now without giving up market exposure, provided the replacement holding is genuinely different.
Limitations
This is tax-law dependent and is not advice. Whether a loss is allowable, what counts as a substantially identical repurchase, and how digital assets are treated all vary by jurisdiction and change.

Common mistakes

  • Repurchasing the same asset inside a disallowance window and losing the deduction.
  • Ignoring that the lower replacement basis enlarges the future taxable gain.
  • Harvesting a loss worth less in tax than the trading costs it incurs.

Examples

numeric

A 10,000 loss is realised and offsets a 10,000 realised gain taxed at an assumed 20%, saving 2,000 now. The replacement's basis is 10,000 lower, so a later sale carries a 10,000 larger gain: the 2,000 is deferred, not removed.

Given
  • harvested realised loss: 10,000
  • offsetting realised gain in the same year: 10,000
  • assumed tax rate on that gain: 20%
  • replacement bought at the lower price, so its basis is 10,000 lower
Steps
  1. tax saved this year = 10,000 x 20% = 2,000
  2. future taxable gain is larger by 10,000
  3. if the future rate is also 20%, future tax rises by 2,000
2,000 of tax deferred rather than eliminated, plus whatever the deferral itself is worth over the interval

Rates, offset rules, carry-forward limits and holding-period distinctions are jurisdiction-specific. The figures illustrate the mechanism only and are not a calculation of anyone's tax.

crypto

In the United States the tax authority treats digital assets as property, so a disposal is a taxable event and a loss is realised when the asset is disposed of. Whether the securities wash-sale rule reaches digital assets has been debated and is a question of law.

Commonly confused with

Rebalancing

Rebalancing is triggered by weights drifting from target. Harvesting is triggered by an unrealised loss and the tax calendar. The two can ask for opposite trades in the same position, and the conflict has to be resolved by a written rule.
