Simple interest

ENGLISHSimple interestreference
فارسیبهرهٔ سادهEstablished equivalent
کوردیسووی سادەRecommended equivalent
Category
Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Interest paid only on the original amount, never on interest already earned. Ten percent on 1,000 is 100 every year, year after year.

Professional definition

Interest accrued as a constant fraction of the original principal per period, with no reinvestment of past interest. Common in short-dated money-market conventions, discount instruments and some consumer loan quotes, where it understates the effective cost relative to compounding.

Technical definition

A linear accrual rule: the balance grows arithmetically rather than geometrically, so interest per period is invariant to elapsed time. Over one period simple and compound accrual coincide; beyond that, simple accrual falls behind by the amount of the omitted interest-on-interest, and the gap grows with both the rate and the horizon.

Academic definition

An accrual convention under which interest is computed solely on initial principal, producing a linear rather than exponential growth path for the outstanding balance.

Mechanism

Because past interest is never added to the base, the base never changes, so each period contributes the same amount. All the difference between simple and compound accrual comes from one omitted step: paying a rate on the interest that has already been credited.

In practice

Who uses it
Borrowers reading an instalment quote, money-market desks using day-count conventions, and anyone checking a lender's advertised rate.
Why it matters
Quoting a loan on a simple basis makes it look cheaper than the same cash flows priced with compounding, so comparing like with like requires converting.
Limitations
Rarely the right model for anything held longer than a period; it also says nothing about fees, which often dominate small short loans.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a simple-interest quote directly with a compound annual rate.
  • Ignoring the day-count convention in short-dated calculations.
  • Assuming simple interest means low cost.

Examples

everyday

A lender quotes 'only 12% flat' on a two-year loan of 10,000: 1,200 interest each year, 2,400 in total. A compounding lender charging the same 12% would collect 2,544 over the same two years.

numeric

Principal 10,000 at 12% for two years: simple interest is 2,400 and the balance 12,400. Compounded annually the balance is 12,544, so the omitted interest-on-interest is 144.

Given
  • P = 10,000
  • r = 12% per year
  • t = 2 years
Steps
  1. simple: I = 10,000 x 0.12 x 2 = 2,400
  2. compound: 10,000 x 1.12^2 = 12,544
  3. difference = 12,544 - 12,400
I = 2,400 simple; 144 less than annual compounding

The two conventions agree over exactly one period and diverge after that.

Mathematical foundation

I = P \times r \times t, \qquad A = P(1 + rt)

Variables

POriginal principal, never updatedcurrency

Procedure

Express the rate and the term in the same time unit. Multiply principal by rate by term for the interest, and add the principal for the amount due. For part-periods, apply the stated day-count convention rather than assuming 365.

Worked example

Given
  • P = 10,000
  • r = 12% per year
  • t = 2 years
Steps
  1. I = 10,000 x 0.12 x 2
  2. A = 10,000 + 2,400
I = 2,400 and A = 12,400

Interpretation

The interest figure is proportional to time, so halving the term halves the cost exactly. That proportionality is the signature of simple accrual and does not hold once interest compounds.

Assumptions

  • No interest is reinvested or capitalised during the term.
  • r and t share a time unit; a monthly rate with a year count is the most frequent source of error.
  • Day-count conventions (actual/360, actual/365 and others) change short-dated results and must be stated.
Limits of the formula
  • Understates the true cost of any multi-period borrowing relative to compounding, which is why advertised 'flat' rates are not comparable with annual effective rates.
  • Says nothing about fees, insurance or penalties, which can exceed the interest on small short loans.

Compounding versus Simple Returns
