Simple definition

A loss figure with a confidence attached: the amount a portfolio should not lose on a normal day, where normal means all but the worst one day in a hundred.

Professional definition

The loss threshold that a portfolio is expected to exceed only with a stated probability over a stated horizon. Quoting it requires all three pieces: horizon, confidence level and currency amount, and changing any one of them changes the number.

Technical definition

A quantile of the loss distribution, estimated parametrically from a volatility assumption, historically from an empirical sample, or by simulation. It is not a worst case and not coherent as a risk measure, since it is not sub-additive in general: the VaR of a combined book can exceed the sum of its parts, which is why expected shortfall replaced it in several regulatory frameworks.

Academic definition

The alpha-quantile of the loss distribution of a portfolio over a fixed horizon, conditional on a specified estimation method and distributional assumption.

Mechanism

Parametric VaR works by assuming a shape for the return distribution, so one volatility number plus a quantile multiplier produces a loss figure. That is also its defect: the multiplier is only right if the shape is right, and real return distributions have fatter tails than the normal, so the quantile sits further out than the model places it.

In practice

Who uses it
Bank and fund risk functions, regulators reading capital reports, and any desk with a formal loss limit expressed as a quantile.
Why it matters
It compresses a whole book into one comparable currency figure, which makes limits, aggregation and reporting tractable across desks and asset classes.
Limitations
It is silent about everything past the quantile. It is not a maximum, it depends heavily on the estimation window, and it understates tail risk badly in crypto.

Common mistakes

  • Treating VaR as a worst case rather than a threshold.
  • Comparing VaR figures computed at different horizons or confidences.
  • Estimating VaR from a calm sample and applying it through a stress.

Examples

market

A desk reports a one-day VaR at 99 percent confidence of two percent of book value. In a week where losses exceed it on three consecutive days, nothing was violated by the model; it only ever promised a frequency, not a bound.

numeric

A one-million portfolio with two percent daily volatility, at 99 percent confidence over one day, gives a parametric VaR near 46,500. The same book over ten days scales by the square root of ten to roughly 147,000, if returns are independent.

Given
  • portfolio value V = 1,000,000 (account currency)
  • daily return volatility sigma = 0.02
  • confidence 99 percent, so z = 2.326
  • expected return term set to zero
  • horizon h = 1 day, then h = 10 days
Steps
  1. one-day VaR = 2.326 x 0.02 x 1,000,000 = 46,520
  2. ten-day scaling factor = sqrt(10) = 3.1623
  3. ten-day sigma = 0.02 x 3.1623 = 0.063246
  4. ten-day VaR = 2.326 x 0.063246 x 1,000,000 = 147,109
one-day 99 percent VaR of about 46,520, ten-day about 147,100

The square-root-of-time scaling assumes independent, identically distributed returns. Crypto returns cluster in volatility, so the ten-day figure is optimistic. Nothing here says what happens on the one day in a hundred that breaches the threshold.

Mathematical foundation

\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha,h} = V \left( z_{\alpha}\,\sigma\sqrt{h} - \mu h \right)

Variables

Procedure

Estimate return volatility over a chosen window and state the horizon and confidence. Scale volatility to the horizon by multiplying by the square root of the number of periods. Multiply by the quantile for the confidence level, then by portfolio value. Subtract the expected return over the horizon if the mean term is being carried.

Worked example

Given
  • V = 1,000,000
  • sigma = 0.02 per day
  • h = 1 day
  • alpha = 99 percent, z = 2.326
  • mu = 0
Steps
  1. sigma x sqrt(h) = 0.02 x 1 = 0.02
  2. z x 0.02 = 2.326 x 0.02 = 0.04652
  3. VaR = 0.04652 x 1,000,000
one-day 99 percent VaR = 46,520 account currency

Interpretation

The number is a threshold with a frequency, read as: on about one day in a hundred the loss should be larger than this. It is not a maximum and carries no information about how much larger. Expected shortfall, the average loss given that the threshold is breached, is the standard answer to exactly that gap.

Assumptions

  • The parametric form assumes a normal return distribution. Real series, and crypto series in particular, have fatter tails, so this form understates the quantile.
  • Square-root-of-time scaling assumes independent and identically distributed returns with no volatility clustering and no autocorrelation.
  • Volatility is estimated from a finite window and is therefore itself an estimate, with the window choice materially changing the result.
  • Historical VaR replaces the distributional assumption with the sample, but then cannot produce a loss larger than the worst one in the sample, which is a different way of understating the tail.
  • Linear exposures. Portfolios with options need a delta-gamma or simulation approach rather than this closed form.
Limits of the formula
  • It says nothing whatsoever about the size of losses beyond the quantile; two books with identical VaR can have very different tails.
  • It is not sub-additive in general, so VaR does not aggregate cleanly across desks and can penalise diversification; expected shortfall is coherent and is used for this reason.
  • In crypto, correlated liquidations, venue outages and twenty-four-hour trading all break the assumptions behind the estimate at exactly the moment it matters.

Commonly confused with

Drawdown

VaR is a modelled quantile for a future horizon. Drawdown is a measured fall already recorded on one path. A VaR breach is expected at its stated frequency; a drawdown is simply what happened.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The parametric formula and the quantile multipliers are standard, but estimation choices are not: window length, whether the mean term is carried, and parametric versus historical versus simulated estimation all give different numbers for the same book, and practitioners disagree about which is appropriate. The claim that historical VaR understates crypto tail risk is a judgement supported by the general literature on fat tails and the BIS discussion of crypto leverage, not a precise measured result.
