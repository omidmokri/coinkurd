Dusting Attack

Simple definition

Tiny amounts sent to many addresses, so that when you later spend them together the sender can see those addresses belong to one person.

Professional definition

A privacy attack rather than a theft. The attacker spreads economically negligible amounts across addresses and then watches the chain: if several dusted addresses appear as inputs to one transaction, they are probably controlled by one entity, which links a pseudonymous cluster that can then be followed with off-chain data.

Technical definition

It exploits the common-input-ownership heuristic of UTXO chains, where inputs spent together usually belong to one owner; the dust is bait for that heuristic. The payoff is clustering and then targeting — extortion, tailored phishing, physical risk — not a direct loss. The defence is entirely user-side: leave the dust unspent, use coin control so it is never selected as an input, and never interact with any token or message that arrives with it, because the follow-up is usually a phishing or approval request.

Academic definition

A deanonymisation technique that seeds negligible outputs across addresses to induce input aggregation, exploiting common-input-ownership heuristics to link addresses to a single entity.

Mechanism

Public ledgers are pseudonymous, not anonymous: every spend reveals which outputs one owner could control. Dust costs the attacker almost nothing and turns the owner's own convenience — consolidating small balances — into a linkage signal. The information never expires, which is why unspent dust is harmless and spent dust is not.

In practice

Who uses it
Privacy-conscious holders, compliance and analytics teams, and anyone holding on a chain that spends discrete outputs.
Why it matters
Knowing why dust appears prevents the two harmful responses: consolidating it, or engaging with the token or note attached to it.
Limitations
The heuristic is probabilistic, so a cluster can be wrong; and on account-based chains the dust is more often a lure toward a phishing or approval request than a clustering tool.

Common mistakes

  • Sweeping dust into a consolidating transaction with other funds.
  • Interacting with an unknown token that arrived unsolicited.
  • Reading a small unexplained credit as a gift or an airdrop.

Examples

numeric

Five addresses each receive 500 satoshi. A later spend uses all five as inputs. For the cost of 2,500 satoshi plus fees, the attacker has linked five addresses into one cluster, permanently.

Given
  • Dust per address = 500 satoshi
  • Addresses dusted = 5
  • All five later appear as inputs to one transaction
Steps
  1. Attacker outlay = 5 x 500 = 2,500 satoshi, plus network fees
  2. Addresses linked by the common-input heuristic = 5
Five addresses clustered for an outlay of 2,500 satoshi plus fees

The linkage is an inference, not a proof: shared-input transactions can also be built by several parties. The asymmetry is what matters — the attacker's cost is trivial and the owner's loss of privacy is not reversible.

security

On an account-based chain the same tactic arrives as an unknown token with a name inviting the holder to claim or verify something. The dust is the hook; the site it leads to is the attack.

Commonly confused with

Address Poisoning

Dusting links addresses to one owner. Address poisoning aims to misdirect a future payment. Both arrive as unsolicited transfers, with different goals.

Commonly confused with

Airdrop

An airdrop is a distribution the project announces. Unsolicited dust is not an airdrop, and treating it as one is how the follow-up succeeds.
