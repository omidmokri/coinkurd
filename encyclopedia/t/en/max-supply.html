Simple definition

The largest number of units a protocol will ever create, if its rules never change. Bitcoin's rules sum to a limit of 21 million; many assets have no such limit at all.

Professional definition

The upper bound on cumulative issuance implied by a protocol's emission schedule. It is a consequence of the rules, not a separate parameter: for Bitcoin, the halving of the block subsidy every 210,000 blocks produces a geometric series whose sum is 21 million coins.

Technical definition

A limit that exists only as long as consensus rules are unchanged and nodes continue to enforce them, which makes it a credible commitment rather than a physical constraint. For Bitcoin, the series is 210,000 blocks times 50 coins, halving each era; the infinite sum is exactly 21 million, while integer satoshi arithmetic truncates each subsidy and gives a terminal supply slightly below that. Assets with no cap are not therefore inflationary in real terms: the rate matters more than the bound.

Academic definition

The supremum of cumulative issuance implied by a protocol's reward schedule, derived from that schedule rather than stipulated, and contingent on the persistence of the consensus rules that define it.

Mechanism

A cap is the sum of a declining reward series, so it binds only because every later reward is smaller than the last. Change the schedule and the bound moves, which makes a cap a governance fact as much as an arithmetic one. Integer accounting adds a second effect: once a subsidy rounds to zero the series stops, so the total is finite in practice too.

In practice

Who uses it
Analysts modelling future float, index and valuation providers, protocol designers choosing a schedule, and anyone computing a fully diluted figure.
Why it matters
It sets the denominator for long-horizon dilution: without it, any per-unit projection of future value has no upper anchor at all.
Limitations
A cap says nothing about demand, about how much supply is liquid today, or about whether governance could raise it later.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a cap as a guarantee of appreciation.
  • Using max supply where circulating supply belongs, inflating the implied value.
  • Assuming an uncapped asset must lose purchasing power.

Examples

numeric

Bitcoin's schedule is a geometric series: 210,000 blocks at 50 coins, then at 25, then 12.5, and so on. The sum converges to exactly 21 million, and integer rounding of satoshi leaves the terminal total marginally below it.

Given
  • era length = 210,000 blocks
  • initial subsidy = 50 BTC per block
  • each era halves the subsidy
  • subsidies are integers of satoshi, 1 BTC = 100,000,000 sat
Steps
  1. era 0 issues 210,000 x 50 = 10,500,000 BTC
  2. era 1 issues 5,250,000; era 2 issues 2,625,000; the pattern halves
  3. sum of the infinite series = 210,000 x 50 x 2 = 21,000,000 BTC
  4. with integer satoshi truncation the subsidy reaches zero after 33 eras and the total is 2,099,999,997,690,000 sat
21,000,000 BTC as the exact series limit; 20,999,999.9769 BTC once integer satoshi arithmetic is applied

The shortfall is 2,310,000 satoshi, about 0.0231 BTC. It arises purely from truncating each halved subsidy to a whole satoshi.

market

Two tokens quote the same cap of one billion units. One has released most of it; the other releases over a decade. The identical cap tells you nothing about which faces more selling from new supply.

Procedure

Multiply the blocks per era by the subsidy for that era, then sum across eras. Because each subsidy is half the previous one, the series is geometric with ratio one half and its sum is twice the first term. To get the realised figure instead of the limit, truncate each subsidy to a whole satoshi before summing and stop when it reaches zero.

Worked example

Given
  • era length = 210,000 blocks
  • subsidy in era e = 50 / 2^e BTC
  • common ratio r = 1/2
Steps
  1. first term = 210,000 x 50 = 10,500,000 BTC
  2. geometric sum = first term / (1 - r) = 10,500,000 / 0.5
  3. = 21,000,000 BTC
  4. integer-satoshi variant: subsidies truncate to zero after 33 eras, total = 2,099,999,997,690,000 sat
21,000,000 BTC in the limit; 20,999,999.9769 BTC with integer satoshi truncation

Interpretation

The output is a bound on units ever created, not a statement about how many exist now or how many are liquid. It is implied by the reward rules and holds only while those rules hold.

Assumptions

  • The constants 210,000 blocks and an initial 50-coin subsidy are specific to Bitcoin; other capped assets use different schedules and the same method with different numbers.
  • The exact 21 million figure is the limit of the continuous series. Protocol implementations halve integer satoshi amounts, so the realised terminal supply is slightly lower.
  • The bound presumes the consensus rules are not changed by a future fork.
Limits of the formula
  • It bounds issuance only. Coins that are lost or provably burned reduce usable supply without changing the bound.
  • Many assets have no maximum supply, so the concept does not generalise; for those, the emission rate rather than a cap is the meaningful quantity.

Commonly confused with

Circulating Supply

Max supply is the ceiling the rules imply over all time. Circulating supply is what is tradable now. The gap between them is future issuance plus anything locked, and it can be most of the total.
