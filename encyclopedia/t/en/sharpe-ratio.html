Sharpe Ratio

Simple definition

Return above a risk-free rate divided by how much that return bounced around. It asks how much reward a strategy earned per unit of volatility.

Professional definition

Mean excess return over the risk-free rate divided by the standard deviation of that excess return, measured over a stated period and annualised by convention. It compares strategies on reward per unit of total volatility rather than on return alone.

Technical definition

A ratio of the first to the second moment of the excess-return distribution. Because the denominator is total standard deviation, upside dispersion is penalised exactly like downside dispersion. The estimate is noisy, since its standard error falls only with the square root of sample length, so short track records produce ratios that are largely sampling error. Smoothed or illiquid marks suppress the denominator and inflate the ratio.

Academic definition

The ratio of the mean differential return of a portfolio over a benchmark asset to the standard deviation of that differential return, as formalised by Sharpe (1994).

Mechanism

Dividing by dispersion is what makes two strategies comparable: a return earned with twice the volatility is worth less to an investor who must size positions against a risk budget. The choice of total standard deviation as that divisor is also the measure's main weakness, because it cannot distinguish a strategy that is volatile because it sometimes wins big from one that is volatile because it sometimes loses big.

In practice

Who uses it
Allocators comparing managers, strategy developers ranking variants, and risk teams setting leverage against a risk budget.
Why it matters
It states return per unit of volatility in one number, which makes strategies of different size and leverage roughly comparable.
Limitations
It penalises upside volatility equally, is unstable on short samples, depends on a stated risk-free rate and day count, and is inflated by illiquid or smoothed returns whose marks understate real variability.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a ratio annualised on 365 days with one annualised on 252.
  • Ranking strategies on a few months of data as if the ratio were stable.
  • Trusting a high ratio from an illiquid book priced by model.

Examples

market

Two books return the same over a year, but one did it with half the volatility, so its Sharpe ratio is twice as high. A third book shows the highest ratio of all, until its positions are revalued at prices someone will actually pay.

numeric

Mean daily return is 0.09% against a risk-free 0.01%, so excess return is 0.08%, with a daily standard deviation of 1.20%. The daily ratio is 0.0667 and annualises on 365 days to about 1.27, or about 1.06 on 252.

Given
  • mean daily portfolio return = 0.09%
  • risk-free rate = 0.01% per day
  • standard deviation of the daily excess return series = 1.20%
  • sample length: one year
Steps
  1. mean daily excess return = 0.09% - 0.01% = 0.08%
  2. daily Sharpe = 0.08 / 1.20 = 0.0667
  3. annualised on 365: 0.0667 x sqrt(365) = 0.0667 x 19.105 = 1.27
  4. annualised on 252: 0.0667 x 15.875 = 1.06
annualised Sharpe ratio of about 1.27 on a 365-day year, about 1.06 on 252

Over a one-year sample the sampling error on an annualised Sharpe ratio is of the same order as the ratio itself, so 1.27 and 1.06 are not meaningfully different and neither is evidence of a durable edge.

Mathematical foundation

SR = \frac{\bar{R}_p - \bar{R}_f}{s(R_p - R_f)}, \qquad SR_{\text{ann}} = SR_{\text{period}}\sqrt{T}

Variables

\bar{R}_pMean return of the portfolio or strategy over the periodfraction per period
\bar{R}_fMean return of the risk-free asset, or the chosen benchmark, over the same periodfraction per period

Procedure

Build the excess return series by subtracting each period's risk-free return from the portfolio's return for that period. Take the mean and the sample standard deviation of that excess series and divide. Multiply by the square root of the number of periods in a year if an annual figure is wanted, and record the benchmark, the period and the day count.

Worked example

Given
  • mean daily return = 0.09%
  • risk-free = 0.01% per day
  • s of daily excess returns = 1.20%
  • T = 365
Steps
  1. excess = 0.09% - 0.01% = 0.08%
  2. SR_daily = 0.08 / 1.20 = 0.0667
  3. sqrt(365) = 19.105
  4. SR_ann = 0.0667 x 19.105
SR_ann is approximately 1.27

Interpretation

The figure is reward per unit of volatility. A ratio of one means one unit of annualised excess return per unit of annualised standard deviation. It is only comparable against ratios computed on the same period, day count and benchmark series.

Assumptions

  • The standard deviation must be computed on the excess return series, not on the raw return series. The two differ whenever the risk-free rate varies through the sample.
  • Annualising by the square root of T inherits the independence assumption discussed in the volatility entry. Autocorrelated returns, which smoothed or illiquid marks produce, make the scaled ratio too high.
  • Sharpe (1994) frames the denominator as the standard deviation of the differential return against any chosen benchmark, not necessarily cash, so the benchmark must be named for the figure to mean anything.
  • The day-count convention -- 365 for crypto, about 252 trading days for equities -- changes the headline number and must be stated.
  • Returns must be measured net of fees, funding and slippage, and on the same frequency throughout. Mixing monthly and daily data into one series invalidates the scaling.
Limits of the formula
  • Total standard deviation penalises upside and downside dispersion equally. Downside-only measures, such as the Sortino ratio, replace the denominator with the dispersion of returns below a chosen threshold, which stops rewarding and punishing favourable volatility identically.
  • Estimation error is large on short samples: the sampling error on an annualised ratio from a single year of data is of the same order as the ratio itself, so small differences between two ratios carry no information.
  • It can be inflated without improving the strategy: illiquid positions marked to model, or any practice that smooths reported returns, lowers the denominator without lowering the risk being run.
  • It summarises the distribution by two moments only, so it is blind to skew and to tail losses that arrive rarely but matter most.

Commonly confused with

Drawdown

The Sharpe ratio measures dispersion of returns around their mean. Drawdown measures the worst peak-to-trough path actually travelled. Two books with the same Sharpe ratio can have very different drawdowns.

Two genuine practice divergences are flagged rather than hidden: the denominator should be the standard deviation of excess returns but is often computed on raw returns, and the annualisation day count differs between crypto and equity practice. The statement that sampling error on a one-year annualised ratio is of the same order as the ratio is given qualitatively because the registry holds no source for a precise standard-error formula.
