Simple definition

A fund whose shares trade on a stock exchange like a single share. Buying one share gives you a slice of everything the fund holds, without buying each holding yourself.

Professional definition

A pooled vehicle with listed shares, kept close to the value of its holdings by a creation and redemption process. Large intermediaries may exchange a defined basket of the underlying assets for new fund shares, or hand shares back for the basket, which gives professionals an arbitrage against any gap between price and net asset value.

Technical definition

The structure separates two markets: a primary market where authorised participants create and redeem in large blocks, and a secondary market where everyone else trades existing shares. Price tracks net asset value only as well as that arbitrage functions, so premiums and discounts widen when the basket is hard to trade, closed for the session, or restricted. Fees, tracking difference, and the custody arrangement for the underlying are all borne by the shareholder, who holds a claim on the fund rather than the assets themselves.

Academic definition

A collective investment vehicle whose shares are exchange-listed and whose secondary-market price is linked to net asset value through an in-kind or cash creation and redemption mechanism operated by authorised participants.

Mechanism

Creation and redemption is what separates an ETF from a closed fund. Because a professional can always convert between shares and the underlying basket, any premium invites creation and any discount invites redemption, and the two flows push the listed price back towards net asset value. Weaken that channel — a halt, a hard-to-source basket, a cash-only process — and the link loosens exactly when it is needed.

In practice

Who uses it
Investors wanting diversified or single-asset exposure through a brokerage account, allocators, and advisers building portfolios.
Why it matters
One listed instrument delivers exposure with a broker's reporting and settlement, and for a spot crypto fund it gives price exposure without the holder managing keys.
Limitations
A spot crypto ETF gives price exposure, not coins: shares cannot be withdrawn on-chain, trading follows exchange hours, fees accrue, and custody and issuer risk replace key risk. Availability differs by jurisdiction.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming shares can be redeemed for the underlying coins by a retail holder.
  • Ignoring that a premium or discount can persist when the basket is hard to trade.
  • Comparing funds on headline exposure while ignoring fees and tracking difference.

Examples

numeric

A creation unit is 50,000 shares and net asset value is 20.00 per share, so the basket is worth 1,000,000. With the shares bid at 20.10, an authorised participant buys the basket, creates the unit and sells the shares for 1,005,000 — a 5,000 gross margin whose pursuit pushes the price back down.

Given
  • Creation unit = 50,000 shares
  • Net asset value = 20.00 per share
  • Market bid = 20.10 per share
  • Transaction and basket costs ignored
  • Illustrative figures; no real fund is described
Steps
  1. Basket cost = 50,000 x 20.00 = 1,000,000
  2. Share proceeds = 50,000 x 20.10 = 1,005,000
  3. Gross margin = 1,005,000 - 1,000,000 = 5,000
  4. Margin per share = 0.10, which is the premium being arbitraged away
A 0.50% premium is worth 5,000 on one creation unit before costs

Real costs, basket availability and any restriction on creations determine whether the trade is worth doing. Where it is not, the premium persists — which is why a discount or premium is information about the arbitrage channel, not about the fund's holdings.

market

A spot crypto fund and self-custody give the same price exposure and almost opposite risks. One substitutes a custodian, an issuer and exchange hours for the holder's keys; the other keeps the keys and every duty that comes with them.
