Covered Call

Simple definition

Owning an asset and selling a call on the same amount of it. You keep the premium, and in exchange you agree to sell at the strike if price rises above it.

Professional definition

A long underlying position combined with a written call on the same quantity. Position value at expiry is min(S,K) plus the premium received, so the upside is truncated at the strike while the downside is the asset's own, cushioned only by the premium.

Technical definition

Equivalent by put-call parity to writing a put at the same strike, financed by holding the asset. The position is short gamma and short vega: it profits from quiet markets and from decay of the written option, and loses its advantage precisely in a strong rally, where the forgone gain is unbounded while the premium was finite.

Academic definition

A long position in an underlying asset combined with a short call on the same notional, yielding a concave payoff truncated at the strike plus the premium received.

Mechanism

The premium buys one specific thing: the right to gains above the strike. That tail holds an asset's largest single outcomes, so selling it raises the frequency of small positive results and removes the few large ones. The holder keeps the full downside apart from the premium cushion, which is why the trade reshapes returns rather than reducing risk.

In practice

Who uses it
Holders of an existing long position who are willing to part with it at a chosen price, and funds that write systematically against an index holding.
Why it matters
It converts part of an uncertain future gain into cash received today, at the cost of a fixed ceiling on the outcome.
Limitations
It does not hedge. A fall is absorbed only to the extent of the premium, and the position cannot be held through a rally without either assignment or buying the call back at a loss.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the premium income while the asset itself is unhedged.
  • Writing against a holding one would not actually sell at the strike.
  • Repeatedly buying back written calls in rallies and selling lower ones after falls.

Examples

market

An asset held at 100, with a 105 call written for 3. In a flat market the premium is kept and the asset is still held. In a move to 130 the holder delivers at 105 and keeps 3, having given up 25 of the gain.

numeric

Asset bought at 100, 105 call written for 3. Maximum gain is 8, at or above 105. Breakeven is 97. Maximum loss is 97, if the asset goes to zero. The premium shifts the whole payoff up by 3 and caps it.

Given
  • asset purchase price S_0 = 100
  • written call strike K = 105
  • premium received c = 3
  • one unit, European exercise, fees and any carry ignored
Steps
  1. maximum gain = (105 - 100) + 3 = 8, reached at any S_T >= 105
  2. breakeven = 100 - 3 = 97
  3. maximum loss = 100 - 3 = 97, at S_T = 0
  4. at S_T = 130: position = 105 - 100 + 3 = 8, versus 30 for the asset alone
gain capped at 8; loss bounded only by the asset reaching zero, at 97; at 130 the 25 above the strike is surrendered, 22 net of the premium

Numbers are illustrative. This is a description of the payoff, not a suggestion that the trade is advisable.

Mathematical foundation

\Pi_{\text{expiry}} = \min(S_T,\ K) - S_0 + c, \qquad \Pi_{\max} = K - S_0 + c, \qquad \Pi_{\min} = -(S_0 - c)

Procedure

Take the lower of the expiry price and the strike, because any gain above the strike belongs to the call buyer. Subtract the purchase price and add the premium received. Maximum gain follows by setting the expiry price at or above the strike; maximum loss by setting it to zero.

Worked example

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • K = 105
  • c = 3
  • S_T = 130
Steps
  1. min(130, 105) = 105
  2. 105 - 100 = 5
  3. 5 + 3 = 8
profit 8, against 30 for holding the asset alone

Interpretation

The arithmetic makes the trade-off explicit: a known, small addition today in exchange for an unknown, potentially large subtraction later. Whether that exchange is worth making is a question about the holder's objectives, not about the payoff.

Assumptions

  • Stated per unit, for a European-style written call held to expiry, with the underlying quantity exactly matching the contract size.
  • Fees, the carry or financing of the asset, any dividend or staking yield, and the margin the venue may require on the written leg are all excluded.
  • The equivalence to a written put at the same strike follows from put-call parity and holds under the same frictionless assumptions as parity itself.
  • American-style written calls can be assigned early, which breaks the expiry arithmetic and can force delivery before the holder intended.
Limits of the formula
  • The maximum loss figure assumes the asset can fall to zero; for most underlyings that is a bound, not a scenario, but the premium cushion is small against any large fall.
  • Repeating the trade across many expiries does not accumulate premium without accumulating the truncated tails, and the comparison against simply holding the asset depends entirely on the path of prices.
  • This entry describes mechanics and costs. It is not a recommendation to write calls against any holding.

Commonly confused with

Protective Put

A covered call sells the upside tail for cash now. A protective put buys away the downside tail for cash now. They are opposite trades, not variants of one hedge.
