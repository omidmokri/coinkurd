Kelly Criterion

Simple definition

A formula for how much of your money to risk on a repeated bet so that wealth grows as fast as possible over many rounds, given your chance of winning and the payoff.

Professional definition

The fraction of capital that maximises the expected logarithm of wealth over repeated independent bets. For a binary bet it reduces to the win probability minus the loss probability divided by the payoff odds, and it is a growth criterion, not a comfort criterion.

Technical definition

The growth-optimal fraction, derived by maximising expected log wealth rather than expected wealth. It is asymptotically optimal for long-run growth but highly sensitive to the inputs, because the growth rate is a concave function of the fraction: overestimating edge pushes the bet past the peak, and in the continuous approximation twice the optimal fraction gives zero expected growth.

Academic definition

The capital fraction maximising the expected logarithmic utility of terminal wealth over a sequence of independent, identically distributed favourable wagers.

Mechanism

Maximising expected wealth would tell you to bet everything on any favourable edge, because it ignores that ruin is absorbing. Maximising the expected logarithm instead prices the fact that losses compound against you: the log penalises a halving far more than it rewards a doubling, so the optimum stays strictly below the full bankroll and falls as the edge narrows.

In practice

Who uses it
Quantitative sizing research, systematic funds, and sports and card bettors, where probabilities are better known than in markets.
Why it matters
It gives a principled upper bound on bet size from an edge estimate, and shows that any fraction above it lowers long-run growth rather than raising it.
Limitations
It needs the probability and payoff as inputs and markets supply neither reliably. Full Kelly produces drawdowns most traders and most mandates cannot tolerate, so practice uses a fraction of it.

Common mistakes

  • Applying Kelly with a win probability estimated from a short backtest.
  • Treating the full Kelly fraction as a recommendation rather than a growth bound.
  • Ignoring that correlated simultaneous bets consume the same bankroll.

Examples

market

A desk estimates a 55 percent edge from a short sample and sizes at full Kelly. The true edge is 51 percent; the fraction is now far past the growth peak, and the same strategy that would have compounded slowly instead grinds the account down.

numeric

A bet wins 55 percent of the time and pays two to one. Kelly puts 32.5 percent of capital at risk. Half Kelly, about 16 percent, gives roughly three quarters of the growth with far shallower falls along the way.

Given
  • win probability p = 0.55
  • loss probability q = 0.45
  • net payoff odds b = 2 (win 2 units per 1 unit risked)
  • bets are independent and the edge estimate is treated as exact
Steps
  1. f* = p - q / b
  2. = 0.55 - 0.45 / 2
  3. = 0.55 - 0.225
f* = 0.325, i.e. 32.5 percent of capital per bet

Half Kelly here is 16.25 percent. In the continuous approximation, halving the fraction keeps about three quarters of the growth rate while roughly halving the volatility of the equity path. These are properties of the model, not advice about how to allocate capital.

Mathematical foundation

f^{*} = p - \frac{q}{b} = \frac{bp - q}{b}, \qquad q = 1 - p

Procedure

For a binary bet, take the win probability, subtract the loss probability divided by the net odds, and the result is the fraction of capital to risk. A negative result means the bet has no edge at these odds and the growth-optimal stake is zero. For a trading strategy, b is the ratio of average win to average loss and p the win rate, both estimated from the same sample.

Worked example

Given
  • p = 0.55
  • q = 1 - 0.55 = 0.45
  • b = 2
Steps
  1. q / b = 0.45 / 2 = 0.225
  2. f* = 0.55 - 0.225
  3. cross-check with the other form: (bp - q) / b = (2 x 0.55 - 0.45) / 2 = (1.10 - 0.45) / 2 = 0.65 / 2 = 0.325
f* = 0.325, so 32.5 percent of capital per bet

Interpretation

The output is a growth ceiling, not a target. Staking less than f* grows wealth more slowly but more smoothly; staking more grows it more slowly and far less smoothly. Because the curve is flat near its peak and steep beyond it, the cost of underbetting is small and the cost of overbetting is severe.

Assumptions

  • Bets are independent, identically distributed and repeated many times; a single bet has no long-run growth to optimise.
  • The probability p and the odds b are known exactly. In markets they are estimates, and the formula gives no allowance for that error.
  • Capital is fully divisible, fully reinvested each round, and there is no minimum size, no fee and no borrowing constraint.
  • The binary form applies to a two-outcome bet. Continuous return distributions use f* = mu / sigma squared in the Gaussian approximation, and the statement that twice the optimal fraction gives zero expected growth belongs to that approximation rather than to the discrete case.
Limits of the formula
  • Full Kelly produces equity paths whose drawdowns most traders and most mandates cannot tolerate; this is a property of the criterion, not a misuse of it.
  • Parameter error is punished asymmetrically: overestimating the edge moves the stake past the growth peak, where expected growth falls fast and can turn negative, which is the practical reason fractional Kelly is used.
  • Simultaneous correlated positions share one bankroll, so applying the single-bet formula separately to each over-levers the portfolio.

Commonly confused with

Position Sizing

Fixed-fractional sizing risks a constant share of equity per trade regardless of edge. Kelly makes the share a function of the estimated edge, which is more ambitious and far more fragile to bad estimates.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The derivation and the binary formula are settled mathematics. What is genuinely contested is its application to markets, where p and b are unstable estimates rather than known parameters, and where practitioners disagree about which fraction of Kelly is appropriate; this entry states the growth result and its fragility without recommending any fraction. The Gaussian result about twice the optimal fraction is stated as belonging to the continuous approximation.
