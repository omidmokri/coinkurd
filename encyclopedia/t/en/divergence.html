Simple definition

When price makes a new extreme but the indicator does not — a sign that the force behind the move is smaller than the move itself.

Professional definition

A disagreement between the sequence of price pivots and the matching sequence of oscillator pivots. Bearish divergence pairs a higher price high with a lower oscillator high; bullish divergence pairs a lower price low with a higher oscillator low.

Technical definition

A comparison of two pivot sequences, so it inherits every choice made in defining a pivot, the oscillator's parameters, and the window within which two pivots may be paired. It is also persistent: in a strong trend an oscillator can diverge across several swings with no reversal, which makes divergence a report of decelerating momentum rather than a turning signal. It can only be identified once the second pivot has confirmed.

Academic definition

A structural disagreement between the extrema of a price series and those of a derived momentum series over a matched interval.

Mechanism

A bounded momentum oscillator measures the average size of recent moves. If price grinds to a new high in smaller steps than it used before, the average step shrinks even while the level rises, so the oscillator peaks lower. Divergence is therefore a direct report that each unit of price progress is costing more time or smaller candles than it did.

In practice

Who uses it
Oscillator users looking for momentum exhaustion, swing traders timing exits, and systematic developers who must encode a pivot rule before divergence can be tested at all.
Why it matters
It is one of the few oscillator readings that uses the indicator's shape rather than a fixed threshold, so trend saturation does not destroy it the way level signals are destroyed.
Limitations
Divergences recur repeatedly inside strong trends and most resolve by continuation. With no agreed pivot or pairing rule, published hit rates are not comparable with each other.

Common mistakes

  • Reading divergence from a pivot that has not yet confirmed.
  • Pairing pivots dozens of bars apart with nothing in between.
  • Trading it against the higher timeframe with no level to lean on.

Examples

market

Price posts a higher high after a six-week rally while RSI's matching peak comes in lower. Momentum is decelerating. If the trend is strong this can repeat three or four times before anything turns, and often nothing turns.

numeric

Two confirmed price highs at 100 and 105 with RSI peaks of 78 and 71. Price is five percent higher and the oscillator seven points lower: this is regular bearish divergence by definition, and by itself it is not an entry.

Given
  • price pivot high 1 = 100, RSI(14) at that pivot = 78
  • price pivot high 2 = 105, RSI(14) at that pivot = 71
  • both pivots confirmed by a 5-bar rule
  • pairing window = 60 bars
Steps
  1. price change = (105 - 100) / 100 = +5%
  2. oscillator change = 71 - 78 = -7 points
  3. price higher, oscillator lower
regular bearish divergence under this pivot and pairing specification

Widen the pairing window or change the pivot rule and this pair may vanish or a different pair may appear. The event is a function of the specification.

Commonly confused with

Overbought and Oversold

An extreme reading says where the oscillator sits now. Divergence says how its pivots are trending against price. Different readings, different failure modes.
