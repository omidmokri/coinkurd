Book Value

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

What the accounts say the owners' share of a company is worth: everything it owns minus everything it owes, as recorded in the books.

Professional definition

Shareholders' equity as carried in the balance sheet — total assets less total liabilities — and per share, that residual divided by shares outstanding. It is a historical-cost record of capital contributed and profits retained, not an appraisal of what the assets would fetch.

Technical definition

The accounting residual defined by the elements framework: assets less liabilities equals equity. It is built from amounts recognised and measured under the reporting framework, so internally generated brands, trained workforces and most research never appear, while acquired goodwill does. Buybacks above book reduce it, impairments reduce it late, and revaluation models change it without a transaction.

Academic definition

The residual interest in the assets of an entity after deducting all its liabilities, measured on the recognition and measurement bases applied in the financial statements.

Mechanism

Equity is not measured directly; it is what is left after assets and liabilities have been measured. Every recognition rule therefore flows into it. Spending that accounting treats as an expense — research, marketing, training — leaves nothing on the balance sheet, so the firms whose value is built that way show the smallest book value and the largest apparent premium to it.

In practice

Who uses it
Bank and insurance analysts, lenders testing covenant headroom, liquidation and restructuring specialists, and anyone computing a price-to-book ratio.
Why it matters
It is an audited, comparatively stable anchor that exists even when earnings are negative, and it is the base for return on equity and price-to-book.
Limitations
It is a record of cost, not of value. Intangible-heavy firms are understated, revalued property is marked at an estimate, and impairments usually arrive after the economics changed.

Common mistakes

  • Treating book value as what the assets would sell for.
  • Comparing book value across firms without checking goodwill.
  • Ignoring that buybacks above book shrink it mechanically.

Examples

numeric

Total assets 1,900 and total liabilities 1,200 give book equity of 700. Across 40 shares that is 17.5 per share. Strip out acquired goodwill of 300 and tangible book value per share falls to 10.

Given
  • Total assets = 1,900
  • Total liabilities = 1,200
  • Ordinary shares outstanding = 40
  • Acquired goodwill included in assets = 300
Steps
  1. Book equity = 1,900 - 1,200 = 700
  2. Book value per share = 700 / 40 = 17.5
  3. Tangible book equity = 700 - 300 = 400
  4. Tangible book value per share = 400 / 40 = 10
Book value per share 17.5; tangible book value per share 10

A 43 per cent difference between two figures that are both called book value. The variant must be stated.

case

An acquirer pays well above the target's book value and records the excess as goodwill, so group book value rises by the purchase price. Three years later the business disappoints and the goodwill is written off, cutting book value with no cash leaving the company.

Mathematical foundation

BV_{equity} = A - L, \qquad BVPS = \frac{A - L - Pref}{N}

Variables

ATotal assets at their carrying amountscurrency
LTotal liabilities at their carrying amountscurrency
PrefPreferred equity and other claims ranking ahead of ordinary sharescurrency
NOrdinary shares outstanding at the reporting dateshares

Procedure

Read total assets and total liabilities from the balance sheet and subtract. Remove any preferred or non-controlling claim that ranks ahead of the ordinary shares, then divide by shares outstanding. Note separately whether goodwill and other intangibles are inside the figure.

Worked example

Given
  • A = 1,900
  • L = 1,200
  • Pref = 0
  • N = 40
Steps
  1. BV_equity = 1,900 - 1,200 = 700
  2. BVPS = 700 / 40
Book equity 700; book value per share 17.5

Interpretation

The figure states how much of the recorded assets the ordinary shareholders would be entitled to if liabilities were settled at their carrying amounts. That conditional is doing a great deal of work, because carrying amounts are not realisation values.

Assumptions

  • Carrying amounts follow the reporting framework in use: historical cost, cost less impairment, revaluation or fair value depending on the asset class and the election made.
  • Variants are not interchangeable — total book value, tangible book value and book value excluding non-controlling interests are different numbers.
  • The identity holds by construction, so it cannot be used to test whether the balance sheet is right; it only restates how equity was derived.
Limits of the formula
  • Omits internally generated intangibles entirely, so it systematically understates asset-light businesses.
  • Can be reduced by buybacks and by late impairments, both of which change the figure without any change in the underlying business.

Commonly confused with

Market Capitalisation

Market capitalisation is what buyers are paying for the shares today. Book value is what the accounts recorded for the same claim. The two agree only by coincidence.
