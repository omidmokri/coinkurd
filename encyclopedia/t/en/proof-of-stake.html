Simple definition

A rule that gives the right to propose and confirm blocks to participants who lock up coins as a deposit. Break the rules and part of that deposit is destroyed.

Professional definition

Sybil resistance through bonded capital rather than computation. Validators deposit stake, are selected to propose or attest to blocks, earn protocol rewards for correct behaviour, and lose stake through penalties or slashing for equivocation and prolonged absence. On Ethereum a validator bonds 32 ETH and finality comes from supermajority attestation.

Technical definition

The attack cost is capital the protocol itself can destroy, which makes punishment endogenous rather than merely competitive. Equivocation is deterred by slashing, so the nothing-at-stake problem does not arise. Long-range revisions remain possible for a node syncing from scratch, which is why implementations rely on weak subjectivity and recent checkpoints. The dominant centralisation vector is stake concentration through large operators and liquid staking, not hardware.

Academic definition

A consensus mechanism allocating block-proposal and attestation rights in proportion to bonded, slashable capital, so that protocol violations are punished endogenously rather than merely out-competed by honest participants.

Mechanism

The deposit turns identity into something costly to duplicate and, crucially, something the protocol can take away. An attacker must buy or borrow a large share of stake, use it to sign contradictory messages, and watch that stake be burned by the same rules it was trying to break. The penalty is paid in the asset the attack would devalue.

In practice

Who uses it
Validators and staking providers, protocol researchers, and holders deciding whether to stake directly, delegate, or use a liquid staking token.
Why it matters
It secures a chain without continuous energy purchase and makes misbehaviour directly punishable, which allows faster economic finality than cumulative work does.
Limitations
Security is bounded by stake value and by how dispersed stake actually is. Slashing, exit queues and client bugs are real operational risks for the staker.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming staking rewards are risk-free yield rather than payment for operational duty.
  • Ignoring that a liquid staking token can trade away from the staked asset.
  • Treating validator count as a measure of decentralisation.

Examples

numeric

Ethereum bonds 32 ETH per validator, so a pool with 1,000,000 ETH staked runs 31,250 validators. Counting validators therefore counts deposits, not independent operators — one entity can run thousands.

Given
  • stake per validator = 32 ETH (Ethereum protocol value)
  • stake controlled by one operator = 1,000,000 ETH
Steps
  1. 1,000,000 / 32 = 31,250
31,250 validators run by a single operator

The operator figure is illustrative. The point is structural: validator count and operator count are different quantities.

crypto

A validator that signs two different blocks for the same slot is slashed: the protocol burns part of its deposit and ejects it. Proof-of-work has no comparable punishment — a dishonest miner simply loses the race.

Commonly confused with

Staking

Staking is what a holder does with coins; proof-of-stake is the consensus rule that gives those locked coins a role. Many products marketed as staking are lending or yield schemes with no consensus duty at all.
