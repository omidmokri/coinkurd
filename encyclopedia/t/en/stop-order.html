Stop Order

Simple definition

A waiting instruction that becomes a market order the moment a trigger price is touched. It gets you out — at whatever price exists after the trigger.

Professional definition

A conditional order held by the venue until its trigger is reached, then released as a market order. It therefore guarantees execution and not price: the fill can sit far from the stop level when the move that triggered it is a gap or a cascade.

Technical definition

Two prices matter and they are not the same. The trigger is compared by the venue against a nominated reference — last, mark or index price — and the eventual fill is set by whatever depth remains when the released market order arrives. Because triggers are crossed precisely in fast one-sided conditions, realised slippage on stops is systematically worse than on ordinary market orders. Which reference price arms the trigger is a venue setting and changes when the stop fires.

Academic definition

A contingent instruction that converts to a marketable order upon a price trigger, retaining execution certainty while surrendering price certainty under exactly the conditions in which price certainty is most valuable.

Mechanism

A stop is invisible in the book, so it adds no depth and removes some the moment it fires. Stops cluster at the same obvious levels, so one triggering trade releases a wave of same-direction market orders into a book already thinned by the move. That is the mechanical core of a stop cascade.

In practice

Who uses it
Every trader using a protective exit, plus systematic strategies whose risk rule is written as a price distance from entry.
Why it matters
It enforces a loss limit without the trader watching the screen, which is why it remains the standard risk-control instrument.
Limitations
It promises nothing about the fill. Gaps, trading halts and thin weekend books can hand you a price far beyond the level you chose.

Common mistakes

  • Describing a stop order as a guaranteed exit price.
  • Placing stops at round numbers where everyone else places them.
  • Not checking which reference price the venue triggers on.

Examples

market

A share closes at 50 with a protective stop at 48. Overnight news gaps it open at 41, the stop triggers and fills near 41. The order behaved exactly as designed; it was never a promise about price.

numeric

A 500-unit stop triggers at 48.00. Only 100 units rest at 47.95, 150 at 47.80 and 250 at 47.40, so the average fill is 47.63 — about 1.6% below the level chosen.

Given
  • sell stop for 500 units, trigger 48.00
  • resting bids after the trigger: 100 at 47.95, 150 at 47.80, 250 at 47.40
  • fees excluded
Steps
  1. proceeds = 100 x 47.95 + 150 x 47.80 + 250 x 47.40 = 4795 + 7170 + 11850 = 23815
  2. average fill = 23815 / 500 = 47.63
  3. shortfall vs trigger = (48.00 - 47.63) / 48.00 = 0.0077
average fill 47.63, about 77 basis points worse than the trigger price

The stop did execute in full. The 77 basis points are the price of that certainty, and they widen as depth thins.

Commonly confused with

Stop-Loss

A stop-loss is the risk decision: where the trade is wrong. A stop order is one venue instrument for carrying that decision out, and not the only one.
