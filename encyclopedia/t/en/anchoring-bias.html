Anchoring Bias

Simple definition

A first number sticks. Once a price, a target or a forecast is in view, later judgements stay close to it, even when that first number was arbitrary.

Professional definition

The tendency for a quantitative judgement to be pulled toward a salient starting value, with insufficient adjustment away from it. Tversky and Kahneman showed the pull survives even when the participants know the anchor was generated at random. In markets the anchor is usually an entry price, a round number or a published target.

Technical definition

Anchoring is an adjustment failure rather than an information failure: the anchor need carry no information, and may be known to be irrelevant, yet estimates still migrate toward it. The 1974 demonstration used a spun wheel as the anchor. The anchors that matter to a trader are mostly self-generated — the fill price, the prior high, the level where a position first turned negative — and they persist through revisions of the underlying case, because what they fix is the comparison, not the valuation.

Academic definition

A judgemental bias in which estimates of an uncertain quantity are assimilated toward an initially presented or self-generated value, with adjustment away from that value that is systematically insufficient.

Mechanism

Judging an unfamiliar quantity is effortful, so the mind starts from whatever value is already available and corrects from there. Correction stops early, so the estimate keeps the anchor's imprint. A position supplies its own anchor at the moment of the fill, which is why the same chart reads differently to a holder and to an observer with no position.

In practice

Who uses it
Discretionary traders setting targets, analysts revising forecasts, and anyone holding a position taken at a remembered price.
Why it matters
Naming the anchor separates two questions that feel like one: what this asset is worth now, and what it cost you.
Limitations
Knowing about the effect does not remove it; the 1974 experiments found the pull present in participants who were told the anchor was random.

Common mistakes

  • Setting an exit at break-even because that is where the position started.
  • Reading a round number as a level rather than as an anchor.
  • Treating a published price target as information about value.

Examples

market

A token traded at 40 last year and at 4 today. Holders who bought near 40 often describe 4 as cheap. The earlier price is not evidence about present value; it is the anchor their judgement starts from.

numeric

Two traders estimate a fair level for the same asset. One is shown a recent high of 120 first, the other a recent low of 60. Their estimates typically straddle the number each saw, although both looked at identical data afterwards.

Given
  • anchor A = 120 for trader 1, anchor B = 60 for trader 2
  • both then see the same price history and the same fundamentals
  • a hypothetical unbiased estimate of 90 for both
Steps
  1. trader 1 adjusts downward from 120 and stops early, reporting about 105
  2. trader 2 adjusts upward from 60 and stops early, reporting about 75
  3. the gap of about 30 is attributable to the anchor, not to the data
two estimates about 30 apart from identical evidence

Illustrative numbers chosen to show the direction and the insufficiency of adjustment. The 1974 paper establishes the pattern; it fixes no size of effect for market estimates.

Commonly confused with

Support and Resistance

A support level is a claim about where orders sit in the book. An anchor is a claim about where a judgement starts. The same round number can be both, or neither.
