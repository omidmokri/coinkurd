Scaling In and Out

Risk Management

Professional

Simple definition

Building a position in parts instead of all at once, and closing it in parts too. Each part changes your average price and how much is still at risk.

Professional definition

Entering or exiting a position in tranches. Scaling in spreads execution across prices and reduces the dependence of the outcome on one fill; scaling out banks part of a gain while leaving the rest exposed. Both change the position's average price, which is the size-weighted mean of the fills.

Technical definition

The average price is the sum of quantity times price over the total quantity. Two distinct practices share the name and have opposite risk profiles: adding in the direction of the move, which raises exposure after confirmation and worsens the average entry, and adding against the move, which improves the average entry while increasing size into an adverse trend. Only the first is bounded by a predefined total risk unless the second has its full intended size budgeted from the start.

Academic definition

The decomposition of a position's execution into multiple tranches, altering the cost basis as a quantity-weighted mean and modifying the distribution of realised outcomes relative to a single-fill execution.

Mechanism

Splitting an execution lowers the variance of the entry price around the period's average, at the cost of certainty about the size you end up holding. Scaling out does the mirror thing to the exit: it narrows the spread of outcomes by taking some gain early, which also removes the right tail that pays for the losing trades.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders working size that the order book cannot absorb at once, trend followers adding on confirmation, and anyone taking partial profit at defined levels.
Why it matters
It separates two decisions that a single order conflates: whether the idea is valid, and how much exposure the account should carry right now.
Limitations
Each tranche pays costs, and the plan has to state the total size and total risk up front or it becomes an unplanned increase in exposure.

Common mistakes

  • Adding to a losing position with no pre-budgeted total size.
  • Scaling out of winners while letting losers run full size.
  • Ignoring the extra fees and slippage of multiple fills.

Examples

numeric

A trader buys one unit at 100, one at 96 and two at 92. Total four units at an average of 95. The average looks better than the first fill, but the position is now four times the original size, so the risk per point is four times as large.

Given
  • fill 1: 1 unit at 100
  • fill 2: 1 unit at 96
  • fill 3: 2 units at 92
  • total quantity = 4 units
Steps
  1. cost = (1 x 100) + (1 x 96) + (2 x 92) = 100 + 96 + 184 = 380
  2. average price = 380 / 4 = 95
  3. loss at 90 = 4 x (95 - 90) = 20
  4. loss at 90 on the first fill alone would have been 1 x (100 - 90) = 10
Average 95 on four units; a move to 90 now costs 20 instead of 10.

Averaging down improves the entry price and doubles the loss at the same adverse price. The total size must be budgeted before the first fill.

market

A fund needs to buy more than the visible depth allows. It works the order over a session in tranches, accepting an uncertain average near the period's volume-weighted price rather than paying the full impact of one large market order.

Procedure

Decide the total intended size and the total risk before the first order, then divide them into tranches with a rule for when each one is placed. After every fill, recompute the average price as the cost of all fills divided by the total quantity, and recompute the distance from the average to the stop to check that total risk has not drifted above the budget.

Worked example

Given
  • 1 unit at 100
  • 1 unit at 96
  • 2 units at 92
  • stop for the whole position at 90
Steps
  1. total cost = 100 + 96 + 184 = 380
  2. total quantity = 4
  3. average = 380 / 4 = 95
  4. risk per unit = 95 - 90 = 5
  5. total risk = 4 x 5 = 20
Average 95, total risk 20 against a 90 stop.

Interpretation

The average price is only an accounting figure: it fixes where the position breaks even, not whether the position should exist. Total risk is the number that matters, and it is size times the distance from the average to the stop.

Assumptions

  • Fills are recorded at executed prices, including slippage, and fees are accounted for separately.
  • All tranches are in the same instrument and the same direction. Mixing instruments requires a different aggregation.
  • The stop applies to the whole position. Per-tranche stops produce a different and usually larger total risk.
  • The intended total size and total risk are set before the first fill, so adding tranches does not raise the risk budget.
Limits of the formula
  • A better average price is not a better position: it is the same market exposure at a larger size, with the loss at any adverse price scaled by the quantity.
  • Scaling out reduces the variance of outcomes and also reduces the mean, because the largest winners are the trades that are exited early.
  • Each tranche pays spread and fees, so a many-tranche plan needs a wider edge than its single-fill equivalent.

Commonly confused with

Dollar-Cost Averaging

Dollar-cost averaging invests a fixed amount at fixed intervals as a long-term accumulation policy. Scaling in is execution of one planned trade across several fills, with a stop and a total size already defined.
