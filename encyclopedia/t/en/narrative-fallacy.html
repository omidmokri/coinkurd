Narrative Fallacy

Simple definition

A story that explains a price move feels like evidence for what comes next. It is not. The story was built after the move, and many stories fit the same chart.

Professional definition

The tendency to accept a coherent explanation of past prices as grounds for a forecast. Coherence and predictive content are separate properties: an account can fit every observation to date and still have no testable implication for tomorrow.

Technical definition

Three documented mechanisms do the work: representativeness, which treats a vivid story as more probable than a dull base rate; availability, which supplies whichever cause is in circulation; and hindsight, which makes the chosen cause feel inevitable. The label narrative fallacy comes from popular writing on uncertainty rather than from the research literature, and its status as a distinct bias is not established. What is testable is the forecast a narrative implies, if it implies one at all.

Academic definition

The treatment of post-hoc explanatory coherence as evidential support for prediction; the underlying heuristics are documented, while the compound label itself is informal rather than an established construct.

Mechanism

Explanations are cheap after the fact because the outcome is already known, and the mind reaches for the cause that is easiest to retrieve. A good story also compresses many facts into one, which feels like understanding. The compression is where the forecast is lost: the details that were discarded are precisely the ones that would have let the story be wrong.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone reading market commentary, writing a thesis for a position, or explaining a move after it happened.
Why it matters
Asking what observation would make this story false converts a narrative into a hypothesis, which can be tested or set aside.
Limitations
Narratives are not noise. They coordinate attention and can move flows, which makes them real as a mechanism even when they are worthless as evidence about value.

Common mistakes

  • Taking a cause offered after a move as information about the next one.
  • Confusing a story that fits all the data with a story that predicted any of it.
  • Dropping a position's stated invalidation once a new explanation is available.

Examples

market

The same monthly figure is reported as the reason for a rally one month and for a sell-off the next. Both reports are coherent. Neither was written before the move, and neither states what would have falsified it.

crypto

A protocol upgrade is named as the driver of a rise. The upgrade was public for months, so the rise it supposedly caused followed a widely known fact — which is the opposite of what the story needs in order to be informative.

Commonly confused with

Efficient Market Hypothesis

The efficient-market hypothesis is a testable claim about how fast information enters prices. The narrative fallacy is about mistaking explanation for information in the first place, and it applies whether or not the hypothesis holds.
