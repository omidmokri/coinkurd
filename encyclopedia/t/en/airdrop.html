Airdrop

Foundation

Simple definition

A distribution of tokens to many addresses without charging for them, usually to reward past use of a service or to spread a new token widely. Receiving one can still have tax consequences.

Professional definition

A distribution mechanism in which an issuer allocates tokens to addresses meeting a snapshot criterion, typically past protocol usage, holding of another asset, or completion of defined tasks. Claims are usually pull-based through a contract, and unclaimed allocations may expire back to a treasury.

Technical definition

A marketing and distribution device whose economics are those of a stock split in reverse: nothing is created, the issuer transfers part of its allocation to users it wants to retain. Eligibility rules invite gaming through many addresses, so designs add thresholds, activity weighting or identity checks. Claim pages are also a standard phishing target, since a user expecting to sign a claim is primed to approve an unfamiliar transaction. Tax treatment differs by jurisdiction and is not optional in the ones that address it.

Academic definition

A non-reciprocal allocation of newly issued or reserved tokens to a set of addresses determined by eligibility criteria, used to bootstrap distribution, reward prior participation, or decentralise governance holdings.

Mechanism

The issuer is buying distribution and attention with inventory it already holds, so the cost falls on existing holders through dilution rather than on the issuer's cash. Because eligibility is announced or guessed in advance, activity appears before the snapshot and often disappears after it, which is why many designs weight by duration rather than by a single-day balance.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol teams bootstrapping distribution, early users of new protocols, security teams reviewing claim contracts, and tax advisers in jurisdictions that have addressed it.
Why it matters
It spreads holdings and governance weight quickly, and for users it is one of the few ways a token arrives without a purchase.
Limitations
Receipt may be taxable where rules exist, the claim transaction itself is a phishing surface, and tokens distributed free are frequently sold immediately, which is a known supply event.

Common mistakes

  • Signing an unfamiliar claim transaction from a link rather than a verified contract.
  • Assuming a free receipt has no tax consequence.
  • Farming many addresses without reading the eligibility rules.

Examples

numeric

An issuer allocates 5 percent of a 400 million supply to past users, so 20 million tokens across 200,000 eligible addresses — an average of 100 each, though activity weighting makes the median far lower.

Given
  • total supply = 400,000,000 tokens
  • airdrop allocation = 5 percent
  • eligible addresses = 200,000
Steps
  1. 0.05 x 400,000,000 = 20,000,000 tokens
  2. 20,000,000 / 200,000 = 100 tokens per address on average
20,000,000 tokens distributed; mean allocation 100 tokens per address

Hypothetical figures. Weighted designs are strongly skewed, so the mean says little about what a typical address receives.

everyday

A message offers a claim and asks for a signature on a page that looks official. The signature may approve spending rather than receiving. Verifying the contract address before signing is the whole defence.

Commonly confused with

Token Unlock

An airdrop puts tokens in new hands at no charge. An unlock releases tokens already allocated to someone. Both add to tradable supply, but one widens the holder base and the other does not.
