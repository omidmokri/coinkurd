Foundation

Simple definition

A survey score for how households feel about their finances and the economy, now and in the months ahead. It records opinions, not spending.

Professional definition

An index built from household answers about current conditions and expectations, usually including expected inflation. Compilers scale answers to a base period, publish current-conditions and expectations sub-indexes, and the level is only comparable within one provider's own series.

Technical definition

A sentiment statistic whose link to actual spending is weak and unstable: households can report deep pessimism while continuing to spend, especially when employment and credit hold up. Its more defensible use is the expectations detail, in particular household inflation expectations, which central banks monitor because expectations feed into wage and price setting. Question wording and scaling differ by compiler.

Academic definition

A composite survey index of household appraisals of present and expected economic conditions, used as a sentiment proxy whose predictive relation to realised consumption is contested.

Mechanism

Confidence surveys work through expectations: a household that expects to lose income may postpone large purchases, and one that expects prices to rise may bring them forward. Both effects exist, they pull in opposite directions, and which dominates depends on employment and credit — which is why sentiment alone forecasts spending poorly.

In practice

Who uses it
Central banks tracking inflation expectations, consumer-sector analysts, and macro desks reading survey calendars.
Why it matters
The expectations component, especially expected inflation, is a genuine input to policy thinking, and turning points in sentiment sometimes cluster around cycle turns.
Limitations
The level is provider-specific and not comparable across surveys; the relationship to realised spending is weak enough that it should never be used alone.

Common mistakes

  • Forecasting consumption from a sentiment index alone.
  • Comparing two providers' confidence levels as one series.
  • Treating survey inflation expectations as a price forecast.

Examples

numeric

Thirty percent of households say conditions improved and 45 percent say they worsened. The net balance is minus 15: pessimists outnumber optimists, which is a statement about opinion and not about spending.

Given
  • share reporting improvement = 30%
  • share reporting deterioration = 45%
  • remainder reporting no change = 25%
Steps
  1. net balance = 30 - 45
  2. = -15 percentage points
net balance = -15

Shares are invented. A net balance is one common survey construction; published confidence indexes rescale such balances to a base period in ways that differ by compiler.

market

Sentiment readings sit near historic lows for several quarters while retail sales keep growing. Analysts who sold the consumer sector on the survey alone were trading the opinion, not the spending.

Commonly confused with

Fear and Greed Index

Consumer confidence surveys households about their own economic situation. A market fear-and-greed gauge scores traders' positioning and price behaviour. Different populations, different inputs, different uses.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The predictive value of sentiment indexes for realised consumption is genuinely contested and this registry contains no study that settles it, so the entry states the link as weak and unstable rather than quantifying it. No published confidence index, provider or level is named. Response shares in the example are invented, and the net-balance construction shown is only one of several used by compilers.
