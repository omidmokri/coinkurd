Simple definition

Believing your estimate is more precise than it is. The forecast may still be reasonable; the confidence interval around it is too narrow.

Professional definition

Miscalibration of subjective uncertainty, usually appearing as intervals that are too tight and as an inflated sense of one's own relative skill. In trading its signature is turnover: a trader who believes their information is better than the market's will act on it more often.

Technical definition

The behavioural claim is about calibration, not about optimism: a well-calibrated forecaster who says ninety percent is right about nine times in ten, and overconfidence is the systematic shortfall against that line. Barber and Odean linked the bias to measurable cost in a US discount-brokerage sample for 1991 to 1996, where gross returns were close to a market benchmark but net returns were not, with the gap tracking turnover. That is a cost of trading frequency, not a proof of any individual's miscalibration.

Academic definition

Systematic miscalibration of subjective probability distributions, typically manifesting as excessively narrow confidence intervals and overestimation of relative ability, and empirically associated with elevated trading turnover.

Mechanism

Confidence is built from the ease of constructing a story, and a story can be built from very little. Feedback that would correct it is slow, noisy and asymmetric: wins are recalled as skill and losses as circumstance, so calibration never gets tested. Acting on an uncalibrated edge then has a fixed price, because costs are charged per trade whether the edge was real or not.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone who records forecasts with probabilities, and anyone comparing their own turnover with their measured edge per trade.
Why it matters
Calibration is checkable: write the probability down in advance and count how the stated ninety percents actually resolved.
Limitations
The brokerage evidence is about average behaviour in one sample, period and market structure. It identifies a cost channel; it does not establish that any particular trader is miscalibrated.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a run of wins as evidence of calibration.
  • Raising size after a good month rather than after a change in measured edge.
  • Recording the forecast but not the probability attached to it.

Examples

numeric

In the Barber and Odean sample, gross returns were near a market benchmark while net returns lagged it, and the most active accounts lagged by several times more than the average account. The difference is the cost of acting often.

Given
  • sample: individual accounts at a US discount brokerage, 1991 to 1996
  • gross portfolio returns close to a market index benchmark
  • net returns below the benchmark, with the shortfall increasing in turnover
Steps
  1. gap between gross and net = commissions plus the bid-ask spread paid on each round trip
  2. gap widens roughly in proportion to how often the portfolio is turned over
  3. no change in stock-picking quality is needed to produce the gap
the measured underperformance of the most active accounts is attributed to trading costs, not to worse selection

Figures are not restated here because the published magnitudes belong to one sample, one period and one market structure. The direction and the mechanism are the citable result.

everyday

Ten forecasts recorded at ninety percent confidence. If only five came true, the forecasts were not wrong so much as the confidence was: the same calls at sixty percent would have been well calibrated.

Commonly confused with

Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is how much variation in outcome you are willing to accept. Overconfidence is a mistaken belief about how much variation there is. A well-calibrated trader can still choose to take a lot of risk.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The entry deliberately does not restate the percentage figures from the 2000 paper, because they describe one brokerage sample over 1991 to 1996 and are often quoted as though they were general. What is claimed is the paper's structure of result: net returns fell short of a benchmark while gross returns did not, and the shortfall increased with turnover. The attribution of high turnover to overconfidence is the authors' interpretation, not a separately measured link. The Kurdish headword is a proposal and needs native-expert review.
