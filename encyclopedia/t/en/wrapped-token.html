Simple definition

A token on one chain that stands in for an asset held somewhere else. Its value rests entirely on whoever holds the real asset actually honouring redemptions.

Professional definition

A representation token minted against a deposit of an underlying asset and redeemable for it, used to make an asset usable on a chain where it is not native. The wrapper is a claim, so its credibility is the credibility of the custody arrangement behind it: a custodian, a multisig, a contract holding the asset natively, or a validator set.

Technical definition

What backs a wrapper is the whole content of the instrument. Three structures recur and they are not equivalent: a single custodian holding reserves with periodic attestation, a threshold multisig or committee, and a same-chain contract wrapper such as a deposit contract that holds the native asset and issues a standard token one-for-one. Only the last removes counterparty risk, and even then upgrade or admin powers in the contract reintroduce it. A wrapper trading at par proves demand, not reserves.

Academic definition

A transferable claim issued on a host ledger against a deposited reference asset, whose peg to that asset is maintained by a redemption right and is therefore contingent on the solvency, honesty and operational continuity of the custody mechanism.

Mechanism

The peg is held by arbitrage against redemption: if the wrapper trades below the underlying, someone buys it and redeems at par. Remove or gate redemption and the arbitrage stops, so the wrapper's price can detach from the asset it names while that asset is unaffected. The custody structure, not the ticker, determines what the holder owns. Wrapper risk adds to the underlying asset's own risk.

In practice

Who uses it
DeFi users bringing an asset to a chain that lacks it, protocol teams choosing which wrapper to accept as collateral, and risk teams reviewing custody and attestation.
Why it matters
It lets an asset be used as collateral, liquidity or a trading pair on a chain where it does not natively exist.
Limitations
The holder owns a claim, not the asset. Reserves, redemption rights, attestation frequency and admin powers differ by wrapper and are the actual subject of analysis.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a wrapper as interchangeable with the native asset in risk terms.
  • Reading a price at par as proof reserves are intact.
  • Assuming two wrappers of the same asset carry the same risk.

Examples

comparison

One wrapper is minted by a named custodian that publishes attestations. Another is minted by a bridge committee with a signing threshold. Both quote one-for-one; the holder of the second is exposed to that committee, not to a custodian.

numeric

If 100,000 wrapper units are outstanding, the reserve claim is 100,000 of the underlying. An attestation showing 99,400 means 0.6 percent of the claim is unbacked, and redemption is first-come under stress.

Given
  • wrapper units outstanding = 100,000
  • stated reserves = 99,400 units of the underlying
  • redemption promised one-for-one
Steps
  1. shortfall = 100,000 - 99,400 = 600 units
  2. 600 / 100,000 = 0.6 percent of the claim
0.6 percent of outstanding wrappers has no reserve behind it

Hypothetical figures. An attestation is a point-in-time statement about balances, not a continuous guarantee, and it does not establish the legal enforceability of the redemption right.

Commonly confused with

Bridged Asset

Wrapping and bridging overlap but answer different questions: wrapping is about the claim and who honours it, bridging is about moving value between chains and who validates the message. A bridged asset usually arrives as a wrapper, and then both risks apply.
