Standard Deviation

Foundation

Simple definition

A single number saying how far values typically sit from their own average. Small means the values cluster together; large means they are spread out.

Professional definition

The square root of the average squared deviation from the mean, expressed in the same units as the data. In finance it is the standard measure of dispersion of returns and the denominator of most risk-adjusted performance measures.

Technical definition

For a sample it is the square root of the sum of squared deviations divided by n-1, the Bessel correction that makes the variance estimate unbiased when the mean is itself estimated from the same data. Squaring makes the statistic sensitive to outliers, and because it is defined on a symmetric scale it describes the body of a skewed return distribution far better than its tails.

Academic definition

The square root of the variance of a random variable or sample, measuring dispersion about the mean in the original units of measurement.

Mechanism

Deviations are squared before averaging so that positive and negative departures do not cancel, and the square root restores the original units. The n-1 denominator corrects for taking deviations from an estimated mean rather than the true one: dividing by n systematically understates dispersion, and the error matters most on short samples.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone computing volatility, Sharpe ratios, confidence intervals, Bollinger Bands or position sizes from historical returns.
Why it matters
It is the common currency of dispersion: comparable across series, additive in variance, and the input every standard risk measure expects.
Limitations
It assumes dispersion is a useful summary, which fails for strongly skewed or fat-tailed data, and squaring gives a single extreme observation outsized influence.

Common mistakes

  • Using the n denominator and reporting it as the sample standard deviation.
  • Adding standard deviations instead of adding variances.
  • Quoting a figure without the period it was computed over.

Examples

everyday

Two classes average the same exam mark. In the first, nearly everyone scored close to the average; in the second, half scored very high and half very low. The averages are identical and the standard deviations are not.

numeric

Five daily returns are 2%, -1%, 3%, 0% and 1%. The mean is 1% and the squared deviations sum to 10. Dividing by n-1 gives a variance of 2.5 and a standard deviation of 1.58% per day.

Given
  • five daily returns: 2%, -1%, 3%, 0%, 1%
  • sample estimator, denominator n-1
Steps
  1. mean = (2 - 1 + 3 + 0 + 1) / 5 = 1.0%
  2. deviations: 1, -2, 2, -1, 0
  3. sum of squared deviations = 1 + 4 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 10
  4. sample variance = 10 / (5 - 1) = 2.5
  5. s = sqrt(2.5) = 1.58%
sample standard deviation = 1.58% per day

With the n denominator the same five numbers give sqrt(10/5) = 1.41%. On a five-observation sample the choice of denominator changes the answer by more than 10%.

Procedure

Take the mean of the observations. Subtract it from each observation to get the deviations, square each one, and add them up. Divide that sum by n-1, which gives the sample variance, and take the square root to return to the original units.

Worked example

Given
  • observations: 2, -1, 3, 0, 1 (percent per day)
  • n = 5
Steps
  1. x-bar = 5 / 5 = 1
  2. deviations: 1, -2, 2, -1, 0
  3. squared: 1, 4, 4, 1, 0; sum = 10
  4. s squared = 10 / 4 = 2.5
  5. s = sqrt(2.5)
s = 1.58 percent per day

Interpretation

s is in the same units as the data, so a daily return series gives a daily figure. For a roughly normal sample about two thirds of observations fall within one s of the mean, but that proportion is a property of the normal distribution and not of the statistic itself.

Assumptions

  • The n-1 (Bessel) denominator applies when the mean is estimated from the same sample, which is the normal case in finance. The population form divides by n and is correct only when the true mean is known independently.
  • Observations are treated as drawn from one distribution. Mixing two regimes into one sample produces a figure that describes neither.
  • The statistic is computed on the data as given. It does not know whether the inputs are simple returns, log returns or prices, and these give different answers on the same series.
  • Variances add for independent series, standard deviations do not. Any aggregation must be done in variance space.
Limits of the formula
  • Squared deviations make the figure dominated by the largest observations, so one crash can set the value for an entire window.
  • It is symmetric and carries no information about skew, so two assets with the same standard deviation can behave very differently on the downside.

Volatility

Standard deviation is the statistic. Volatility is the financial quantity built from it: the standard deviation of returns, annualised by a stated convention.
