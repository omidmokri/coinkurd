Simple definition

A narrowing shape whose two boundaries slope the same way: a rising wedge tilts up, a falling wedge tilts down.

Professional definition

A converging consolidation in which both boundaries slope in the same direction. That separates it from a triangle, where one boundary is flat or the two slope oppositely, and from a channel, where they run parallel. Classical reading calls a rising wedge bearish and a falling wedge bullish — that is, resolving against its own slope.

Technical definition

A wedge is the sign of two fitted slopes plus a convergence condition, which makes it more parameter-sensitive than a triangle: a small change in the pivot rule turns the same price action into a channel or a triangle instead. The contraction in range is measurable. The classical directional claim, that the shape resolves counter to its slope, is the least supported part and no statistic in this registry backs it.

Academic definition

A convergent consolidation whose bounding lines share the sign of their slope, conventionally read as resolving counter to that slope.

Mechanism

Both boundaries sloping the same way while the range narrows means the trend is still making progress, but each push covers less ground than the last. The rate of advance is falling while direction holds. That deceleration is a real measurement; the classical inference that deceleration must end in a reversal against the slope does not follow from it.

In practice

Who uses it
Chart analysts tracking trend deceleration, and detector authors who must distinguish a wedge from a channel or a triangle.
Why it matters
It reports two things at once: direction is intact, and the rate of advance together with the swing amplitude is falling.
Limitations
The classification flips with the pivot rule, the directional reading is the weakest classical claim, and no statistic in this registry supports it.

Common mistakes

  • Shorting a rising wedge purely because the shape is called bearish.
  • Fitting the boundaries to two pivots and treating the slope as established.
  • Labelling a parallel channel a wedge because it is narrow.

Examples

chart

Highs come in at 110, 114 and 116 while lows come in at 100, 108 and 113. Both lines rise, the lower one faster, so the range closes from ten points to three — a rising wedge by construction.

numeric

Over eight bars the upper boundary gains six points and the lower gains thirteen. The range contracts from ten to three. The contraction is measured; the bearish label attached to it is not.

Given
  • upper boundary: 110 at bar 1, 116 at bar 9
  • lower boundary: 100 at bar 3, 113 at bar 11
  • range measured between the fitted lines
Steps
  1. upper gain = 116 - 110 = 6 over 8 bars, slope = 0.75 per bar
  2. lower gain = 113 - 100 = 13 over 8 bars, slope = 1.625 per bar
  3. both slopes are positive and the lower is steeper, so the lines converge while rising
  4. range at the start = 10; at the end = 3
A rising wedge: range contracts from 10 to 3 while both boundaries rise.

The measurable content is the contraction and the two slopes. The classical bearish reading of a rising wedge is a convention this registry does not support with any statistic.

Commonly confused with

Triangle Pattern

In a triangle one boundary is flat or the two slope against each other. In a wedge both slope the same way, so the whole shape drifts as it narrows.
