Yield Farming

Simple definition

Moving crypto between protocols to collect the extra tokens they hand out for providing liquidity or lending. The advertised numbers are rewards for taking risk, not interest.

Professional definition

The practice of supplying capital to DeFi protocols in order to capture incentive emissions on top of the base return. Yield typically combines trading fees or borrow interest with newly issued governance tokens, and the headline rate assumes the reward token holds its value.

Technical definition

A return built from three separable components: a genuine economic return such as pool fees or borrow spread; an emissions component paid in a token the protocol creates at will; and a mark-to-market change on the supplied assets themselves, which includes divergence loss for pool positions. Quoted APY usually annualises a current emission rate and ignores dilution, exit liquidity for the reward token, and the correlation between falling token prices and falling incentives.

Academic definition

The allocation of capital across decentralised protocols to harvest protocol-issued incentives in addition to intrinsic returns, where the realised return depends jointly on emissions, asset prices and contract integrity.

Mechanism

Emissions are a subsidy paid to attract capital, so the rate is highest exactly where the protocol has least organic demand. As farmers arrive, the same emission is split across more capital and the quoted rate falls, while selling pressure from harvested rewards pushes the reward token down. The high number is therefore self-cancelling by construction, and what remains is the base fee income and the risk.

In practice

Who uses it
Liquidity providers, DeFi-focused funds, and protocol teams designing incentive programmes.
Why it matters
Early emissions can pay far more than base fees, and providing liquidity is one of the few on-chain activities with a measurable revenue line.
Limitations
The advertised rate is not a yield on a deposit. It can be diluted, cut by governance, or exceeded by divergence loss, reward-token depreciation, gas costs and contract failure — and all of these tend to arrive together.

Common mistakes

  • Annualising a current emission rate as if it were contractual.
  • Counting rewards at grant price rather than at the price actually realised.
  • Chasing the highest rate into the least audited contract.

Examples

numeric

A 10,000 position is offered a 50% annual rate in emissions. Rewards arrive worth 5,000 at grant, but the reward token loses 60% before they are sold, leaving 2,000; divergence loss of 5.72% costs 572 and gas 50. The realised return is about 13.8%, not 50%.

Given
  • Capital supplied = 10,000
  • Advertised emission rate = 50% per year
  • Reward token falls 60% between grant and sale
  • Pool price ratio moves by a factor of 2, giving 5.72% divergence loss
  • Gas and transaction costs = 50
  • Illustrative assumptions only
Steps
  1. Rewards at grant = 10,000 x 0.50 = 5,000
  2. Rewards realised = 5,000 x 0.40 = 2,000
  3. Divergence loss = 10,000 x 0.0572 = 572
  4. Net = 2,000 - 572 - 50 = 1,378
  5. Return = 1,378 / 10,000
About 13.8% realised against a 50% headline

Pool fees earned are not included and would add to the result. The point is that the headline rate is one of four terms, and the other three are not fixed.

market

A pool pays an eye-catching rate on its first weekend and a fraction of it a month later. Nothing failed: capital arrived, the same emission was divided more thinly, and the reward token had been sold by every earlier participant.

Commonly confused with

Staking

Staking bonds a network's own token to secure consensus. Yield farming supplies capital to applications in exchange for incentive tokens.
