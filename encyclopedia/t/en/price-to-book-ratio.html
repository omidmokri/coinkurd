Price-to-Book Ratio

ENGLISHPrice-to-Book Ratioreference

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Professional

Simple definition

The share price divided by the accounting value of the company's own capital per share. Above 1 means the market values the business for more than its recorded net assets.

Professional definition

Market price per share over book value of equity per share. It compares what buyers will pay with what the accounts say the owners' stake is worth, so it is most informative where assets are carried near their economic value, as in banks and insurers.

Technical definition

A ratio of a market price to a historical-cost accounting residual. Book equity excludes most internally generated intangibles and is reduced by buybacks and write-downs, so asset-light and research-intensive firms carry structurally high ratios with no implication about value. Under a residual-income view the justified ratio rises with the spread of return on equity over the cost of equity, which is why price-to-book and return on equity must be read together.

Academic definition

The ratio of market capitalisation to the book value of shareholders' equity, interpretable as an increasing function of the excess of return on equity over the required return on equity.

Mechanism

Book equity is what the accounts recorded; market price is what buyers will pay for the earnings that equity can produce. The ratio measures the premium the market puts on the firm's ability to earn more than its cost of capital. Where accounting omits the real assets, it drifts far above one for reasons that are not valuation.

In practice

Who uses it
Bank and insurance analysts, value-oriented screeners, liquidation and distressed analysts, and anyone valuing a firm whose earnings are temporarily absent.
Why it matters
Book equity is more stable than earnings, so the ratio still works when profit is negative and a price-to-earnings ratio cannot be computed.
Limitations
Book value is an accounting number at historical cost; it ignores most internal intangibles and is distorted by buybacks, revaluations and impairment timing.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a software firm expensive on price-to-book.
  • Reading a ratio below one as a free asset without checking for impairments.
  • Comparing price-to-book without comparing return on equity.

Examples

market

A bank whose loan book is carried close to realisable value trades near one times book, while a software firm whose main assets are code and customer relationships that were expensed as incurred trades at many times book. The gap is mostly an accounting artefact.

numeric

Shareholders' equity is 700 across 40 shares, so book value per share is 17.5. At a price of 56 the price-to-book ratio is 3.2 — the market pays 3.2 times the recorded owners' stake.

Given
  • Shareholders' equity (book) = 700
  • Shares outstanding = 40
  • Market price per share = 56
Steps
  1. Book value per share = 700 / 40 = 17.5
  2. P/B = 56 / 17.5 = 3.2
P/B = 3.2

If goodwill of 300 were excluded, tangible book value per share would be 10 and the ratio 5.6. The variant must be stated whenever the figure is quoted.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Take shareholders' equity attributable to ordinary shares from the balance sheet, divide by shares outstanding to get book value per share, then divide the market price by that figure. Decide explicitly whether goodwill and other intangibles are included, and apply the same choice to every company being compared.

Worked example

Given
  • Total assets = 1,900
  • Total liabilities = 1,200
  • Ordinary shares outstanding = 40
  • Market price per share = 56
Steps
  1. Book equity = 1,900 - 1,200 = 700
  2. BVPS = 700 / 40 = 17.5
  3. P/B = 56 / 17.5
P/B = 3.2

Interpretation

A ratio near one says the market expects the firm to earn about its cost of equity. Well above one says it expects a durable excess return, or that the accounts understate the assets. Below one says it expects destruction of capital, or doubts the carrying values.

Assumptions

  • Book equity is measured at historical cost under the reporting framework in use; revaluation models, impairment timing and lease capitalisation all change it without any economic event.
  • Variants are not interchangeable: total book value, tangible book value excluding goodwill and intangibles, and book value per share adjusted for buybacks give materially different ratios.
  • The residual-income reading of the justified ratio assumes clean surplus accounting and a stable cost of equity.
Limits of the formula
  • Structurally high for firms whose main assets are research, brands or software that accounting expensed rather than capitalised.
  • A ratio below one is not evidence of a bargain; it often signals that the market disbelieves the carrying value of the assets.

Commonly confused with

Book Value

Book value is the accounting residual itself, in currency. Price-to-book is the multiple the market pays on it. One is a balance-sheet figure, the other a market judgement about that figure.
