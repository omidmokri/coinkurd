Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The total a business charges customers for goods or services it has actually delivered in a period, before any costs are subtracted. It is the top line of the income statement.

Professional definition

Inflow recognised from ordinary activities — goods sold, services performed, fees earned — stated net of discounts and returns. The trigger is delivery of the promised performance, not receipt of cash, so revenue and collection routinely differ in timing.

Technical definition

Under IFRS 15 revenue is recognised as the performance obligations in a contract are satisfied, with the transaction price allocated across obligations by standalone selling price. Amount and timing therefore rest on judgements: when control transfers, how variable consideration is estimated, whether the entity is principal or agent. Two firms with identical cash receipts can lawfully report very different revenue. It is a measured estimate, not an observed fact.

Academic definition

The gross inflow of economic benefits from an entity's ordinary operating activities in a reporting period, recognised upon satisfaction of contractual performance obligations rather than upon cash receipt.

Mechanism

Revenue is an accrual measure. The accounting question is whether a promise to the customer has been kept, so cash can arrive a year early as a prepayment or a year late as a receivable. That gap between performance and payment is where revenue becomes a judgement — and where aggressive recognition and gross-versus-net presentation games live.

In practice

Who uses it
Equity analysts, auditors, lenders and anyone comparing growth across companies or rebuilding a firm's unit economics.
Why it matters
It sizes the business and anchors most valuation multiples, and it is the line almost every margin is measured against.
Limitations
Recognition policy, gross-versus-net presentation and currency translation all move the number. Revenue says nothing about whether those sales earned anything.

Common mistakes

  • Reading revenue growth as cash growth.
  • Comparing a gross-revenue marketplace with a net-revenue one.
  • Treating a one-off contract milestone as a run rate.

Examples

market

A software firm signs a three-year contract and collects the whole fee upfront. Cash jumps this quarter; revenue is spread across twelve quarters, and the unperformed part sits on the balance sheet as deferred revenue, which is a liability.

numeric

A 1,200 annual subscription is billed and collected on 1 January. By 31 March only three months of service have been delivered, so recognised revenue is 300 and the remaining 900 is deferred revenue, not revenue.

Given
  • Contract price 1,200 for 12 months of service
  • Cash collected in full on 1 January
  • Service delivered evenly; reporting date 31 March
Steps
  1. Monthly revenue = 1,200 / 12 = 100
  2. Months performed by 31 March = 3
  3. Revenue = 3 x 100 = 300
  4. Deferred revenue = 1,200 - 300 = 900
Revenue 300, deferred revenue 900, cash collected 1,200

All three figures describe the same contract. Quoting the cash as revenue would overstate the quarter fourfold.

Mathematical foundation

Revenue = \sum_{i} P_i \times Q_i

Variables

P_iNet realised price of product or service line icurrency per unit
Q_iQuantity of line i delivered in the periodunits

Procedure

Split recognised revenue into the lines that produced it, take each line's net realised price and the quantity actually delivered in the period, multiply, and sum. Compare the decomposition across periods to see whether growth came from price or from volume.

Worked example

Given
  • Line A: 10,000 units delivered at a net price of 25
  • Line B: 2,000 units delivered at a net price of 90
Steps
  1. Line A = 10,000 x 25 = 250,000
  2. Line B = 2,000 x 90 = 180,000
  3. Revenue = 250,000 + 180,000
Revenue = 430,000

Interpretation

The decomposition explains why revenue changed — price, mix or volume — but it does not decide what may be recognised. That is set by the accounting standard, not by arithmetic.

Assumptions

  • This is an analytical decomposition of revenue already recognised, not the recognition rule. What may be recognised, and when, is set by IFRS 15 or the equivalent national framework.
  • Prices are net of discounts, rebates, returns and allowances. Gross bookings, billings and gross merchandise value are different quantities and are not revenue.
  • Price and quantity must be measured over the same period and in one currency; translation of foreign sales changes the total without changing anything operational.
Limits of the formula
  • Breaks down for bundled contracts where no single unit price exists and the transaction price has to be allocated by judgement.
  • Says nothing about whether the revenue was profitable or will ever be collected.

Commonly confused with

Free Cash Flow

Revenue is what was earned under accrual rules; free cash flow is what was left in cash after capital spending. A company can grow revenue every year and never generate cash.

Commonly confused with

Protocol Revenue

Company revenue is recognised under an accounting standard and audited. Protocol revenue is a measurement convention applied to on-chain fees, with no standard setter and no audit.
