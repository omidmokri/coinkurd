Expected Value

Quantitative Finance

Foundation

Simple definition

The average result you would get if you could repeat a decision many times: each outcome multiplied by its chance, all added together.

Professional definition

The probability-weighted mean of the possible outcomes of a random variable. In trading it is the expectancy of a rule set per trade: win rate times average win, minus loss rate times average loss, after costs.

Technical definition

A first moment, and therefore a statement about long-run averages under repetition rather than about any single realisation. It requires the probabilities and outcome sizes to be stable and the number of trials to be large enough for the average to settle. A positive expectancy with a fat-tailed loss distribution can still ruin an account sized too large to survive the path.

Academic definition

The probability-weighted mean of a random variable's outcomes, the first moment of its distribution, equal to the limit of the sample average under repeated independent trials.

Mechanism

Expectation is linear, so the expected value of a sequence of independent bets is the sum of their expected values. That is why a small positive edge compounds given enough repetitions, and why sizing is a separate question: expected value says nothing about the variance of the path, and a path that touches zero ends the sequence.

In practice

Who uses it
Strategy developers evaluating a rule set, risk managers sizing bets, and anyone comparing a high win rate with a high payoff ratio.
Why it matters
It is the only figure that combines how often a rule wins with how much it wins, so it exposes a high win rate that still loses money.
Limitations
Estimated probabilities come from a finite past. If the win rate or the payoff drifts, yesterday's expectancy describes a strategy that no longer exists.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a strategy by win rate without the average win and loss.
  • Computing expectancy before fees, funding and slippage.
  • Assuming a positive expectancy guarantees survival at any position size.

Examples

market

One rule wins seven trades in ten but takes small profits and large losses, so it bleeds. Another wins three in ten with payoffs three times its losses, and it gains. Win rate alone ranked them the wrong way round.

numeric

A rule wins 40% of the time, averaging 2.0R on winners and losing 1.0R on losers. Expectancy is 0.20R before costs. Take off 0.05R of fees and slippage and the edge is 0.15R per trade, on average, over many trades.

Given
  • win rate p = 0.40
  • average win = 2.0R
  • average loss = 1.0R (entered as a positive magnitude)
  • round-trip costs = 0.05R per trade
  • R is the unit of risk per trade
Steps
  1. EV before costs = 0.40 x 2.0 - 0.60 x 1.0
  2. = 0.80 - 0.60 = 0.20R
  3. EV after costs = 0.20 - 0.05 = 0.15R
expected value = 0.15R per trade after costs

A 0.15R edge is an average over many trades. It says nothing about the next trade, nor about the depth of the drawdown on the way to the average.

Mathematical foundation

\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i x_i, \qquad \text{Expectancy} = p \cdot \overline{W} - (1 - p) \cdot \overline{L}

Procedure

List every outcome and its probability, check that the probabilities sum to one, multiply each outcome by its probability and add the products. For a two-outcome trading rule the same calculation collapses to win rate times average win minus loss rate times average loss, with costs subtracted inside the outcome values.

Worked example

Given
  • p = 0.40
  • average win = 2.0R
  • average loss = 1.0R
  • costs = 0.05R per trade
Steps
  1. 0.40 x 2.0 = 0.80
  2. 0.60 x 1.0 = 0.60
  3. 0.80 - 0.60 = 0.20R
  4. 0.20 - 0.05 = 0.15R
expectancy = 0.15R per trade after costs

Interpretation

The result is a per-trial average in the units of the outcomes. Positive means the rule gains on average over many repetitions. It is not a forecast of the next trial and not a measure of how rough the path will be.

Assumptions

  • The probabilities p_i are known and sum to one, and the outcome set is exhaustive. In trading they are estimated from a past sample and are themselves uncertain.
  • The two-outcome expectancy form collapses a whole distribution of results into one average win and one average loss, which hides skew: a rule carried by a single enormous winner has a very different path from one with many moderate winners, at identical expectancy.
  • Trials are treated as independent and identically distributed. Correlated trades -- one idea expressed in several positions -- break this and make the realised average far noisier than the formula suggests.
  • Costs, funding and slippage must be inside the outcome values. Expectancy computed on gross outcomes is systematically overstated.
Limits of the formula
  • It is a first moment only. Two rules with identical expected value can have completely different variance, drawdown and risk of ruin, which is why position sizing is a separate decision.
  • Convergence to the expected value needs many independent trials. Over a few dozen trades the realised average is dominated by noise, so a short record neither confirms nor refutes an edge.
  • Research on decision-making under risk finds that people systematically do not act on expected value, weighting losses more heavily than equivalent gains, so the figure describes the bet rather than the behaviour.

Commonly confused with

Win Rate

Win rate says how often a rule wins. Expected value combines that with how much it wins and loses, so a rule can have a high win rate and a negative expected value.

The formula is standard probability and not in dispute. The entry deliberately separates expectancy from survival: a positive expected value says nothing about risk of ruin, and that distinction is stated in math.limits rather than left implicit. The behavioural point is attributed to Kahneman and Tversky (1979) as evidence about choices, not as a claim about the mathematics.
