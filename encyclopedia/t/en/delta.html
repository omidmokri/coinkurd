Simple definition

How much an option's price moves when the underlying moves one unit. A delta of 0.5 means roughly half a unit of option value per unit of underlying.

Professional definition

The first partial derivative of option value with respect to the underlying price, ∂V/∂S. It runs from 0 to 1 for a long call and from −1 to 0 for a long put, and it is the hedge ratio: the quantity of underlying that offsets the option's directional exposure locally.

Technical definition

A model-derived local sensitivity, exact only in the limit of an infinitesimal move. Under Black-Scholes a call's delta is N(d1) and a put's is N(d1)−1. Delta is not the probability of finishing in the money: that quantity is N(d2) under the same model, and it is a risk-neutral probability rather than a real-world one. Because delta itself changes with the underlying, with volatility and with time, a delta hedge is valid only until the next move.

Academic definition

The first-order sensitivity of a contingent claim's value to the price of its underlying, equal under the Black-Scholes framework to the standard normal distribution function evaluated at d1.

Mechanism

Delta is a slope. Far below a call's strike the premium barely reacts to the underlying, so the slope is near zero; far above it the option behaves almost like the asset itself and the slope approaches one. In between the slope changes continuously, and the rate of that change is gamma, which is why a single delta number is a photograph rather than a rule.

In practice

Who uses it
Market makers hedging an inventory of options, portfolio managers measuring real directional exposure, and desks that quote strikes by delta rather than by price.
Why it matters
It converts a non-linear position into an equivalent quantity of the underlying, which is the only way to aggregate options with spot and futures in one risk number.
Limitations
It is a local first-order number from a model. It is wrong for large moves, decays in reliability near expiry, and changes if the volatility input changes.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a 0.30 delta as a 30% chance of expiring in the money.
  • Holding a delta hedge unchanged through a large move.
  • Comparing deltas across underlyings without adjusting for notional size.

Examples

market

A desk long 10 calls of delta 0.45 on a 100-unit contract is long about 450 units of the underlying. Selling 450 units makes the book delta neutral — until the underlying moves and the delta is no longer 0.45.

numeric

An option with delta 0.45 and the underlying up 2 gains roughly 0.90 of premium. The actual gain differs because delta rose during the move; the first-order estimate understates a long call and overstates a long put.

Given
  • delta = 0.45
  • change in underlying = +2
  • all other inputs held fixed
Steps
  1. first-order change = 0.45 x 2 = 0.90
approximately +0.90 of premium per unit of underlying exposure

This is a linear approximation. Adding the gamma term gives a second-order estimate; neither is exact for a large move.

Procedure

Delta is defined as the partial derivative of option value with respect to the underlying, everything else held fixed. In the Black-Scholes framework it has a closed form: compute d1 from the inputs above and evaluate the standard normal distribution function at it for a call, then subtract one for the put on the same strike and expiry.

Worked example

Given
  • delta of a long call = 0.45
  • position: 10 contracts, multiplier 100
  • underlying moves +2
Steps
  1. delta-equivalent exposure = 0.45 x 10 x 100 = 450 units
  2. first-order P&L = 450 x 2 = 900
  3. hedge = sell 450 units of the underlying
450 units of delta-equivalent exposure; a first-order gain of 900 on a +2 move

Interpretation

Delta answers one question: how does this position behave against the next small move? It is a hedge ratio first and an exposure measure second. Any probability reading is borrowed from a model and is not what the derivative measures.

Assumptions

  • Delta as a number, not as a definition, is a model output. The closed forms above are Black-Scholes and inherit its assumptions: continuous trading, no frictions, a constant volatility and lognormal price dynamics.
  • Stated for European-style options on an underlying with no dividend or carry. A yield q multiplies the call delta by e^{-qT} and changes the put accordingly.
  • The hedge interpretation assumes continuous rebalancing. In discrete time a delta hedge leaves residual risk whose size grows with gamma and with the move.
  • The probability reading is not an identity. Under Black-Scholes the risk-neutral probability of a call finishing in the money is N(d2), not N(d1), and N(d2) is a risk-neutral quantity that need not equal any real-world frequency.
Limits of the formula
  • First order only: for a large move the delta estimate is systematically wrong, and the error is signed by gamma.
  • Near expiry the delta of an at-the-money option becomes unstable, flipping between extremes on small moves, which makes mechanical delta hedging expensive precisely when it matters.
  • Delta depends on the volatility input, so two desks using different volatilities will hedge the same contract with different quantities.
