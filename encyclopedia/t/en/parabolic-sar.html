Parabolic SAR

Simple definition

A dot that trails price and jumps to the other side when price touches it. It marks where a mechanical trailing stop would sit next.

Professional definition

Wilder's stop-and-reverse system. Each period the dot moves a fraction of the way toward the most extreme price reached in the current trend. That fraction, the acceleration factor, steps up every time a new extreme is made, so the dot tightens faster the longer the trend runs. When price touches the dot, the position flips.

Technical definition

The recursion adds the acceleration factor times the remaining distance to the trend's extreme point. The factor starts at 0.02, steps by 0.02 at each new extreme and is capped at 0.20. On a reversal the new stop is set to the prior trend's extreme and the factor resets. Because the dot accelerates regardless of volatility and the system is always in the market, a sideways range produces continuous reversals. Wilder also clamps the long stop below the previous two lows.

Academic definition

A recursive trailing-stop function that converges on the extreme price of the prevailing trend at a geometrically increasing rate and reverses the position on contact.

Mechanism

The dot is a convergence rule, not a forecast. Each step closes a fixed proportion of the remaining gap to the trend's extreme, and raising that proportion after every new extreme makes the stop tighten faster the longer the trend persists. The cost is structural: in a sideways market the dot catches price quickly in both directions, so the system reverses repeatedly with no trend to pay for it.

In practice

Who uses it
Trend-following system developers, and traders who want a fully mechanical trailing stop with no discretionary input.
Why it matters
It produces one completely determined stop level per bar, which tightens automatically as a trend extends.
Limitations
It is always in the market, so it reverses continuously in a range; the acceleration parameters are arbitrary defaults; and it ignores volatility entirely, unlike an ATR-based stop.

Common mistakes

  • Trading every flip in a sideways market.
  • Changing the acceleration step and then citing default-parameter studies.
  • Reading the dot as a target rather than a stop.

Examples

market

In a sustained advance the dots sit well below price at first and close in as new highs accumulate, until one shallow pullback touches them and the system flips short at the top of the move it just rode.

numeric

In a long trend the stop sits at 100 with the highest high at 110 and the factor at 0.04, so next bar it rises to 100.40. A new high at 112 steps the factor to 0.06 and the following stop becomes about 101.10.

Given
  • long position, current SAR = 100
  • extreme point (highest high so far) = 110
  • acceleration factor = 0.04
  • then a new high at 112 raises the factor by the 0.02 step
Steps
  1. next SAR = 100 + 0.04 x (110 - 100) = 100 + 0.40 = 100.40
  2. new high 112 makes EP = 112 and AF = 0.06
  3. following SAR = 100.40 + 0.06 x (112 - 100.40) = 100.40 + 0.696 = 101.096
The stop advances from 100 to 100.40, then to about 101.10 as the factor steps up.

Wilder's clamp still applies: the long stop may not be set above the lower of the previous two lows, which can override the value computed here.

Mathematical foundation

SAR_{t+1} = SAR_t + AF_t \left( EP_t - SAR_t \right), \qquad AF \in \{0.02,\, 0.04,\, \dots,\, 0.20\}

Variables

SAR_tCurrent stop-and-reverse levelprice units
EP_tExtreme point: highest high of the current long trend, or lowest low of the current short trendprice units
AF_tAcceleration factor: starts at 0.02, steps by 0.02 at each new extreme, capped at 0.20ratio (dimensionless)

Procedure

While long, each bar set the next stop to the current stop plus the factor times the distance from the stop to the highest high of the trend. Step the factor up by 0.02 only when a new high is made, never past 0.20. Do not place the stop above the lower of the previous two lows. If price trades at or below the stop, reverse: the new stop becomes the trend's highest high, the factor resets to 0.02, and the extreme point becomes the new trend's lowest low. Mirror everything for a short.

Worked example

Given
  • long position, SAR = 100
  • EP = 110
  • AF = 0.04
  • then a new high prints at 112
Steps
  1. next SAR = 100 + 0.04 x (110 - 100) = 100.40
  2. new high 112: EP = 112, AF = 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.06
  3. following SAR = 100.40 + 0.06 x (112 - 100.40) = 101.096
SAR = 100.40, then approximately 101.10.

Interpretation

The value is a stop level for the next bar, nothing else. Its distance from price shrinks as the trend extends, which means the system gives back less of a long trend and is stopped out earlier in a choppy one.

Assumptions

  • Wilder's defaults are a starting factor of 0.02, a step of 0.02 and a maximum of 0.20. All three are exposed by charting platforms, and changing any of them changes every value in the series.
  • The factor steps up only when a new extreme is made in the current trend, not on every bar.
  • Wilder's clamp places the long stop no higher than the lower of the previous two lows, and the short stop no lower than the higher of the previous two highs. Implementations differ in whether they apply it, which is a real source of disagreement between platforms.
  • On a reversal the new stop starts at the previous trend's extreme point, so the first stop after a flip can be far from price.
Limits of the formula
  • The system is always in the market: there is no flat state, so a sideways range produces an uninterrupted stream of reversals and costs.
  • No volatility input enters the recursion, so one parameter set behaves very differently across instruments and timeframes, unlike an ATR-scaled stop.
  • Because the recursion depends on where the trend was declared to start, two platforms with different history or different clamp handling can show different dots on the same chart.

Commonly confused with

Trailing Stop

A trailing stop is any rule that moves a stop in one direction only. Parabolic SAR is one specific accelerating rule that also reverses the position on contact.
