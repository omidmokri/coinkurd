Simple definition

The rule that a broker or platform must not trade ahead of a customer's order to profit from the price move that order will cause.

Professional definition

A conduct prohibition grounded in order-handling duty: an intermediary entrusted with a client order must not deal for itself or a related party on the strength of that knowledge before the client's order is executed. The wrong is the misuse of information held in confidence, not the fact of trading early.

Technical definition

The duty attaches to a relationship, which is what distinguishes the offence from the mempool behaviour of the same name. On a public chain the pending order is visible to all by design, no one is entrusted with it, and the contested resource is transaction ordering rather than confidential information, so conduct that is economically similar may breach no order-handling duty anywhere. Detection in the regulated case uses timestamped audit trails comparing proprietary activity against client-order windows.

Academic definition

The prohibition on an intermediary dealing in advance of a client order on the basis of information held in a fiduciary or equivalent capacity, distinct from pre-execution transparency in permissionless ledgers where no such relationship exists.

Mechanism

An intermediary is given an informational advantage only so that it can execute. Using it for its own account converts a service into an extraction, and the client pays in a worse fill without ever seeing why. The rule therefore polices a relationship rather than a pattern of trades, which is exactly why it does not transplant cleanly onto a public mempool, where no one was entrusted with anything.

In practice

Who uses it
Brokers, execution desks, platform operators handling client flow, compliance monitoring and supervisors reviewing audit trails.
Why it matters
It underpins client-order priority, information barriers and personal-account dealing policies inside intermediaries.
Limitations
The prohibition requires a relationship and a regime. Whether it reaches a crypto platform depends on jurisdiction and on how the platform's role is characterised; the on-chain equivalent may be governed by nothing.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the on-chain behaviour and the regulated offence are the same thing.
  • Thinking an automated system removes the duty a firm owes its clients.
  • Expecting on-chain mitigations such as private routing to answer a conduct breach.

Examples

regulatory

A desk receives a large client order and buys for its own book first. On a public chain, an unrelated searcher sees the same intention in the mempool. The first is a breach of duty; the second breaches no confidence, because none existed.

numeric

A review compares timestamps: twenty client orders a day give 1,200 seconds of pre-order windows in a 23,400-second session, about five per cent. Finding forty per cent of proprietary fills inside them is eight times the chance rate.

Given
  • 20 client orders per session
  • 60-second window examined before each client order
  • Session length = 23,400 seconds
  • Observed share of proprietary fills inside those windows = 40%
Steps
  1. Window time = 20 x 60 = 1,200 seconds
  2. Chance share = 1,200 / 23,400 = 5.1%
  3. Concentration ratio = 40% / 5.1% = 7.8x
Proprietary fills concentrated about eight times above the chance rate

A surveillance screening statistic with invented inputs, not a legal test and not a method for anything. Concentration selects an account for human review; legitimate explanations exist and must be considered.

JURISDICTION WARNING: rules differ by country and change. Whether a duty exists, who owes it, and whether a crypto platform is characterised as an intermediary at all are matters of each jurisdiction's own law; no statute, rule or case is cited, because none is in the closed source registry. Whether the on-chain form of the behaviour is unlawful anywhere is unsettled and differs by country: this entry states only that it breaches no order-handling duty where no such relationship exists. See the separate front-running entry for the on-chain phenomenon. The surveillance statistic uses invented inputs and is a screening metric, not a legal test; the entry contains no operational detail.
