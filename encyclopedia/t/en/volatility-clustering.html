Volatility Clustering

Simple definition

Big moves tend to be followed by big moves and quiet days by quiet days. The direction stays unpredictable; the size of the moves does not.

Professional definition

The empirical regularity that the magnitude of returns is positively autocorrelated even when the returns themselves are not. Squared or absolute returns show slowly decaying autocorrelation, so current variance carries information about the variance of the next period.

Technical definition

Persistence in the conditional second moment of a return series. Returns can be close to serially uncorrelated in the mean while their squares are strongly dependent, which is consistent with a martingale and inconsistent with an independent-increment random walk. It implies that unconditional distributions are fat-tailed as a mixture of normals with changing scale, and that risk scaled by the square root of time is wrong at some horizons.

Academic definition

Positive serial dependence in the conditional variance of returns, such that squared or absolute returns exhibit persistent autocorrelation despite approximately uncorrelated levels.

Mechanism

News arrives in bursts and is absorbed over time rather than instantly, while leverage, margin and risk limits make participants react to realised losses by trading more. Both channels make today's turbulence raise tomorrow's expected turbulence. Because direction is competed away but the scale of uncertainty is not, the mean can be unforecastable while the variance is highly forecastable.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk managers sizing exposure, option traders, anyone setting stop distances from recent volatility, and econometricians specifying variance models.
Why it matters
It makes near-term risk partly forecastable, which is the single most reliable statistical regularity used in position sizing and margin setting.
Limitations
The clustering is in magnitude, not direction. Knowing that volatility will be high says nothing about which way the move will go.

Common mistakes

  • Treating volatility measured in a calm month as the risk of the next month.
  • Scaling risk by the square root of time across a regime change.
  • Reading clustering as a directional signal.

Examples

market

After a sharp sell-off, daily ranges stay wide for weeks even on days with no news. Direction over that stretch is a coin flip, but a position sized for the quiet period before it is now carrying several times the intended risk.

numeric

Under a simple ARCH(1) rule with base variance 0.00002 and a coefficient of 0.3, a minus 3% day lifts the next day's conditional standard deviation to about 1.70%, against a quiet-state level of about 0.45%.

Given
  • omega = 0.00002
  • alpha = 0.3
  • yesterday's shock = -0.03, so squared shock = 0.0009
Steps
  1. conditional variance = 0.00002 + 0.3 x 0.0009 = 0.00002 + 0.00027 = 0.00029
  2. conditional sd = sqrt(0.00029) = 0.01703
  3. quiet-state sd = sqrt(0.00002) = 0.00447
conditional sd about 1.70% versus about 0.45% in the quiet state

Parameters are illustrative. In ARCH the effect decays after one period; the persistence seen in real series needs the lagged-variance term that GARCH adds.

Mathematical foundation

\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, \varepsilon_{t-1}^2, \qquad \omega > 0,\ \ 0 \le \alpha < 1

Variables

\sigma_t^2Conditional variance of the return at time t, given information up to t-1squared return
\omegaConstant floor of the conditional variancesquared return
\alphaWeight on the most recent squared shock; sets how strongly yesterday's move raises today's variancedimensionless
\varepsilon_{t-1}Previous period's return innovation (return minus its conditional mean)return

Procedure

Fit a mean equation first and keep its residuals. Test whether squared residuals are autocorrelated; if they are, estimate the variance equation by maximum likelihood. Report the coefficients with the sample period, because they are not stable across regimes.

Worked example

Given
  • omega = 0.00002
  • alpha = 0.3
  • epsilon_(t-1) = -0.03
Steps
  1. epsilon^2 = 0.0009
  2. sigma_t^2 = 0.00002 + 0.3 x 0.0009 = 0.00029
  3. sigma_t = 0.01703
conditional standard deviation about 1.70%

Interpretation

A positive alpha is the formal content of clustering: the size of the last move, not its sign, raises expected risk. The model says nothing about direction, so it informs position size and never entry.

Assumptions

  • ARCH(1) is the simplest specification that produces clustering; one lag decays too quickly to match observed persistence, which is why GARCH adds a lagged variance term.
  • Alpha below one is needed for a finite unconditional variance in this form.
  • The variance equation is symmetric in the sign of the shock. Asymmetric variants in the wider literature allow negative returns to raise variance more, and this entry does not take a position on which fits better.
Limits of the formula
  • Clustering is a statement about conditional magnitude only; nothing about it supports a directional forecast.
  • Estimated coefficients shift with the sample and the frequency, so a variance forecast from one period should not be carried into a different market regime unchanged.

Commonly confused with

Autocorrelation

Plain autocorrelation of returns is dependence in direction and is weak in liquid markets. Clustering is autocorrelation in squared returns, which is strong. A series can have the second without the first.
