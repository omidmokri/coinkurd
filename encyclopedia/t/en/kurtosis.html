Kurtosis

Simple definition

A number measuring how much of a distribution's variation comes from rare extreme values rather than ordinary ones. High kurtosis means occasional shocks dominate.

Professional definition

The fourth standardised moment. The normal distribution has kurtosis exactly 3, so practitioners usually quote excess kurtosis, which is kurtosis minus 3 and is zero for the normal. Positive excess kurtosis — the usual finding for asset returns — means heavier tails than the normal model allows.

Technical definition

The ratio of the fourth central moment to the squared variance. It is driven by the fourth power of deviations, so the largest few observations in a sample determine almost all of it. For distributions with tail index below 4 the population fourth moment is infinite, in which case a sample value is not an estimate of anything and grows with sample size instead of converging.

Academic definition

The fourth standardised central moment of a distribution, equal to 3 for the Gaussian law; its excess form subtracts 3 so that the Gaussian case is the zero point.

Mechanism

Raising deviations to the fourth power weights the extremes enormously: an observation ten times the typical deviation contributes ten thousand times as much as a typical one. Kurtosis therefore measures tail weight, not the peakedness of the middle, which is the most common misreading of it.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk managers validating a distributional assumption, option traders pricing wings, and anyone deciding whether a standard-deviation-based risk figure is usable.
Why it matters
A high excess kurtosis is the cheapest available warning that a normal-based risk model will understate large losses on this series.
Limitations
The statistic is dominated by a handful of observations, so it is unstable, and it may not converge at all when the underlying fourth moment is infinite.

Common mistakes

  • Reading kurtosis as peakedness of the centre rather than tail weight.
  • Comparing a raw kurtosis of 3.2 with an excess kurtosis of 3.2.
  • Reporting a kurtosis estimate without the sample size.

Examples

market

Two daily return series both show 1% standard deviation. One has excess kurtosis near zero, the other near eight. The second will produce multi-standard-deviation days that the first essentially never does, on the same measured volatility.

numeric

Five results: plus 1, plus 1, plus 1, plus 1, minus 4. The fourth moment is 52 and the squared second moment is 16, so kurtosis is 3.25 and excess kurtosis is 0.25 — barely above normal, because one outlier in five is not yet a heavy tail.

Given
  • observations: +1, +1, +1, +1, -4
  • n = 5
  • raw (Pearson) kurtosis using population moments
Steps
  1. mean = 0
  2. m2 = (1+1+1+1+16)/5 = 4
  3. m4 = (1+1+1+1+256)/5 = 260/5 = 52
  4. kurtosis = m4 / m2^2 = 52 / 16
  5. excess = 3.25 - 3
kurtosis = 3.25, excess kurtosis = 0.25

Verified numerically. This entry reports raw kurtosis and labels the excess form explicitly. Many statistical packages print excess kurtosis under the plain label 'kurtosis', which is the most frequent source of error in comparing published figures.

Mathematical foundation

\beta_2 = \frac{E\!\left[(X-\mu)^4\right]}{\sigma^4}, \qquad \gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3

Procedure

Compute the mean and subtract it from every observation. Average the fourth powers of the deviations to get the fourth moment, and the squares to get the variance. Divide the fourth moment by the squared variance. Then decide and state whether you are reporting this raw figure or the excess figure three units lower, because the same data supports both labels and they are routinely confused.

Worked example

Given
  • observations +1, +1, +1, +1, -4
  • n = 5
  • raw Pearson kurtosis
Steps
  1. mean = 0
  2. m2 = 20/5 = 4
  3. m4 = 260/5 = 52
  4. m2^2 = 16
  5. beta2 = 52/16 = 3.25
  6. gamma2 = 3.25 - 3
raw kurtosis 3.25, excess kurtosis 0.25

Interpretation

Read it against 3 if raw, against 0 if excess, and never without knowing which. A high value says the distribution's variation is concentrated in a few extreme observations, which is a direct warning about the reliability of any standard-deviation-based risk figure computed from the same series.

Assumptions

  • This entry uses raw Pearson kurtosis, for which the normal reference value is 3; the excess convention subtracts 3 and is what most statistical software reports under the plain name.
  • A bias-corrected sample estimator also exists and differs from both forms in small samples, so three numbers can legitimately be called 'the kurtosis' of one dataset.
  • The population fourth moment must be finite for the sample statistic to estimate anything; with a tail index below 4 it is not.
Limits of the formula
  • Kurtosis does not measure peakedness. A distribution can have a flat centre and extreme tails and still report high kurtosis, because the fourth power only responds to distance from the mean.
  • Because a few observations dominate, the estimate changes sharply when the sample window is extended or shortened, which makes period-to-period comparisons unreliable.

Commonly confused with

Fat Tails

Kurtosis is one scalar summary of tail weight. Fat tails are the underlying property, and a distribution can have infinite kurtosis while still having a perfectly well-defined tail shape.

Three conventions circulate under the single word 'kurtosis': raw Pearson kurtosis (normal = 3), excess kurtosis (normal = 0) and a bias-corrected sample estimator. This entry uses raw kurtosis throughout and labels the excess form explicitly wherever it appears; readers comparing published figures must check which convention the source used.
