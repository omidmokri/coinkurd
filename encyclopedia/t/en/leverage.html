Simple definition

Controlling a larger position than your own money would buy, by posting part of the value and borrowing the rest. It multiplies gains and losses by the same factor.

Professional definition

The ratio of notional exposure to the equity supporting it. At ten times leverage a one percent move in the underlying is a ten percent move in equity, so the same price path produces an entirely different account outcome.

Technical definition

A multiplier on exposure, not on edge. It scales both tails symmetrically in percentage terms but not in consequence, because losses are bounded below by zero equity and forced closure, so a leveraged position can be removed from the market before a thesis has time to be right. Effective leverage drifts as equity changes, rising automatically as a position loses.

Academic definition

The ratio of a position's notional value to the equity capital supporting it, which scales the return on equity by the same factor applied to the return on the underlying.

Mechanism

Leverage comes from financing: the venue advances the difference between notional and posted equity and keeps the position as security. That is why the lender's protection, not the borrower's conviction, sets the limit. Because equity shrinks as a position loses while notional barely changes, effective leverage climbs on the way down, which is the opposite of what the holder needs.

In practice

Who uses it
Futures and perpetual traders, margin equity accounts, market makers financing inventory, and funds using notional exposure to match a mandate.
Why it matters
It allows an exposure to be carried with less capital tied up, which frees collateral for other uses and makes small, reliable spreads worth trading.
Limitations
It changes nothing about the probability of being right. Permitted ratios differ by jurisdiction, venue and instrument, and retail limits in particular vary widely.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the leverage setting as the risk rather than the stop distance.
  • Forgetting that effective leverage rises as equity falls.
  • Assuming a venue's maximum offered leverage is a usable one.

Examples

market

Two accounts take the same view with the same stop distance, one at two times leverage and one at twenty. The twenty-times account is closed by the venue during an ordinary overnight wick; the two-times account is still holding when the move it expected arrives.

numeric

Equity of 1,000 supports a notional of 10,000, so leverage is ten. A five percent adverse move in the underlying costs 500, which is half the equity. A ten percent move would exhaust it entirely, before any maintenance requirement is even reached.

Given
  • posted equity E0 = 1,000 (account currency)
  • notional N = 10,000
  • entry price P0 = 100, so quantity Q = 100 units
  • adverse price move = -5 percent, then -10 percent
  • fees and funding excluded
Steps
  1. L = N / E0 = 10,000 / 1,000 = 10
  2. at -5 percent: change in price = -5, PnL = 100 x (-5) = -500
  3. return on equity = -500 / 1,000 = -50 percent, which is 10 x (-5 percent)
  4. at -10 percent: PnL = 100 x (-10) = -1,000, equity = 0
leverage 10; a 5 percent adverse move costs 50 percent of equity and a 10 percent move costs all of it

In practice the position is closed before equity reaches zero, because the maintenance margin requirement bites first. See the liquidation entry for that calculation.

Mathematical foundation

L = \frac{N}{E_0}, \qquad \frac{\Delta E}{E_0} = L \times \frac{\Delta P}{P_0}

Procedure

Divide notional exposure by the equity backing it to get the ratio. To translate a price move into an account outcome, multiply the percentage move in the underlying by that ratio. Recompute the ratio after any profit or loss, because equity has changed while notional has not.

Worked example

Given
  • N = 10,000 account currency
  • E0 = 1,000 account currency
  • adverse move of 5 percent in the underlying
Steps
  1. L = 10,000 / 1,000 = 10
  2. delta E / E0 = 10 x (-0.05) = -0.50
  3. equity after the move = 1,000 x (1 - 0.50) = 500
  4. effective leverage now = 10,000 / 500 = 20 if the notional is unchanged
a 5 percent adverse move halves the equity and doubles the effective leverage

Interpretation

Leverage is a scaling factor on outcomes and on nothing else. The last line of the worked example is the part that matters: a losing leveraged position becomes more leveraged on its own, which is why losses accelerate rather than stay proportional.

Assumptions

  • A linear contract quoted in the account currency with a constant quantity; inverse and quanto contracts need a different derivation.
  • Fees, funding payments and borrowing interest are excluded and all reduce equity independently of price.
  • Leverage is measured at inception. Effective leverage changes with every mark-to-market and is not the number entered on the order ticket.
  • Isolated collateral. Under cross margin the whole account backs the position and the ratio must be computed on account equity.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity scales returns, not probabilities: a higher ratio does not make a view more or less likely to be correct.
  • Maximum permitted leverage is set by regulation and venue policy and differs by jurisdiction, product and client classification, so no universal figure can be quoted.
  • Because loss is bounded by forced closure, the downside is not the mirror image of the upside even though the percentage multiplier is symmetric.

Commonly confused with

Margin

Leverage is the ratio; margin is the collateral that produces it. Posting more margin on the same notional lowers leverage, and the two numbers are related but answer different questions.

The ratio and the return-scaling identity are definitional and uncontested. What is jurisdiction-dependent, and deliberately left unquantified here, is the maximum leverage a client may be offered: that depends on the regulator, the product and the client's classification, and the cited regulator material describes the risks rather than any single limit.
