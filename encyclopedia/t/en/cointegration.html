Simple definition

Two series that each wander without limit, but whose gap keeps returning to a stable level. Neither is predictable alone; the distance between them is.

Professional definition

A property of two or more non-stationary series for which some fixed linear combination is stationary. That combination is the equilibrium relation, and deviations from it decay, which is what an error-correction term measures.

Technical definition

If each series has a unit root but a linear combination of them does not, the series are cointegrated and share a common stochastic trend. The Engle-Granger representation links this to an error-correction form in which changes respond to the lagged deviation with a negative coefficient, giving a decay rate for the deviation. Cointegration is a statistical property of a sample, established by test and never by inspection, and tests have low power in short samples.

Academic definition

The existence of a stationary linear combination of individually integrated series, equivalent to an error-correction representation in which deviations from the long-run relation are mean-reverting.

Mechanism

Two series are cointegrated when one shared stochastic trend drives both, so the trend cancels in the right linear combination and only a stationary residual is left. Something must enforce that cancellation — a production link, an arbitrage channel, a shared input — and when the link breaks the residual stops reverting. Nothing in the statistics tells you whether the link still exists.

In practice

Who uses it
Econometricians modelling related price series, relative-value and basis traders, and risk teams building hedge ratios from levels rather than returns.
Why it matters
It licenses modelling the relationship between two non-stationary series directly, which a regression on levels without cointegration cannot do without producing spurious results.
Limitations
It is a sample property with no guarantee of continuing. An in-sample cointegrating relationship can break without warning, and it is not a law of the market.

Common mistakes

  • Concluding cointegration from a high correlation of returns.
  • Regressing one price level on another without a unit-root and residual test.
  • Reusing a hedge ratio estimated years earlier without retesting.

Examples

market

A futures price and its underlying spot price each wander, but the basis between them is held together by delivery and carry. The basis is the stationary combination, and it reverts because settlement enforces convergence at expiry.

numeric

A fitted relation gives a hedge ratio of 1.4 and an error-correction coefficient of minus 0.2 per day. A deviation then has a half-life of about 3.1 days — the clock a trade on that spread would have to live within.

Given
  • cointegrating vector: z_t = y_t - 1.4 x_t
  • error-correction coefficient gamma = -0.2 per day
  • deviation decays geometrically at rate (1 + gamma) = 0.8
Steps
  1. half-life solves 0.8^h = 0.5
  2. h = ln(0.5) / ln(0.8)
  3. h = 3.106
half-life about 3.1 days

The half-life is conditional on the relation holding. If the link breaks, gamma is no longer negative and the deviation does not come back at all.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Confirm each series has a unit root. Estimate the cointegrating coefficient, then test the residual for stationarity; a stationary residual is the evidence of cointegration. Estimate the error-correction equation to obtain the decay coefficient, and re-test out of sample before relying on either number.

Worked example

Given
  • beta = 1.4
  • gamma = -0.2 per day
  • decay factor = 1 + gamma = 0.8
Steps
  1. solve 0.8^h = 0.5
  2. h = ln(0.5) / ln(0.8) = 3.106
deviation half-life about 3.1 days

Interpretation

Beta says how much of the second series offsets the first; gamma says how quickly a deviation is pulled back. A gamma near zero means the relation exists statistically but is too slow to be useful, and a positive gamma means it is not reverting at all.

Assumptions

  • Both series are integrated of order one and the cointegrating vector is constant over the sample; a time-varying beta breaks the representation.
  • The two-step Engle-Granger procedure assumes a single cointegrating relation. With more than two series the number of relations must itself be estimated, and system methods give different answers.
  • Test outcomes depend on the lag length, the deterministic terms included and the sample window, so a positive finding is not a property of the pair but of that specification on that sample.
Limits of the formula
  • Unit-root and cointegration tests have low power in short samples: failing to reject is weak evidence, and rejecting on a few hundred observations is fragile.
  • The relation can stop holding for economic reasons the statistics cannot see, and most published relations decay, so a fitted beta and gamma should be treated as perishable.

Commonly confused with

Correlation

Correlation measures co-movement of returns over a window and says nothing about levels. Cointegration is about levels staying tied together. Two series can be highly correlated and drift apart forever, or barely correlated and never separate.
