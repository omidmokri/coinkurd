Trailing Stop

Simple definition

A stop that follows the price when the trade goes your way and never moves back. It locks in more of the gain the further price travels, and it exits for you when price turns.

Professional definition

A stop level defined as the best price reached since entry, less a fixed offset, so it only ever ratchets in the favourable direction. The offset can be a currency amount, a percentage, or a volatility multiple such as a multiple of average true range, which makes the distance adapt to the instrument.

Technical definition

For a long, the stop at time t is the maximum of its previous value and the highest price since entry minus the offset, which makes it monotonically non-decreasing by construction. Three decisions change its behaviour materially: the offset size, whether the reference is closing prices or intrabar highs, and whether the stop is evaluated on bar close or continuously. A continuous intrabar evaluation exits more often and on worse prices than a close-only rule.

Academic definition

A path-dependent exit rule in which the liquidation threshold is a monotone function of the running extremum of the price path, offset by a fixed or volatility-scaled margin.

Mechanism

The offset buys room for normal noise. Too small and ordinary oscillation inside a trend closes the position early; too large and a sharp reversal gives back a large share of the gain before the stop is touched. Because the level only ratchets, the rule guarantees that realised gains are at least the best price reached less the offset, and guarantees nothing about gaps through it.

In practice

Who uses it
Trend-following traders, systematic strategies with no fixed target, and anyone who wants an exit rule that does not require watching the screen.
Why it matters
It removes the exit decision from judgement and keeps a position alive for as long as the trend continues, which is where most of a trend strategy's return sits.
Limitations
It always gives back the offset, it cannot protect against a gap, and the offset is a parameter that is easy to fit to past data.

Common mistakes

  • Tightening the offset mid-trade after an unrealised gain appears.
  • Using a fixed currency offset across instruments of different volatility.
  • Assuming the exchange's trailing order behaves like the backtest's.

Examples

numeric

A long is entered at 100 with a three times average true range offset, where range is 2.00, so the offset is 6.00. Price reaches 118, lifting the stop to 112. A fall back to 112 exits with 12 kept and 6 given back.

Given
  • entry = 100
  • average true range = 2.00
  • offset = 3 x ATR = 6.00
  • highest price reached = 118
  • stop evaluated on the running high
Steps
  1. initial stop = 100 - 6 = 94
  2. after the high at 118: stop = 118 - 6 = 112
  3. stop never falls back below 112
  4. exit at 112 gives 112 - 100 = 12
12 realised from an 18 maximum unrealised gain; 6 is the structural give-back.

The give-back equals the offset by construction. A smaller offset keeps more of a reversal but exits earlier in the trend.

market

An overnight gap opens below the trailing level. The stop becomes a market order at the open, and the fill is wherever liquidity is, which can be far below the level the rule displayed. The level is a trigger, not a guaranteed price.

Mathematical foundation

S_t = \max \left( S_{t-1}, \ \max_{u \le t} H_u - d \right)

Variables

S_tTrailing stop level at time t, for a long positionprice units
H_uHigh of bar u since entry; some implementations use the close insteadprice units
dOffset: a currency amount, a percentage of price, or a volatility multipleprice units

Procedure

Choose the offset and the reference series before entry. At each evaluation point, compute the running extreme since entry, subtract the offset for a long, and set the stop to the greater of that value and the previous stop. Mirror the signs for a short. Decide once whether evaluation happens on bar close or continuously, and keep that choice identical in testing and in live trading.

Worked example

Given
  • long entry = 100
  • offset d = 6.00, from 3 x an average true range of 2.00
  • running high after four bars = 118
Steps
  1. stop at entry = 100 - 6 = 94
  2. running high 118: candidate = 118 - 6 = 112
  3. max(94, 112) = 112
  4. price later falls to 112 and the stop triggers
Exit at 112 for a realised 12 per unit, against a peak unrealised 18.

Interpretation

The level states the worst price at which the position will be given up from here, assuming a fill at the trigger. The difference between the peak and the stop is the price paid for staying in the trend, and it is fixed by the offset.

Assumptions

  • The stop is a trigger, not a guaranteed fill. Gaps and thin books execute away from it.
  • The reference series is fixed in advance. A high-based trail and a close-based trail produce different exits from the same data.
  • Volatility-scaled offsets assume the volatility estimate is itself stable; a rising estimate widens the stop while the position is open, which some implementations do not allow.
  • No requoting or exchange-side trailing logic that differs from the model.
Limits of the formula
  • It systematically gives back the offset at the end of every winning trend trade, which lowers the average win relative to a fixed target in a range-bound market.
  • The offset is a fitted parameter: optimising it on past data is one of the easiest ways to produce an overfitted backtest.
  • Exchange-native trailing orders may be evaluated on last price, mark price or index price; the choice changes when they trigger.

Commonly confused with

Break-Even Stop

A break-even stop moves once, to the entry price, and then stops moving. A trailing stop keeps moving with every new extreme. The first protects against a loss, the second protects an accumulating gain.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The mechanics are uncontested; the choice of offset, reference series and evaluation frequency are conventions that differ between platforms and are the main source of divergence between a backtest and live results. Whether trailing beats a fixed target depends entirely on the return distribution of the strategy and is not settled in general.
