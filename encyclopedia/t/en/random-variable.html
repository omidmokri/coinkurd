Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A number whose value is not known in advance because it depends on how an uncertain situation turns out. Tomorrow's return on an asset is one.

Professional definition

A rule that assigns a number to every possible outcome of an uncertain experiment, carrying the probabilities of those outcomes with it. Discrete ones take countably many values; continuous ones take values across an interval and are described by a density rather than by point probabilities.

Technical definition

A measurable function from a probability space to the real line. Its law is fully characterised by the distribution function F(x) = Pr(X ≤ x), which is non-decreasing and right-continuous. Realised data are single draws from that law, never the law itself — the central reason one price history cannot reveal the process that produced it.

Academic definition

A measurable mapping from the sample space of a probability model onto the reals, whose induced law is summarised by its cumulative distribution function.

Mechanism

Separating an outcome from the number attached to it is what makes uncertainty computable. Once every outcome carries a value and a probability, averages, dispersion and tail risk all follow mechanically from one object. The cost is that the object is never observed — only its draws are.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone modelling an uncertain quantity: risk managers sizing exposure, quants specifying a strategy's return process, and option desks pricing payoffs.
Why it matters
It is the object every later statistic is computed from. Expected value, variance, value at risk and position sizing are all read off the same random variable.
Limitations
The assignment is a modelling choice, not an observation. Two analysts can attach different random variables to one market and reach defensible but incompatible risk numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a realised price path as the random variable itself.
  • Assuming one fixed law while the generating process is shifting.
  • Carrying discrete-model intuition onto continuous quantities.

Examples

everyday

Before a die is rolled the result is a random variable with six equally likely values. After it lands, the 4 you see is a realisation, not the variable. Markets are read the same way: the chart is the realisation.

numeric

Count the up-closes in five independent sessions, each equally likely to close up or down. The count is a random variable on 0 to 5, and the chance of at most three up-closes is 26 out of 32, or 81.25%.

Given
  • 5 sessions
  • Pr(up close) = 0.5 for each session
  • sessions assumed independent
  • X = number of up-closes
Steps
  1. X is binomial with n = 5 and p = 0.5, so all 32 up/down paths are equally likely
  2. Paths with 0, 1, 2 or 3 up-closes: 1 + 5 + 10 + 10 = 26
  3. Pr(X <= 3) = 26 / 32
Pr(X <= 3) = 0.8125

The fair-coin model illustrates the mechanics only. It is not a claim that daily closes are independent or equally likely.

Procedure

List or describe the possible outcomes. Attach a number to each. Attach a probability to each, with the probabilities summing or integrating to one. The distribution function is then built by accumulating probability from the left: for any x, add the probability of every outcome whose number is x or less.

Worked example

Given
  • X = number of up-closes in 5 independent sessions
  • Pr(up close) = 0.5 each
Steps
  1. Each of the 32 up/down paths has probability 1/32
  2. Number of paths with at most 3 up-closes = 1 + 5 + 10 + 10 = 26
  3. F(3) = Pr(X <= 3) = 26/32
F(3) = 0.8125

Interpretation

The distribution function answers one question at every threshold: how much probability sits at or below this value. It says nothing about which outcome will occur, only how the total probability is laid out across outcomes.

Assumptions

  • A probability space with a consistent probability measure is taken to exist; the definition does not supply it.
  • Discrete variables are described by a mass function and continuous ones by a density, while the distribution function covers both cases.
  • Independence between successive draws is an extra assumption, not part of the definition of a random variable.
Limits of the formula
  • The definition fixes no particular distribution, so it cannot by itself tell you how any market behaves.
  • Some heavy-tailed laws have a perfectly well-defined distribution function while their mean or variance does not exist.

Commonly confused with

Stochastic Process

A random variable is one uncertain number. A stochastic process is a whole family of them indexed by time — a path, not a point.
