Relative Valuation

Professional

Simple definition

Valuing something by what similar things are selling for, using a ratio such as price to earnings, instead of forecasting its own cash.

Professional definition

Pricing an asset by applying a multiple observed on comparable assets to the subject's own metric — earnings, book value, sales, EBITDA or an on-chain quantity. It prices relative to the market as it is, so it inherits whatever the market is currently paying.

Technical definition

A method with two sources of error that are easy to confuse: the choice of peer group and the choice of multiple. Because the multiple is taken from current prices, the output is a relative price rather than a value: if the whole comparison set is expensive, so is the answer, and the method cannot say so. Its discipline lies in matching numerator and denominator — equity multiples on equity metrics, enterprise multiples on pre-financing metrics.

Academic definition

The estimation of an asset's price by applying valuation multiples derived from a set of comparable assets to a corresponding fundamental metric of the subject asset.

Mechanism

The method assumes the market prices similar assets similarly, so the subject's own metric times the peers' multiple approximates its price. It is fast because it borrows other people's discounting and growth assumptions wholesale, and it is fragile for the same reason: the borrowed assumptions are invisible and are never tested.

In practice

Who uses it
Mergers and initial public offering bankers, equity analysts setting target prices, private-market valuers, and screeners ranking a sector.
Why it matters
It needs only a metric and a peer set, so it works where no reliable forecast exists, and it is the language in which transactions are actually negotiated.
Limitations
It prices relative to a market that may be mispriced as a whole, and the peer group is a judgement that moves the answer as much as any input.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a peer group until the multiple supports the desired answer.
  • Applying an equity multiple to a pre-financing metric or the reverse.
  • Using a mean rather than a median when one peer is an outlier.

Examples

numeric

Peers trade at a median price-to-earnings ratio of 18 and the subject's earnings per share is 2.8, implying 50.4 a share against a market price of 56. Using the peer mean of 22 instead implies 61.6 — the conclusion reverses on one choice.

Given
  • Peer price-to-earnings ratios: 12, 16, 18, 20, 44
  • Subject earnings per share = 2.8
  • Subject market price = 56
Steps
  1. Median peer multiple = 18
  2. Implied value = 18 x 2.8 = 50.4
  3. Mean peer multiple = (12+16+18+20+44)/5 = 22
  4. Implied value = 22 x 2.8 = 61.6
50.4 on the median, 61.6 on the mean; market price 56 sits between them

One peer at 44 pulls the mean up by four points. Which statistic is used is a decision, not a calculation.

case

During a sector-wide boom every company in a peer group trades at thirty times earnings, so each one looks fairly valued against the others. Relative valuation cannot detect that the whole set is expensive, because the set is its only reference.

Mathematical foundation

V_{subject} = m_{peers} \times X_{subject}, \qquad m_{peers} = \mathrm{median}\left(\frac{V_i}{X_i}\right)

Variables

m_{peers}Multiple observed on the comparable set, usually a medianratio
X_{subject}The subject's own metric: earnings, book equity, sales, EBITDA or another drivercurrency
V_i / X_iEach peer's value divided by the same metric, measured identicallyratio

Procedure

Define the peer set and write down why each member belongs. Compute the same multiple for every peer from identically measured inputs, take a central statistic, and apply it to the subject's metric. Then report how much the answer changes with the peer set and with the statistic chosen.

Worked example

Given
  • Peer multiples 12, 16, 18, 20, 44
  • Subject metric X = 2.8 of earnings per share
Steps
  1. Median = 18
  2. 18 x 2.8 = 50.4
  3. Mean = 22
  4. 22 x 2.8 = 61.6
Implied value 50.4 (median basis) or 61.6 (mean basis)

Interpretation

The output is a price consistent with how the comparison set is currently priced. It answers whether the subject is expensive relative to its peers, and cannot answer whether the peers themselves are expensive.

Assumptions

  • Numerator and denominator must match in scope: equity value with equity metrics, enterprise value with metrics measured before financing cost.
  • Peers must share the growth, risk and reinvestment characteristics the multiple is implicitly pricing; sharing an industry code is not sufficient.
  • Median and harmonic mean are generally preferred to the arithmetic mean because multiples are ratios with a bounded denominator and are right-skewed.
Limits of the formula
  • Cannot identify a mispricing common to the whole comparison set, so it is a relative statement by construction.
  • Highly sensitive to peer selection, which is a judgement that is rarely documented and easy to work backwards from a desired answer.

Commonly confused with

Discounted Cash Flow

A discounted cash flow model states its own assumptions about growth and risk. A multiple imports someone else's, unexamined. The first can disagree with the market; the second cannot.
