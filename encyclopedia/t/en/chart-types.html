Foundation

Simple definition

The different ways one price series can be drawn: a line through the closes, a bar or candle showing four prices per period, or grids that ignore time altogether.

Professional definition

The family of renderings of the same price data. Line and area charts plot one value per interval; bar and candlestick charts plot open, high, low and close; Renko, Kagi and point-and-figure charts advance only on a price movement, so the horizontal axis is not time.

Technical definition

A chart type is a lossy projection chosen before any analysis begins. Line charts discard range and gaps; OHLC renderings keep range but discard the path inside the bar; Heikin-Ashi substitutes averaged values for real ones; brick and figure charts replace the time axis with a price-step axis, so pattern counts and indicator values computed on them are not comparable with time-based bars. Axis scaling, linear or logarithmic, changes every drawn angle.

Academic definition

A set of visual encodings of a price series, each discarding a different part of the underlying record: one value per interval, four values per interval, or price-step sampling in which elapsed time is not represented.

Mechanism

Every chart is a compression of the same tick record, and compression chooses what to lose. A rendering that drops the extremes hides where trading was rejected; one that drops time hides how long a move took. The visual pattern a trader reacts to is therefore partly a property of the encoding, not of the market.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone choosing a default chart, platform developers setting those defaults, and analysts comparing one signal across two renderings.
Why it matters
The rendering fixes what the eye can see and what any indicator computed from the plotted series can measure.
Limitations
No type is neutral: a pattern obvious on one encoding can be absent on another, and time-independent charts break alignment with volume and with clock-based sessions.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a pattern count between Renko and time-based bars.
  • Reading the angle of a trendline on a log axis as a rate.
  • Treating a Heikin-Ashi close as a tradable price.

Examples

chart

A quiet period looks almost flat on a line chart, shows long wicks on candles, and may print no new brick at all on a Renko chart. Three pictures of one week, and three different answers to whether anything happened.

numeric

One day prints open 100, high 112, low 98, close 101. The line chart keeps a single point at 101. The candle keeps all four, and shows an eleven-point upper wick that the line chart cannot express at all.

Given
  • open = 100
  • high = 112
  • low = 98
  • close = 101
Steps
  1. line chart value = close = 101
  2. candle body = |101 - 100| = 1
  3. upper wick = 112 - 101 = 11
  4. lower wick = 100 - 98 = 2
  5. total range = 112 - 98 = 14
The line chart retains 1 of the 4 values; 14 points of range are not representable on it.

The discarded range is not noise. It records prices that were reached and then rejected, which is exactly what a stop order cares about.

Commonly confused with

Timeframe

Chart type is how one bar is drawn. Timeframe is how much time one bar covers. They are independent choices and both change the picture.
