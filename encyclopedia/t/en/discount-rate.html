Discount rate

Simple definition

The rate used to shrink future money down to today's value. A higher rate means you are treating future money as worth less now.

Professional definition

In valuation, the per-period rate applied to future cash flows to express them at today's date; it bundles the opportunity cost of capital with a premium for the risk of those particular flows. In central-banking usage the same words name the rate charged on lending to banks, which is a different object.

Technical definition

A choice variable in any discounting exercise, not an observable. Conventionally decomposed as a risk-free rate plus premia for credit, liquidity and systematic exposure, with the decomposition itself model-dependent. It must match the cash flows in nominal-versus-real terms, in currency, and in period length, or the valuation is internally inconsistent.

Academic definition

The periodic rate at which future cash flows are converted to present values, reflecting the opportunity cost of capital and a risk adjustment appropriate to the flows being valued.

Mechanism

The rate is the price of waiting plus the price of bearing the uncertainty of waiting. Raising it shrinks distant flows faster than near ones, so it changes not just the level of a valuation but its shape — which is why growth assets are more rate-sensitive than mature ones.

In practice

Who uses it
Valuation analysts, project committees setting hurdle rates, lenders pricing instalment credit, and anyone reading a DCF someone else built.
Why it matters
It is usually the single most consequential input in a valuation, and the one most often chosen for convenience.
Limitations
Not observable. Build-ups from a risk-free proxy plus premia are conventions whose components are estimated with wide error.

Common mistakes

  • Discounting real cash flows with a nominal rate.
  • Reusing one firm's rate for a different risk profile.
  • Tuning the rate until the answer looks acceptable.

Examples

market

Two analysts value the same cash-flow forecast and differ only on the rate: 9% versus 12%. The long-dated flows collapse under the higher rate, and the two conclusions can be far apart with no disagreement about the business itself.

numeric

A single payment of 1,000 due in ten years is worth 613.91 at 5% and 385.54 at 10%. The same promise, a 37% difference in value, decided entirely by the rate.

Given
  • FV = 1,000
  • n = 10 years
  • r = 5% and r = 10%
  • annual compounding
Steps
  1. 1,000 / 1.05^10 = 613.91
  2. 1,000 / 1.10^10 = 385.54
  3. (613.91 - 385.54) / 613.91
≈ 37% lower value at the higher rate

Illustrative rates. The sensitivity grows with maturity: at one year the same rate gap costs under 5%.

Mathematical foundation

DF_{t} = \frac{1}{(1+r)^{t}}, \qquad r = r_{f} + \pi_{risk}

Variables

DF_{t}Discount factor for period tratio
rDiscount rate per perioddecimal
r_{f}Risk-free proxy for the same horizon and currencydecimal
\pi_{risk}Aggregate premium for credit, liquidity and systematic riskdecimal
tNumber of periods to the cash flowperiods

Procedure

Pick a horizon-matched risk-free proxy in the same currency, add premia for the specific risks of the flows, then form the discount factor for each period. Keep nominal with nominal and real with real throughout.

Worked example

Given
  • r_f = 4%
  • risk premium = 5%
  • t = 3 years
Steps
  1. r = 4% + 5% = 9%
  2. DF_3 = 1 / 1.09^3 = 1 / 1.295029
DF_3 ≈ 0.7722

Interpretation

A discount factor of 0.7722 says one unit received in three years is treated as 0.7722 units today at this rate. The additive build-up is a working convention, not a derived identity.

Assumptions

  • The additive build-up r = r_f + premium is a practitioner convention. Multiplicative and model-based alternatives (for example a CAPM-derived rate) give different numbers.
  • One rate for all periods assumes a flat term structure.
  • The central-bank 'discount rate' charged on lending to banks is a separate concept and does not belong in this formula.
Limits of the formula
  • Every component of the premium is estimated, so the rate carries estimation error that the single decimal output hides.
  • Because high rates compress long-dated flows, the rate choice can dominate the entire valuation of a growth asset.

Commonly confused with

Interest Rates and the Federal Reserve

In central banking, 'discount rate' means the rate charged when banks borrow from the central bank. In valuation it means the rate used to discount cash flows. Same words, unrelated objects.
