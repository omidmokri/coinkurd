Portfolio of Strategies

Simple definition

Running several rules together instead of one. If their good and bad periods do not coincide, the combined equity curve is steadier than any single rule's.

Professional definition

Treating strategies as the assets of a portfolio: each has an expected return, a volatility and a correlation with the others, and the allocation problem is the one Markowitz posed for securities. The benefit comes from low correlation between strategy returns, not from the count of strategies.

Technical definition

For equally weighted strategies of equal volatility, portfolio volatility falls as the square root of the number only when returns are uncorrelated; with average pairwise correlation rho, variance has an irreducible floor proportional to rho, so diversification stops paying long before the strategies run out. Strategy correlations are also unstable and rise under stress, exactly when the diversification is being relied on, and a set of variants of one idea is a single strategy carrying extra operational risk.

Academic definition

The construction of an allocation across multiple decision rules, treating each rule's return series as an asset and selecting weights according to expected return, variance and covariance structure.

Mechanism

Variance adds with covariance, not with count. Two rules whose losses arrive at different times each cover the other's drawdown, so the combined path is smoother and the same risk budget supports more capital. Two rules that lose together are one exposure wearing two names, which is why the first question about a strategy portfolio is what each rule is actually betting on.

In practice

Who uses it
Multi-strategy funds, allocators, and individual traders combining a trend rule with a mean-reversion rule.
Why it matters
It is the most reliable way to improve a risk-adjusted result without finding a better signal: lower correlation does the work.
Limitations
Correlations are estimated with error and rise under stress, and each added strategy brings operational and monitoring cost that the variance arithmetic ignores.

Common mistakes

  • Counting variants of one idea as separate strategies.
  • Estimating correlations on calm data only.
  • Adding strategies faster than they can be monitored.

Examples

numeric

Five strategies each at twenty percent volatility: uncorrelated, the portfolio sits near nine percent; at average correlation one half, near fifteen. Adding strategies past that point barely moves the number.

Given
  • N = 5 equally weighted strategies
  • each with annual volatility 20%
  • case A: average pairwise correlation 0
  • case B: average pairwise correlation 0.5
Steps
  1. A: 20 x sqrt((1 + 4 x 0) / 5) = 20 x 0.4472 = 8.94%
  2. B: 20 x sqrt((1 + 4 x 0.5) / 5) = 20 x sqrt(0.6) = 15.49%
  3. floor as N grows in case B: 20 x sqrt(0.5) = 14.14%
8.94% uncorrelated versus 15.49% at rho = 0.5, with a 14.14% floor

The floor is the whole lesson: at an average correlation of one half, no number of strategies brings portfolio volatility below about 14 percent.

institutional

An allocator holds six strategies described as uncorrelated that are all short volatility in different wrappers. The correlation matrix is near zero in calm data and near one in the week that decides the year.

Mathematical foundation

\sigma_p^2 = \sum_{i} w_i^2 \sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i \neq j} w_i w_j \sigma_i \sigma_j \rho_{ij}, \qquad \sigma_p = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{1 + (N-1)\bar{\rho}}{N}}

Variables

w_iCapital weight allocated to strategy ifraction
\sigma_iVolatility of strategy i's return series% per period
\rho_{ij}Correlation between the returns of strategies i and jdimensionless
NNumber of strategies, in the equal-weight special casecount
\bar{\rho}Average pairwise correlation across the strategiesdimensionless

Procedure

Build a return series per strategy on a common period and frequency, net of its own costs. Estimate volatilities and the pairwise correlation matrix, then compute portfolio volatility for the candidate weights. Use the second, equal-weight form as a sanity check on how much diversification the average correlation can possibly deliver, and re-estimate the correlations on stressed sub-periods separately.

Worked example

Given
  • N = 5
  • sigma = 20% for each
  • rho_bar = 0, then rho_bar = 0.5
Steps
  1. rho_bar = 0: sigma_p = 20 x sqrt(1/5) = 8.94%
  2. rho_bar = 0.5: sigma_p = 20 x sqrt(3/5) = 15.49%
  3. limit as N increases at rho_bar = 0.5: 20 x sqrt(0.5) = 14.14%
8.94% versus 15.49%, with an asymptotic floor of 14.14%

Interpretation

The output is the volatility of the combined equity path. The second form shows that average correlation, not the number of strategies, sets how much diversification is available at all.

Assumptions

  • The second form assumes equal weights, equal volatilities and a constant average pairwise correlation; it is a bound for intuition, not a substitute for the full covariance calculation.
  • Correlations are estimated from a finite sample and are not stable: estimates from calm periods systematically understate correlation in stress.
  • Volatility is used as the risk measure. With fat-tailed or path-dependent strategy returns, variance understates the risk that matters.
Limits of the formula
  • The formula says nothing about expected return; a smoother curve built from negative-expectancy rules is still a losing portfolio.
  • Estimation error in a correlation matrix grows with the number of strategies, so beyond a modest N the measured diversification can be an artefact of the estimate.

Commonly confused with

Risk Parity

Risk parity is one weighting rule: equalise each component's risk contribution. A portfolio of strategies is the broader problem of which rules to run and in what proportion; risk parity is one defensible answer to it, not the definition.
