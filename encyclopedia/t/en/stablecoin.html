Simple definition

A token designed to stay worth about one unit of something else, usually a national currency. It holds that value through the assets or rules behind it, not because the price is unable to move.

Professional definition

A cryptoasset that targets a fixed exchange rate against a reference asset. Three families dominate: fiat-collateralised tokens redeemable against issuer reserves, over-collateralised crypto-backed tokens kept solvent by liquidation, and algorithmic designs that defend the peg by expanding and contracting supply without external collateral.

Technical definition

The peg is maintained by arbitrage against a redemption or liquidation channel, so it is only as strong as that channel under stress. Fiat-collateralised designs transfer the risk to issuer solvency, reserve quality and the legal force of the redemption claim. Crypto-backed designs transfer it to collateral volatility, oracle accuracy and liquidation depth. Algorithmic designs rely on continued demand for a sibling token, and that reflexive loop has reversed violently in practice.

Academic definition

A cryptoasset whose design targets a stable exchange rate against a reference asset through collateral backing, redemption rights, or supply-adjusting rules, each of which relocates rather than removes the underlying risk.

Mechanism

Arbitrage does the work: when the token trades below par, someone buys it cheaply and redeems or repays debt at par, retiring supply until the gap closes. Every element of that loop can jam — redemptions can be gated, collateral can fall faster than liquidators can clear it, oracles can lag. The peg is a mechanism with failure modes, not a guarantee.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders parking value between positions, payment and remittance users, lending protocols using them as the unit of account, and supervisors monitoring reserve disclosure.
Why it matters
They let value sit on-chain without the price risk of a volatile asset, which is what makes on-chain lending, settlement and quoting workable.
Limitations
Stability is a claim about a mechanism, not a property of the token. Redemption rights, reserve audits and legal treatment differ sharply by issuer and by jurisdiction.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a stablecoin balance as equivalent to a bank deposit.
  • Assuming a secondary-market price near par proves reserves exist.
  • Reading an algorithmic peg that has held so far as proof it will hold.

Examples

numeric

A crypto-backed stablecoin requires 150 percent collateral: 150 of collateral behind 100 of debt. A 40 percent fall in the collateral leaves 90 against 100, so the position is liquidated and the debt bought back.

Given
  • minimum collateral ratio = 150%
  • debt issued = 100 units of the stablecoin
  • collateral posted = 150 units of value
Steps
  1. collateral falls 40%: 150 x 0.60 = 90
  2. ratio = 90 / 100 = 90%, below the 150% minimum
  3. the protocol liquidates the collateral to retire the 100 of debt
position liquidated; the peg is defended by selling collateral, not by holding a promise

Illustrative ratios. In a fast fall, liquidation may clear below the needed price, leaving the system under-collateralised despite the rule.

market

Under stress, a fiat-collateralised token can trade below par on exchanges while the issuer still honours redemptions at par for approved clients. The discount measures access to redemption, not the reserves themselves.

Commonly confused with

Altcoin

Altcoin is defined by what a token is not — bitcoin. Stablecoin is defined by what it targets — a fixed value. Most stablecoins are altcoins, but the two words answer different questions.
