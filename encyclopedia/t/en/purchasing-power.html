Purchasing power

Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

How much a unit of money actually buys. When prices rise, the same note buys less, so its purchasing power has fallen even though the number on it has not.

Professional definition

The quantity of goods and services a nominal sum commands, measured by deflating that sum with a price index. It is the economically meaningful side of any balance: nominal amounts are only comparable over time after this adjustment.

Technical definition

The reciprocal of the price level, expressed relative to a base period. Because the deflator is an index built from a fixed basket and a chosen aggregation method, measured purchasing power inherits that basket's composition and substitution assumptions; two households facing different consumption bundles experience different effective purchasing power from the same published index.

Academic definition

The real command over goods and services conferred by a nominal monetary amount, obtained by deflating the nominal amount with a price index referenced to a base period.

Mechanism

Money is a claim on goods, not a good, so its value moves inversely with the general price level. Nothing has to happen to the currency itself: if prices double, every nominal balance loses half its command over goods, which is why interest below inflation is a real loss.

In practice

Who uses it
Savers, pensioners, wage negotiators, treasury teams setting cash policy, and any investor comparing returns across years.
Why it matters
It converts a nominal profit into a real one. A gain smaller than inflation over the same period is a loss in goods.
Limitations
The adjustment is only as good as the index chosen; your own basket, your city and your exchange-rate exposure can all differ from the published series.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing amounts from different years without deflating.
  • Assuming the headline index matches your own cost of living.
  • Treating a nominal gain as proof of progress.

Examples

everyday

A salary rises 5% while the cost of the household's usual basket rises 9%. The payslip is larger and the shopping is harder: nominal income up, purchasing power down.

numeric

At 6% annual inflation held for five years, one unit of money keeps about 0.747 of its command over goods — roughly a quarter of the purchasing power is gone with no change in the number held.

Given
  • constant inflation i = 6% per year
  • horizon n = 5 years
  • no interest earned on the holding
Steps
  1. price level factor = 1.06^5 = 1.3382
  2. purchasing-power factor = 1 / 1.3382
≈ 0.747 of the base-period purchasing power

Assumes a single constant rate and one index. An illustrative rate, not an observed one.

Mathematical foundation

V_{real} = V_{nominal} \times \frac{P_{0}}{P_{t}}, \qquad \frac{P_{0}}{P_{t}} = \frac{1}{(1+i)^{n}}

Variables

V_{real}The amount expressed in base-period goodsbase-period currency units
V_{nominal}The amount as written, in current unitscurrent currency units
P_{0}, P_{t}Price index in the base period and at time tindex points
iInflation rate per period, assumed constant in the right-hand formdecimal or %
nNumber of periodsperiods

Procedure

Divide the nominal amount by the ratio of the current price index to the base index. If instead you are given a constant inflation rate, compound it over n periods and divide by the result. Always state which index and which base period you used.

Worked example

Given
  • V_nominal = 1,000
  • i = 6% per year
  • n = 5 years
Steps
  1. (1.06)^5 = 1.3382
  2. 1,000 / 1.3382
V_real ≈ 747 in base-period units

Interpretation

The result is the same sum restated in the goods of the base period. It says nothing about whether the holding was a good decision — only what it now commands.

Assumptions

  • One index represents the relevant basket. A headline consumer index, a producer index and a household's own basket give different answers.
  • The compounded form on the right assumes a single constant inflation rate; with a varying rate, chain the actual index levels instead.
  • Index construction choices — basket weights, substitution treatment, quality adjustment — are part of the measurement, not neutral details.
Limits of the formula
  • Comparisons across countries also require an exchange-rate step, which introduces its own variation.
  • Over long horizons the basket itself changes, so very long purchasing-power comparisons are indicative rather than exact.

Commonly confused with

Consumer Price Index

The consumer price index measures the price level. Purchasing power is what that level does to a sum of money — roughly its reciprocal, not the index itself.
