Liquidation Threshold

Simple definition

The level of coverage at which a protocol is allowed to sell your collateral. Cross it and anyone can repay part of your loan and take collateral plus a bonus.

Professional definition

The parameter that defines the boundary of a solvent position: a maximum loan-to-value, or equivalently a minimum collateral ratio. Once the position's measured coverage reaches it, liquidation becomes permissionless, and the price that puts it there can be computed in advance.

Technical definition

The threshold is set below the maximum loan-to-value allowed at origination, so there is a corridor between what can be borrowed and what triggers sale. It is asset-specific and governance-settable, which makes it a parameter risk as well as a market one: a position can become liquidatable because a vote lowered the threshold for its collateral. Crossing it does not guarantee a sale, only permission; whether a liquidator acts depends on the bonus against gas and market impact.

Academic definition

The protocol-defined coverage boundary at which a collateralised position becomes eligible for permissionless liquidation, expressed as a maximum loan-to-value or an equivalent minimum collateral ratio.

Mechanism

The threshold is placed where the remaining buffer still covers the cost of enforcing it: the gap between the last feed price and the achievable sale price, plus the liquidator's bonus. Set it too close to full coverage and liquidations themselves leave bad debt; set it too far and borrowers are sold out needlessly. Everything between the opening limit and the threshold is deliberate slack.

In practice

Who uses it
Borrowers computing a liquidation price, liquidation bots, risk teams calibrating per-asset parameters, and anyone stress-testing a leveraged on-chain position.
Why it matters
It converts an abstract risk into a single collateral price, which is the number a borrower can actually monitor and plan around.
Limitations
The threshold is a parameter, not a promise: governance can change it, and the feed that measures the position may not match the market at the moment it matters.

Common mistakes

  • Planning to repay at the threshold rather than well before it.
  • Assuming the liquidation price is fixed when interest keeps raising the debt.
  • Reading one asset's threshold as the rule for a whole multi-collateral position.

Examples

numeric

A debt of 8,000 is backed by 10 units of collateral with a liquidation threshold of 80 percent loan-to-value. The liquidation price is 1,000 per unit, so the collateral can fall 50 percent from 2,000 before the position is eligible.

Given
  • D = 8,000
  • Q = 10 units
  • liquidation threshold LT = 0.80 of collateral value
  • current price P = 2,000
Steps
  1. liquidation price = D / (Q x LT) = 8,000 / (10 x 0.80) = 1,000
  2. distance = 1 - 1,000 / 2,000 = 50%
  3. equivalently, minimum collateral ratio = 1 / 0.80 = 1.25
Liquidation price 1,000 per unit, i.e. a 50% fall from the current 2,000.

If interest lifts the debt to 8,400 the liquidation price rises to 1,050, with no price move at all. The 0.80 parameter is illustrative and differs by asset and protocol.

market

In a fast fall, a position can be liquidated at a price the market had already passed through, because the feed updated once during the move. The borrower sees a sale below the computed liquidation price and the bonus still goes to the liquidator.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Take the threshold for the specific collateral asset, not a protocol-wide figure. Divide the debt by the collateral quantity times that threshold to get the liquidation price. Compare it with the current feed price to express the distance as a percentage fall, and recompute whenever debt, quantity or the parameter changes.

Worked example

Given
  • D = 8,000
  • Q = 10
  • LT = 0.80
  • current P = 2,000
Steps
  1. Q x LT = 10 x 0.80 = 8
  2. P_liq = 8,000 / 8 = 1,000
  3. fall to threshold = 1 - 1,000 / 2,000 = 50%
  4. with D grown to 8,400 by interest: P_liq = 8,400 / 8 = 1,050, fall = 47.5%
P_liq = 1,000 now, rising to 1,050 as interest accrues.

Interpretation

The output is a price, which is what makes the threshold usable: it says exactly where the position stops being the borrower's to manage. The distance to it shrinks from either side — a falling collateral price or a rising debt — so it has to be recomputed, not set once.

Assumptions

  • One collateral asset and one debt asset. With several collaterals the test is run on the weighted sum, and the implied liquidation price of any single asset depends on the others.
  • The threshold is the liquidation parameter, which protocols set below the maximum loan-to-value permitted at origination.
  • Prices are the protocol's feed prices; the sale itself may clear elsewhere.
Limits of the formula
  • Crossing the threshold grants permission to liquidate, it does not cause a sale: thin markets or high gas can leave an eligible position standing, and that is how bad debt accumulates.
  • The parameter is governance-settable and asset-specific, so a liquidation price computed today is conditional on parameters that may change.
  • A gap move can take the realised sale well below the computed price; the identity does not model feed latency.
