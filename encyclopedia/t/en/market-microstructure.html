Simple definition

The study of how trades actually happen: who posts prices, who takes them, what rules match them, and why the price you get differs from the price you saw.

Professional definition

The branch of finance that examines the machinery of exchange — order types, matching rules, quote setting, tick size, transparency — and shows how that machinery determines spreads, depth, price impact and the speed at which information enters prices.

Technical definition

The economics of trading mechanisms. It treats price as the output of a specific institutional process rather than a frictionless equilibrium: dealers and limit-order submitters supply immediacy and are paid through the spread, which decomposes into order-processing cost, inventory risk and adverse selection. Market design — continuous auction versus call auction, price-time versus pro-rata priority, pre-trade transparency — changes measurable outcomes, so findings are venue-specific and rarely transfer unchanged.

Academic definition

The field analysing how trading mechanisms, information asymmetry and intermediation jointly determine transaction prices, transaction costs and the rate at which private information is incorporated into observed prices.

Mechanism

Prices move because orders arrive, and orders arrive from traders with different information and different urgency. Whoever quotes a firm price has granted a free option to a better-informed counterparty; the spread is the fee charged for that option plus the cost of carrying inventory. Change the matching rules and you change the fee, so microstructure results describe a venue, not markets in general.

In practice

Who uses it
Execution desks, market makers, exchange designers, regulators studying market quality, and quantitative researchers modelling transaction costs.
Why it matters
It explains the gap between a strategy's paper return and its realised return, which for high-turnover strategies is usually the whole result.
Limitations
Most of the theory was built for equity dealer markets; crypto venues differ in fee structure, settlement, fragmentation and the absence of a consolidated tape.

Common mistakes

  • Treating quoted spread as the cost actually paid.
  • Importing an equity-market result into a crypto venue unchanged.
  • Measuring market quality by volume rather than by cost.

Examples

market

Two venues show the same quoted spread. On the first, depth refills in milliseconds; on the second, a single large order clears the book and the quote takes seconds to return. Identical headline spread, very different cost of trading size.

numeric

Bid 99.95, ask 100.05, mid 100.00 — a quoted spread of 10 basis points. A market buy that walks the book fills at an average of 100.07, so the effective spread is 14 basis points: the real cost was 40% above the posted one.

Given
  • bid = 99.95
  • ask = 100.05
  • mid M = 100.00
  • average fill price P = 100.07 (buy, D = +1)
Steps
  1. quoted spread = 100.05 - 99.95 = 0.10, i.e. 0.10 / 100.00 = 10 bp
  2. effective spread = 2 x (+1) x (100.07 - 100.00) = 0.14
  3. 0.14 / 100.00 = 14 bp
effective spread = 14 bp against a quoted spread of 10 bp

The mid is measured at order arrival. Using the mid at fill time instead would hide most of the impact, which is why the benchmark must be stated.

Mathematical foundation

S_{\text{eff}} = 2 D (P - M), \qquad D = +1 \text{ for a buy}, \quad D = -1 \text{ for a sell}

Variables

Procedure

Record the mid-quote at the moment the order reaches the venue. Sign the trade by which side initiated it. Take the difference between fill price and that mid, and double it to express a one-way cost as a round-trip spread. Divide by the mid for a basis-point figure.

Worked example

Given
  • M = 100.00 at order arrival
  • buyer-initiated fill at P = 100.07
  • D = +1
Steps
  1. P - M = 0.07
  2. S_eff = 2 x 1 x 0.07 = 0.14
  3. 0.14 / 100.00 = 0.0014 = 14 bp
S_eff = 0.14 price units, or 14 bp of the mid

Interpretation

The effective spread states what immediacy actually cost on that fill. Comparing it with the quoted spread separates the cost of crossing the posted quote from the cost of consuming depth beyond it.

Assumptions

  • The mid-quote is only one benchmark; volume-weighted and opening-price benchmarks give different cost numbers from the same fills.
  • Trade signing is required and is not always observable: on venues without a side flag, it has to be inferred, and inference rules disagree on trades at the mid.
  • The literature further splits the effective spread into a realised-spread component and a price-impact component, measured against a later mid; those decompositions depend on the horizon chosen.
Limits of the formula
  • It measures one execution, not a strategy: for a large parent order the relevant cost is the implementation shortfall over the whole fill sequence.
  • In fragmented markets without a consolidated quote, the mid itself is ambiguous, so cross-venue comparisons are not reliable.

Commonly confused with

Market Structure Shift

Microstructure is the economics of the trading mechanism. A market structure shift is a chart-reading idea about swing highs and lows. The words overlap; the subjects do not.
