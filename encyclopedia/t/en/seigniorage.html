Seigniorage

Simple definition

The profit an issuer makes from creating money: the money is worth more than it costs to produce. For a token protocol, the equivalent is the value of newly issued units.

Professional definition

For physical currency, the difference between the face value of money put into circulation and the cost of producing it. For a central bank more broadly, the income earned on assets bought with newly issued non-interest-bearing liabilities. For a token protocol, the market value of newly emitted supply, which accrues to whoever receives the emission.

Technical definition

Measured in competing ways: the minting margin on physical issuance; the flow of real resources obtained by expanding the monetary base, often written as the change in base money divided by the price level; and the central bank's net interest income on its asset portfolio. Token emission is the structural analogue — new units are claimed without a purchase — and dilutes existing holders unless demand grows at least as fast as supply.

Academic definition

The real resources accruing to the issuer of money by virtue of issuance, variously measured as the minting margin, the real value of base-money expansion, or interest income on assets acquired against non-interest-bearing liabilities.

Mechanism

Issuing money is exchanging a cheaply produced liability for real assets, so the issuer captures the difference. The limit is demand to hold: push issuance past it and the price level rises, which transfers purchasing power from existing holders instead — the same mechanism that makes token emission a dilution of current holders.

In practice

Who uses it
Finance ministries and central banks estimating issuance income, macro analysts studying monetary financing, and token holders reading emission schedules.
Why it matters
It names who gains from new units being created, which is the central distributional question in both monetary policy and tokenomics.
Limitations
Measurement is definition-dependent, and for crypto the analogy is structural rather than legal: a protocol has no state power to compel acceptance.

Common mistakes

  • Treating seigniorage as costless revenue for the issuer.
  • Comparing figures computed on different definitions.
  • Reading token emission as revenue rather than dilution.

Examples

everyday

A note that costs a few units to print circulates at a face value hundreds of times higher. The issuer received real goods or assets for it; the difference is the classic minting margin.

numeric

A protocol emits 1,000,000 new tokens in a year against a circulating supply of 50,000,000. Existing holders are diluted by about 1.96% of ownership; at a token price of 2 the emission transfers 2,000,000 of value to the recipients.

Given
  • circulating supply before = 50,000,000
  • new emission = 1,000,000 over the year
  • token price assumed constant at 2
Steps
  1. dilution = 1,000,000 / 51,000,000 = 1.96%
  2. value of emission = 1,000,000 x 2
1.96% dilution; 2,000,000 of value transferred

Illustrative supply and price. Holding the price constant is a simplification: emission itself can affect the price.

Procedure

State which definition you are using before computing anything. For minting, subtract production cost from face value issued. For base-money seigniorage, deflate the change in base money by the price level. For a protocol, multiply newly emitted units by their market price and report the dilution alongside.

Worked example

Given
  • new emission = 1,000,000 tokens
  • price = 2 per token
  • supply before = 50,000,000
Steps
  1. value = 1,000,000 x 2 = 2,000,000
  2. dilution = 1,000,000 / 51,000,000
2,000,000 of value emitted; 1.96% dilution

Interpretation

Each measure answers a different question: what the mint earned, what real resources the issuer extracted, or what value a protocol handed to emission recipients. None of them is the issuer's net benefit, because issuance also carries obligations and, past the demand to hold, inflation.

Assumptions

  • The three expressions are competing measures of related but distinct quantities; the literature does not agree on one definition, and figures are not comparable across definitions.
  • The base-money measure assumes the expansion is willingly held; beyond that point the gain comes through the price level instead.
  • The token form holds price constant while supply grows, which is a simplification: emission can itself move the price.
Limits of the formula
  • Central-bank issuance income is offset by operating costs and, where assets fall in value, by losses, so gross measures overstate the net benefit.
  • The crypto analogy is structural, not legal: a protocol cannot compel anyone to hold or accept its token, so emission value depends entirely on voluntary demand.

Commonly confused with

Supply Emission

Supply emission is the schedule by which new token units are created. Seigniorage is the value that accrues to whoever receives them, and the question of who that is.
