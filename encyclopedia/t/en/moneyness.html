Simple definition

Where the underlying sits relative to the strike. In the money means exercising would pay something now; out of the money means it would not.

Professional definition

The relation between the underlying price and the strike, labelled in the money, at the money or out of the money. For a call, in the money means S above K; for a put, S below K. The label is a statement about intrinsic value today, not about the chance of profit.

Technical definition

Usually expressed as the ratio S/K, as log-moneyness ln(S/K), or on a forward basis F/K, and sometimes standardised by dividing log-moneyness by the volatility times the square root of time to make maturities comparable. Definitions differ across texts and desks, so a moneyness figure is only comparable within one stated convention, and at the money may mean the spot strike or the forward strike.

Academic definition

A normalised measure of the position of the underlying price relative to a contract's strike, used to classify contingent claims and to index the implied-volatility surface.

Mechanism

Moneyness exists because the strike is frozen and the underlying is not, so a single contract passes through all three labels during its life. The label matters because it tells you how the premium is composed: an in-the-money option is mostly intrinsic value, an out-of-the-money option is entirely time value and therefore hostage to the volatility input.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone reading an option chain, volatility traders indexing a surface by strike, and risk teams bucketing exposures by distance from the strike.
Why it matters
It is the fastest way to see what a premium is made of, and it is the axis along which implied volatility is compared across strikes.
Limitations
The labels are snapshots of today's price relation. They carry no probability, and they change the moment the underlying moves.

Common mistakes

  • Reading at the money as a fifty-fifty chance of finishing in the money.
  • Comparing moneyness across maturities without standardising for time.
  • Assuming in the money at any point means profitable overall.

Examples

market

With the underlying at 104, the 100 call is in the money and the 100 put out of the money; the 110 call is out of the money. One move to 112 relabels all three without a single contract changing.

numeric

Underlying 104, strike 100. Simple moneyness is 1.04 and log-moneyness about 0.0392. The call holds 4 of intrinsic value; the put with the same strike holds none, so its whole premium is time value.

Given
  • S = 104
  • K = 100
Steps
  1. S / K = 104 / 100 = 1.04
  2. ln(1.04) = 0.03922
  3. call intrinsic = max(104 - 100, 0) = 4
  4. put intrinsic = max(100 - 104, 0) = 0
moneyness 1.04, log-moneyness 0.0392; call 4 intrinsic, put 0 intrinsic

Log-moneyness is preferred on a surface because it is symmetric: a move up by a factor and down by the same factor are equal distances from zero.

Procedure

Divide the underlying price by the strike for simple moneyness, or take the natural logarithm of that ratio for log-moneyness. To compare across maturities, use the forward instead of spot and divide by volatility times the square root of time remaining.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 104
  • K = 100
Steps
  1. M = 104 / 100 = 1.04
  2. m = ln(1.04) = 0.03922
M = 1.04, m = 0.0392 — in the money for a call, out of the money for a put

Interpretation

A moneyness figure places a contract on an axis; it does not price it. Its main use is to make strikes comparable so that implied volatility can be plotted against them, which is how the smile becomes visible.

Assumptions

  • Competing definitions are in genuine use: S/K, K/S for puts in some texts, F/K on a forward basis, and log or standardised forms. A figure is meaningless until the convention is stated.
  • At the money is itself ambiguous: it may mean the strike nearest spot, the strike equal to the forward, or the strike where a call and put have equal value.
  • Standardised moneyness requires a volatility input, so it inherits that input's model-dependence.
  • Simple and log forms are stated for a single underlying price and a single strike, before any adjustment for dividends, carry or funding.
Limits of the formula
  • Moneyness is not a probability. The frequently repeated reading of at the money as a fifty-fifty outcome requires distributional assumptions the label itself does not make.
  • Because the classification depends on a moving underlying, historical statements about a contract's moneyness are only valid with a timestamp.

Commonly confused with

Delta

Moneyness is a price relation you can read off a screen. Delta is a model-derived sensitivity. Desks often quote strikes by delta, which blurs the two, but they are not the same object.
