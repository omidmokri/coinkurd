Simple definition

A chart shape where highs and lows converge: each swing is smaller than the one before, so the range narrows toward a point.

Professional definition

A consolidation whose successive highs and lows converge, drawn as two boundary lines. Ascending, descending and symmetrical variants differ in which boundary is flat. The shape describes a contracting range; the direction in which it eventually resolves is not part of the pattern.

Technical definition

A triangle exists only relative to a pivot rule, a minimum number of touches on each boundary, a convergence tolerance and a cut-off before the apex. Change any of them and the number of triangles found in a dataset changes by a large factor, which is why a published success rate is a property of one detection specification. What the shape robustly describes is falling realised range inside the consolidation, which is measurable; the directional claim is not.

Academic definition

A consolidation pattern defined by convergent boundaries fitted to successive extrema, characterised by contracting range prior to resolution.

Mechanism

Converging extremes mean each attempt in either direction is being met sooner than the one before, so the price distance over which buyers and sellers disagree is shrinking. A narrow range also packs the resting stops on both sides closer together, so when a boundary finally gives way the orders that fire are concentrated. That concentration, not the shape, is why resolutions out of tight ranges are often abrupt.

In practice

Who uses it
Pattern traders, volatility-breakout systems that need a contraction filter, and option traders watching realised range compress before an event.
Why it matters
It marks a contraction in range with identifiable boundaries, which supplies a trigger and a tight invalidation inside one structure.
Limitations
The drawing is subjective, breaks fail often, the direction of resolution is not predicted by the shape, and any hit rate quoted is a property of the detection code.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a triangle success rate without the detection rules behind it.
  • Redrawing a boundary after price has already passed it.
  • Assuming an ascending triangle has to break upward.

Examples

chart

Highs come in at 120, 116 and 113 while lows come in at 100, 104 and 107. Two boundaries converge and the range has shrunk from twenty points to six. The contraction is measurable; the resolution direction is not implied.

numeric

The widest part of the same triangle measured twenty points. A break above 113 with the conventional measured move projects 133. The projection is a convention drawn from the pattern's own height, not a probability.

Given
  • widest height of the triangle = 20 points
  • upper boundary at the break = 113
  • measured-move convention: add the height at the break point
Steps
  1. target = 113 + 20 = 133
  2. invalidation back inside the boundary, for example a close below 110
measured-move target = 133, with invalidation near 110

The measured move is a drawing convention. It gives a level to plan against, not an expected value, and no source in this registry attaches a hit rate to it.

Commonly confused with

Trendline

A triangle is two converging trendlines plus a claim about what the convergence implies. Drawing the lines is geometry; the implication is the contested part.
