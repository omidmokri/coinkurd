Liquidity premium

Simple definition

The extra return investors ask for holding something hard to sell. Two assets with the same cash flows can trade at different prices purely because one is easier to exit.

Professional definition

The component of required return that compensates for the cost and uncertainty of exiting a position. It shows up as a yield spread between otherwise comparable instruments, and it widens in stress precisely when the ability to sell is most valuable.

Technical definition

A premium inferred rather than observed: it is whatever part of a spread remains after credit, duration, tax and optionality are accounted for, which makes its measurement residual and model-dependent. The direction is well established in fixed income, where off-the-run issues yield more than comparable on-the-run ones. Estimated magnitudes vary widely across studies, markets and periods, and the premium is not a stable payment for patience.

Academic definition

The component of an asset's expected return attributable to the expected cost and uncertainty of transacting, identified as the residual spread after controlling for other priced risks.

Mechanism

Exiting an illiquid position costs spread, market impact and time, and the size of that cost is unknown in advance. A buyer therefore pays less today for the same future cash flows, and the lower price is the premium. Because selling becomes hardest in stress, the premium is largest exactly when holders most want out.

In practice

Who uses it
Bond investors comparing on-the-run and off-the-run issues, private-market allocators, and anyone pricing a lock-up or a thinly traded token.
Why it matters
It explains why a higher yield is not free money: part of the extra return is payment for accepting exit risk.
Limitations
Measured as a residual, so it absorbs whatever the model left out. Estimates differ widely by study and period, and the premium is not reliably harvested.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the whole yield gap as a liquidity premium.
  • Assuming illiquidity is always compensated.
  • Sizing an illiquid position as if it could be exited at the marked price.

Examples

market

Two bonds from the same issuer mature on the same day, but one is widely held and actively traded while the other rarely changes hands. The quiet one yields more, and the gap is not a credit judgement.

numeric

A liquid bond yields 5.00% and a near-identical illiquid one 5.60%. If credit and maturity differences explain 0.25 points, the residual 0.35 points is attributed to liquidity — an inference, not a measurement.

Given
  • liquid yield = 5.00%
  • illiquid yield = 5.60%
  • credit and maturity adjustment = 0.25 points
Steps
  1. raw spread = 5.60% - 5.00% = 0.60%
  2. residual = 0.60% - 0.25%
inferred liquidity premium ≈ 0.35 percentage points

Illustrative yields. The residual also contains anything the adjustment failed to capture, so the figure is an upper bound on confidence, not a precise value.

Commonly confused with

Bid-Ask Spread

The spread is an observable transaction cost right now. The liquidity premium is an inferred addition to expected return for bearing that cost and its uncertainty over time.
