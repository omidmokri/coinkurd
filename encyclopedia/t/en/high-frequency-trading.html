Simple definition

Strategies whose edge comes from acting in microseconds: quoting, cancelling and capturing tiny profits thousands of times a day rather than holding any view on direction.

Professional definition

A family of latency-sensitive strategies — passive quoting, statistical arbitrage between correlated instruments, and taking quotes that have gone stale — marked by very short holding periods, high order-to-trade ratios and near-flat end-of-day positions. Returns come from many small captures, not from direction.

Technical definition

HFT is defined by a technology constraint, not by one strategy. Two claims about it are both well argued. The liquidity-provision case: competitive electronic quoting has narrowed spreads and cut the cost of small trades. The adverse-selection case: a faster participant quotes to the uninformed and withdraws from the informed, so measured spreads fall while the cost borne by slow participants in size, and during stress, need not. Both effects are real; the net sign is contested and depends on which trader, instrument and period is measured.

Academic definition

A class of automated strategies characterised by sub-millisecond reaction times, high order-to-trade ratios and minimal retained inventory, whose profitability depends on relative rather than absolute speed.

Mechanism

Speed buys two things: priority in the queue at a given price, and the ability to cancel before an informed trader arrives. Both are relative, so only the fastest participant's advantage is real. That makes latency spending a race with no stable resting point, and transfers much of the gross profit from the strategy into infrastructure.

In practice

Who uses it
Specialist firms, exchange market-making programmes, and crypto desks arbitraging between venues and between spot and perpetuals.
Why it matters
Where it works it is a volume business with small per-trade profits and tight inventory limits, and it supplies much of the visible quoting in liquid books.
Limitations
Capacity is bounded by the flow available, the advantage is relative and decays as rivals invest, and the market-quality question is genuinely unresolved.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a backtest's quoted prices were reachable without colocated infrastructure.
  • Reading a high order-to-trade ratio as evidence of manipulation by itself.
  • Calling every fast strategy market making.

Examples

market

A quoting firm shows both sides of a liquid pair. When a large informed buyer starts lifting offers, it cancels and reprices within microseconds; the slower quote left on the book is the one that gets filled at the old price.

numeric

Capturing half a basis point per round trip on turnover of 200 million units of notional a day yields 10,000 units of gross profit. One adverse-selection event of 50,000 removes five such days, which is why limits are enforced per second.

Given
  • capture = 0.5 bps = 0.00005 of notional per round trip
  • daily turnover = 200,000,000 units of notional
  • one adverse event = 50,000 units of loss
Steps
  1. 0.00005 x 200000000 = 10000 per day gross
  2. 50000 / 10000 = 5
10,000 per day gross; one 50,000 event costs five days of it

Illustrative arithmetic, not a market figure. It shows the structure of the business: thin per-trade margins and a loss distribution dominated by rare adverse events.

Commonly confused with

Market Making

Market making is an economic role: quoting both sides and carrying inventory. HFT is a technology class. Much HFT is market making, but a latency arbitrageur that only takes liquidity is not a market maker, and a slow manual market maker is not HFT.
