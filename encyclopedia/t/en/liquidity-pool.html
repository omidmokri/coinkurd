Liquidity Pool

Simple definition

A shared store of two or more assets locked in a contract, which traders buy from and sell into. Whoever deposits the assets earns a share of the trading fees.

Professional definition

A smart contract holding reserves of a token pair against which swaps execute. Depositors receive a claim on the reserves, usually as a pool token, and the pool's reserve balances rather than an order book determine the quoted price and the depth available at it.

Technical definition

The pool is the balance sheet of an invariant-based market maker: reserves fix the feasible trade set, and a depositor owns a pro-rata claim on whatever composition the reserves end up in, not on the assets originally deposited. That composition drifts against the depositor as price moves, which is divergence loss. Concentrated-liquidity designs let a provider restrict the range over which reserves are usable, raising fee density and the chance of being wholly on one side.

Academic definition

A contract-held reserve of assets against which a deterministic pricing function executes trades, where depositors hold fractional claims on the post-trade reserve composition rather than on their original contributions.

Mechanism

A pool replaces the promise of a quote with an inventory that is always there. Because the contract never refuses a trade, it must let its price move far enough to make the next trade unattractive; the reserves do that automatically. The depositor therefore sells whatever is rising and buys whatever is falling, and the fee is the payment for standing on that side.

In practice

Who uses it
Liquidity providers from individuals to professional market makers, protocol treasuries bootstrapping a market, and anyone trading against the pool.
Why it matters
It creates a tradable market for an asset with no order book and no designated market maker, and it pays depositors a share of flow.
Limitations
A pool's depth is its only depth. Reserves can be drained by a contract bug, and a quote from a thin pool is not evidence of a market price.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a pool by its advertised yield rather than by fees earned per unit of risk.
  • Depositing into a pool whose token contract can mint or freeze at will.
  • Confusing the pool token balance with ownership of the original two assets.

Examples

crypto

A stablecoin-to-stablecoin pool uses a curve that stays almost flat near parity, so a large swap between two pegged assets barely moves the rate. The same size in a volatile pair of the same depth would move it far more.

numeric

A provider deposits 5,000 of quote value into a pool whose reserves are worth 500,000, taking a one percent share. The pool collects 1,200 in fees over a month, so the provider's fee income is 12 before any price move is counted.

Given
  • pool reserves before deposit = 500,000 quote units
  • deposit = 5,000 quote units
  • fees collected by the pool over one month = 1,200 quote units
  • the provider's share stays constant over the month
Steps
  1. share = 5,000 / (500,000 + 5,000) = 0.990%
  2. fee income = 1,200 x 0.00990 = 11.88
About 11.9 quote units of fees on 5,000 deposited, roughly 0.24% for the month.

This is gross fee income only. Divergence loss and the price change of the deposited assets are separate terms and can exceed it.

Commonly confused with

Market Depth

Depth on an order book is a stack of resting orders that can be cancelled. Pool reserves cannot be withdrawn mid-trade, but their quote worsens continuously with size.
