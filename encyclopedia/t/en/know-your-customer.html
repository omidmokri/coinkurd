Simple definition

The checks a regulated firm runs to establish who a customer actually is, before it holds their money or executes their trades.

Professional definition

The identification and verification limb of customer due diligence: collecting identity evidence, confirming it against independent data, identifying the beneficial owners behind a corporate client, and recording the purpose of the relationship. Onboarding plus periodic refresh, not a one-off form.

Technical definition

A risk-based obligation imposed on obliged entities, not on assets. Scope follows activity — custody, exchange, transfer, issuance — so identical business can be in scope in one state and outside it in another. Depth of verification must scale with assessed customer risk, which is why opaque ownership chains and higher-risk geographies attract enhanced diligence while low-risk retail accounts may be simplified.

Academic definition

The identification and verification component of customer due diligence, by which an obliged entity establishes and periodically reconfirms the identity and beneficial ownership of each customer relationship.

Mechanism

A pseudonymous ledger records addresses, not people. Identification is therefore pushed to the only point where an address can be tied to a person: the intermediary that opens the account. The penalty for failure falls on the firm, not the customer, so firms collect more than they need and restrict more than customers expect.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchanges, brokers, custodians and payment firms; onboarding and compliance teams; and every customer asked for documents.
Why it matters
It decides whether an account opens, what evidence is required, and when activity must be escalated or a withdrawal held.
Limitations
Requirements, thresholds and acceptable documents differ by jurisdiction and are revised. Firm policy is usually stricter than the statute. This is not legal advice.

Common mistakes

  • Treating verification as a one-off event rather than a relationship that must be periodically refreshed.
  • Assuming the customer's national rules govern a platform incorporated somewhere else.
  • Reading completed verification as evidence that the platform is solvent or licensed.

Examples

regulatory

A corporate account clears every individual identity check and then stalls on beneficial ownership: the applicant cannot evidence who ultimately controls the entity. The signatory's identity was never the obstacle.

numeric

A firm holding 100,000 accounts refreshes low-risk files every three years, standard every two and high-risk annually. The resulting load, roughly 36,800 reviews a year, is why risk tiering exists.

Given
  • 85,000 low-risk accounts on a three-year refresh cycle
  • 13,000 standard-risk accounts on a two-year cycle
  • 2,000 high-risk accounts on a one-year cycle
Steps
  1. 85,000 / 3 = 28,333
  2. 13,000 / 2 = 6,500
  3. 2,000 / 1 = 2,000
  4. 28,333 + 6,500 + 2,000 = 36,833
About 36,800 periodic reviews per year

The portfolio mix and the cycle lengths are illustrative firm policy. No jurisdiction's statutory refresh interval is quoted here, because refresh obligations differ by country and change.
