Market Capitalisation

ENGLISHMarket Capitalisationreference

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Price multiplied by the number of units in circulation. It scales an asset for comparison; it is not the money invested in it and not what the whole stock could be sold for.

Professional definition

The last traded price times circulating supply, used to rank assets by size. Three supply measures give three different numbers: circulating excludes locked and unissued units, total counts all issued units, and fully diluted valuation applies today's price to the maximum eventual supply.

Technical definition

An accounting product of a marginal price and a stock of units, so it assumes the last trade would clear for every remaining unit — false whenever depth is thin or float is concentrated. Circulating supply is a data-provider judgement rather than a protocol fact: treasury, vesting, burned and bridged balances are treated differently across providers. No cash moves when market capitalisation changes; the whole figure is repriced by the marginal trade.

Academic definition

The product of an asset's prevailing market price and the quantity of units outstanding, used as a size measure under the counterfactual assumption that the marginal price applies to the entire outstanding stock.

Mechanism

The figure is linear in price, so the entire stock is revalued by whatever the last trade printed, however small that trade was. That is why a thin market can post a large capitalisation: the multiplier does not know how much size the market could actually absorb, and nothing in the formula references depth or realised flows.

In practice

Who uses it
Index builders, portfolio managers sizing by weight, screeners ranking assets, and anyone comparing two tokens of different unit price.
Why it matters
It makes assets with wildly different unit prices comparable in size, which is the basis of index weights, dominance ratios and most screening.
Limitations
It is a stock valued at a marginal price. It ignores depth, float concentration and the supply still to be issued, and it is not realisable.

Common mistakes

  • Saying money flowed into an asset because its market capitalisation rose.
  • Comparing two tokens on market cap while ignoring fully diluted valuation.
  • Treating a provider's circulating-supply figure as a protocol fact.

Examples

numeric

A token trades at 2.50 with 400 million units circulating and 1 billion units ever to exist. Market capitalisation is 1 billion; fully diluted valuation is 2.5 billion. The gap is future supply waiting to arrive.

Given
  • price P = 2.50 per unit
  • circulating supply = 400,000,000 units
  • maximum supply = 1,000,000,000 units
Steps
  1. MC = 2.50 x 400,000,000 = 1,000,000,000
  2. FDV = 2.50 x 1,000,000,000 = 2,500,000,000
MC = 1,000,000,000 and FDV = 2,500,000,000 in the quote currency

Illustrative figures. FDV holds today's price constant across supply that does not exist yet, which is why it is a ceiling scenario rather than a valuation.

market

Two assets can share a market capitalisation while one trades hundreds of millions a day and the other almost nothing. Same size label, completely different cost to build or exit a position of any real size.

Mathematical foundation

MC = P \times S_{circ}, \qquad FDV = P \times S_{max}

Procedure

Take one price and one supply figure measured at the same instant, then multiply. Use circulating supply for market capitalisation and maximum supply for fully diluted valuation. Report which supply measure and which price source were used, because both choices change the answer materially.

Worked example

Given
  • P = 2.50
  • S_circ = 400,000,000
  • S_max = 1,000,000,000
Steps
  1. MC = 2.50 x 400,000,000
  2. FDV = 2.50 x 1,000,000,000
MC = 1,000,000,000 ; FDV = 2,500,000,000

Interpretation

The result is a size label in currency units, comparable across assets only when the supply definitions match. It is not money invested, not an achievable sale value, and not a claim about the asset's worth.

Assumptions

  • Price and supply are read at the same moment from stated sources; mixing a stale supply figure with a live price produces a number that matches neither.
  • Circulating supply definitions differ between data providers over treasury, vesting, burned and bridged balances, so capitalisations are not strictly comparable across sources.
  • For listed equities the analogue is shares outstanding, and index providers often use a free-float adjusted count instead.
Limits of the formula
  • The marginal price is assumed to apply to the whole stock, which fails exactly when it matters: thin depth, concentrated float, or a forced exit.
  • Fully diluted valuation prices supply that does not yet exist at today's price and ignores the time value of that delay.
  • The measure says nothing about cash ever paid in, so it cannot be read as flows.

Commonly confused with

Trading Volume

Market capitalisation is a stock valued at one instant; volume is a flow measured over a period. A large capitalisation with negligible volume describes an asset nobody can leave cheaply.

The formula is arithmetic and uncontested; the uncertainty is in the inputs. Circulating-supply figures are provider judgements and differ materially between data sources, so any capitalisation or dominance figure is only as comparable as its supply definition.
