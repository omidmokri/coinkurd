Foundation

Simple definition

A loan you can buy and sell. The issuer owes you periodic interest and the stated amount back at the end; until then the price moves with market rates.

Professional definition

A transferable debt contract with a defined maturity, a stated face amount and, usually, periodic coupons. The holder is a creditor, not an owner: payments are contractual, ranked ahead of equity, and capped — upside comes from yield and price, not from the issuer's growth.

Technical definition

A claim on a fixed schedule of nominal cash flows whose market price is the present value of that schedule at the yield demanded for its credit and maturity. Because the cash flows are fixed in currency, any change in required yield must be absorbed by price, which is the arithmetic source of the inverse price-yield relationship rather than a behavioural one.

Academic definition

A transferable debt instrument conferring a contractual entitlement to specified interim payments and a terminal principal repayment, priced as the discounted value of that payment schedule.

Mechanism

The schedule of payments is written into the contract and cannot change, so the only free variable is what someone will pay for it today. If buyers demand a higher yield, the same fixed payments must be bought for less; that is why price falls when yields rise. Credit and liquidity concerns act through the yield demanded.

In practice

Who uses it
Treasury and pension funds matching liabilities, banks managing interest-rate exposure, and retail savers seeking contractual income.
Why it matters
It is the asset class where cash flows are known in advance, so the risk sits in credit, inflation and rates rather than in earnings.
Limitations
A known nominal schedule is not a known real one: inflation can erase the return even when every payment arrives on time.

Common mistakes

  • Calling bonds safe without separating credit risk from rate risk.
  • Buying a long maturity for income while ignoring price sensitivity.
  • Reading the coupon as the return.

Examples

market

An investor holds a five-year bond paying 4% a year. New issues of similar credit start paying 6%. Nothing about the old bond has changed, yet nobody will pay par for it any more: the discount is the market equalising the two.

numeric

A bond with face 1,000, annual coupon 4% and three years left, priced at a required yield of 6%, is worth 946.54. The 53.46 discount exists purely so that a 4% coupon delivers a 6% return.

Given
  • face F = 1,000
  • annual coupon = 40
  • n = 3 years
  • required yield y = 6%
  • annual payments
Steps
  1. 40/1.06 + 40/1.06^2 + 40/1.06^3 = 106.92
  2. 1,000/1.06^3 = 839.62
  3. price = 106.92 + 839.62
price = 946.54, a discount of 53.46 to face

Illustrative yields. Accrued interest and day-count conventions are ignored for clarity.

Mathematical foundation

P = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1+y)^{t}} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^{n}}

Variables

PClean price of the bond todaycurrency
CCoupon payment per periodcurrency
FFace or par amount repaid at maturitycurrency
yRequired yield per period for this credit and maturitydecimal
nNumber of periods remainingperiods

Procedure

List the remaining coupons and the face repayment with their dates. Discount each at the required yield for that maturity and add. With semi-annual coupons, halve the annual yield and double the period count, following the market's stated convention.

Worked example

Given
  • F = 1,000
  • C = 40 per year
  • n = 3
  • y = 6%
Steps
  1. coupons: 37.74 + 35.60 + 33.58 = 106.92
  2. principal: 1,000 / 1.191016 = 839.62
  3. P = 106.92 + 839.62
P = 946.54

Interpretation

A price below face means the coupon is lower than the yield buyers require; a price above face means the opposite. The discount or premium is the arithmetic bridge between a fixed coupon and a moving market yield.

Assumptions

  • Payments are assumed certain; credit risk enters only through the yield used, not through the cash flows.
  • A single flat y for all maturities is a simplification of the term structure.
  • The clean-price form ignores accrued interest and market-specific day-count conventions.
Limits of the formula
  • The formula prices the promise, not the probability of being paid; a defaulted bond's realised value has nothing to do with this expression.
  • Embedded options such as call or put features break the fixed-schedule assumption entirely.

Commonly confused with

Dividend

A coupon is a contractual obligation of a borrower; a dividend is a discretionary distribution by a company to its owners. Missing a coupon is default, cutting a dividend is a decision.
