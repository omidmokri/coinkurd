Profit Margin

ENGLISHProfit Marginreference

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

How much of each unit of sales is left as profit, written as a percentage. A 8 per cent margin means 8 units of profit for every 100 of revenue.

Professional definition

Profit divided by revenue at a stated level of the income statement: gross margin after cost of sales, operating margin after operating costs, net margin after interest and tax. The level must be named, because the three answer different questions about the same business.

Technical definition

A ratio of two figures from one statement, so it is immune to balance-sheet size but fully exposed to classification choices: what sits in cost of sales rather than in overheads, how capitalised development is amortised, and whether the revenue denominator is gross or net all move it. Margin levels are meaningful within an industry and almost meaningless across industries with different asset intensities and turnover.

Academic definition

The ratio of a specified measure of periodic profit to revenue for the same period, expressing the proportion of sales retained after the costs deducted at that level.

Mechanism

A margin is the residual after a chosen set of costs, so moving a cost from one line to another changes the margin without changing the business. It also reflects pricing power: where competitors can enter easily, margins compress towards the cost of capital, which is why durable high margins are evidence of a barrier and not of good management alone.

In practice

Who uses it
Analysts comparing companies within a sector, operators tracking their own cost lines, lenders assessing cushion against cost shocks, and acquirers modelling synergies.
Why it matters
It removes scale, so a small firm and a large one in the same industry can be compared, and it reacts quickly when pricing or input costs change.
Limitations
It ignores the capital employed to earn the sale, so a high margin on slow-turning assets can be a worse business than a thin margin on fast ones.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a gross margin with a competitor's operating margin.
  • Comparing margins across industries with different asset intensity.
  • Ignoring that reclassifying costs changes the margin by itself.

Examples

numeric

On revenue of 1,500 a firm reports operating profit of 190 and net income of 120. Operating margin is 12.7 per cent and net margin 8 per cent; the gap is interest and tax, not operations.

Given
  • Revenue = 1,500
  • Operating profit (EBIT) = 190
  • Net income = 120
Steps
  1. Operating margin = 190 / 1,500 = 0.1267
  2. Net margin = 120 / 1,500 = 0.0800
Operating margin 12.7%; net margin 8.0%

Quoting 'a 12.7 per cent margin' without the level invites comparison with a competitor's net margin, which is not a comparison.

market

A supermarket chain earns a net margin near two per cent and a software vendor near twenty-five. Neither number says which is the better business: the supermarket turns its assets over many times a year and the software firm does not need many assets at all.

Procedure

Choose the level of profit first, take that figure and the revenue for exactly the same period and entity, divide, and express as a percentage. Then check how the company classifies its costs, because that classification, not only its performance, sets the gross and operating figures.

Worked example

Given
  • Revenue = 1,500
  • EBIT = 190
  • Net income = 120
Steps
  1. 190 / 1,500 = 0.126667
  2. 120 / 1,500 = 0.08
Operating margin 12.67%; net margin 8.00%

Interpretation

The figure is the share of revenue retained after the costs charged at that level. It describes pricing and cost structure, and says nothing about how much capital was tied up to make the sale.

Assumptions

  • The level of profit must be stated. Gross, operating, pre-tax, net and EBITDA margins are all called 'margin' in practice and are not comparable with one another.
  • Revenue must be the same base in both terms: a gross-bookings denominator against a net-revenue numerator produces a meaningless figure.
  • Classification of costs between cost of sales and overheads is an accounting policy, so gross margins are only comparable where the policies match.
Limits of the formula
  • Blind to capital intensity and asset turnover, so margin alone cannot rank business quality across different models.
  • Movable by reclassification, by capitalising rather than expensing development, and by changes in revenue presentation, none of which affect cash.

Commonly confused with

Return on Equity

Margin is profit per unit of sales; return on equity is profit per unit of owners' capital. A business can have a thin margin and an excellent return on equity, or the reverse.
