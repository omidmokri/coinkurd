Price-to-Earnings Ratio

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The share price divided by profit per share. A P/E of 20 means you are paying twenty times one year of current profit for the share.

Professional definition

Price per share over earnings per share, or equivalently market capitalisation over net income. A relative-value yardstick: it compresses the market's view of growth, risk and earnings quality into one number, which only means something against a peer or a history.

Technical definition

A ratio of a forward-looking market price to a backward or forecast accounting profit, so the two halves are not measured on the same basis. Under a constant-growth model the justified multiple rises with the payout ratio and expected growth and falls with the required return, which is why a high P/E is not by itself expensive. The ratio is undefined for loss-making firms and distorted whenever earnings are near a cyclical peak or trough.

Academic definition

The ratio of market price per share to earnings per share, interpretable under a dividend discount model as an increasing function of expected growth and payout and a decreasing function of the required rate of return.

Mechanism

The multiple is a price for a stream of earnings, so it encodes what buyers believe about that stream: how fast it grows, how certain it is, and how much of it will be returned to them. Two firms with the same current earnings trade at different P/Es because those beliefs differ, not because one is mispriced by arithmetic.

In practice

Who uses it
Equity analysts, screeners, index providers and allocators comparing a company with its peer group or its own history.
Why it matters
It is the fastest way to ask what the market is paying for a unit of profit, and it travels well across a sector where accounting is similar.
Limitations
Undefined for loss-makers, unstable for cyclicals, and not comparable across sectors, accounting regimes or capital structures.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a low P/E cheap without asking why earnings are expected to fall.
  • Mixing a trailing P/E with a peer's forward P/E.
  • Comparing P/E across companies with very different leverage.

Examples

market

A cyclical steelmaker shows a P/E of 5 at the top of its cycle because earnings are temporarily at a peak, and a P/E of 40 two years later on depressed earnings. Neither number describes the business; both describe where the cycle is.

numeric

A share trades at 56 and basic EPS is 2.8, so the trailing P/E is 20. If next year's EPS is forecast at 3.5, the forward P/E on the same price is 16, and the two multiples answer different questions.

Given
  • Price per share = 56
  • Trailing basic EPS = 2.8
  • Forecast EPS next year = 3.5
Steps
  1. Trailing P/E = 56 / 2.8 = 20
  2. Forward P/E = 56 / 3.5 = 16
Trailing P/E = 20; forward P/E = 16

The forward figure is lower only because it uses a forecast. Comparing one company's forward P/E with another's trailing P/E is not a comparison.

Mathematical foundation

P/E = \frac{P_0}{EPS}, \qquad \frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{b}{r - g}

Variables

P_0Current market price per sharecurrency
EPSEarnings per share, trailing twelve months or forecastcurrency per share
bPayout ratio: the fraction of earnings paid out as dividendsratio
rRequired rate of return on the equityratio per period
gExpected constant growth rate of earningsratio per period

Procedure

Divide the price per share by earnings per share, using the same share class and currency for both. For the justified multiple, divide the payout ratio by the required return less the expected growth rate, which requires g to be strictly below r.

Worked example

Given
  • Price per share = 56
  • Trailing EPS = 2.8
  • Payout ratio b = 0.4, required return r = 0.09, expected growth g = 0.05
Steps
  1. P/E = 56 / 2.8 = 20
  2. Justified forward multiple = 0.4 / (0.09 - 0.05)
  3. = 0.4 / 0.04 = 10
Observed trailing P/E = 20; justified forward multiple on these assumptions = 10

Interpretation

The observed multiple is a price. The justified multiple is what a set of growth, payout and discount-rate assumptions would support. A gap between them is a statement about the assumptions, not proof that the market is wrong.

Assumptions

  • Competing definitions circulate: trailing twelve-month P/E, forward P/E on forecast earnings, and cyclically adjusted variants that use an average of several years of real earnings. They are different numbers and must not be mixed in one comparison.
  • The constant-growth form requires a stable payout ratio and g strictly below r; it is a model of a mature firm, not a general identity.
  • Price is a market observation while earnings are an accounting estimate, so accounting policy differences move the ratio without anything economic changing.
Limits of the formula
  • Meaningless when earnings are zero or negative, and arbitrarily large when they are merely small.
  • Says nothing about debt: two companies with the same P/E and different leverage are not priced alike on an enterprise basis.

Commonly confused with

Intrinsic Value

A P/E tells you what the market charges for a unit of earnings. An intrinsic-value estimate tells you what one analyst's assumptions imply the stream is worth. The first is observed, the second is constructed.
