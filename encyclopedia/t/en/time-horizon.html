Time Horizon

Category
Professional Investment
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

How long money can stay invested before it is needed. The date it is needed, not the investor's patience, is what sets it.

Professional definition

The length of time before capital must be available for its intended purpose. A portfolio usually has several horizons at once, one per planned outflow, and the nearest binding horizon constrains what may be held against that part of the capital.

Technical definition

A constraint rather than a preference. For independent, identically distributed returns the dispersion of the cumulative return grows with the square root of the horizon while the dispersion of the annualised return shrinks at the same rate, which is why a long horizon reduces uncertainty about the average annual outcome and increases it about the final amount. Neither effect makes a long horizon a substitute for risk capacity, and real return series are not exactly independent, so both scalings are approximations.

Academic definition

The period before which invested capital must be liquidated for its designated purpose, entering portfolio construction as a constraint on acceptable liquidity and on tolerable interim loss.

Mechanism

Risk becomes a loss only when a holding has to be sold. A long horizon changes nothing about an asset's volatility; it changes the probability that a decline has to be realised, because there is time to hold the position through it. Shorten the horizon and the same volatility becomes a far larger risk of a forced sale.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone planning around a known future outflow: pension funds with liabilities, institutions with spending rules, and individuals with dated commitments.
Why it matters
It decides which part of capital can be exposed to interim loss at all, so it is logically prior to any allocation decision.
Limitations
A stated horizon is often wrong. Employment changes, liabilities accelerate, and a horizon that an outside event can shorten is not the horizon to plan against.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a long horizon as if it reduced an asset's volatility.
  • Using one horizon for a portfolio that funds several dated outflows.
  • Confusing willingness to wait with the ability to wait.

Examples

numeric

With a 15% annual standard deviation and independent returns, the dispersion of the average annual return over nine years is 5%, while the dispersion of the nine-year cumulative return is 45%. Both come from the same assumption.

Given
  • one-period (annual) standard deviation of log return: 15%
  • horizon T = 9 years
  • returns assumed independent and identically distributed
Steps
  1. dispersion of the cumulative return = 15% x sqrt(9) = 45%
  2. dispersion of the average annual return = 15% / sqrt(9) = 5%
annualised dispersion falls from 15% to 5% while cumulative dispersion rises from 15% to 45%

The second figure is what 'time reduces risk' usually means. The first is why it does not reduce the risk of ending with less than was put in.

everyday

One portfolio funds a payment in eighteen months and a retirement in twenty-five years. Treating it as having a single horizon forces one of the two to be planned wrongly; the two obligations have to be matched separately.

Mathematical foundation

\sigma_{T} = \sigma \sqrt{T}, \qquad \sigma_{\bar{R}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}}

Variables

\sigmaStandard deviation of the one-period log return% per period
TNumber of periods in the horizonperiods
\sigma_{T}Standard deviation of the cumulative log return over the whole horizon% over the horizon
\sigma_{\bar{R}}Standard deviation of the average per-period return over the horizon% per period

Procedure

Take the one-period standard deviation of log returns. Multiply by the square root of the number of periods for the dispersion of the cumulative outcome; divide by the same square root for the dispersion of the average annual outcome. The two move in opposite directions from the same input, which is the whole point.

Worked example

Given
  • sigma = 15% a year
  • T = 9 years
Steps
  1. sqrt(T) = 3
  2. cumulative dispersion = 15% x 3 = 45%
  3. average-annual dispersion = 15% / 3 = 5%
45% dispersion in the final amount, 5% dispersion in the average annual rate

Interpretation

A longer horizon makes the average annual rate more predictable and the ending amount less so. It therefore supports holding a volatile asset only to the extent that the capital genuinely will not be needed earlier.

Assumptions

  • Returns are independent and identically distributed and the scaling is stated for log returns, so that the cumulative return is a sum.
  • Real series show volatility clustering and some mean reversion, so both scalings are approximations rather than identities.
  • The result says nothing about the mean. A longer horizon also compounds a negative expected return.
Limits of the formula
  • Square-root-of-time scaling understates tail outcomes when returns have fat tails or when volatility itself is persistent.
  • The argument that a long horizon makes a risky allocation safer rests on the second expression alone and is contested; the first expression is why.

Commonly confused with

Risk Tolerance

A horizon is a fact about when money is needed. Tolerance is a disposition about bearing loss. A patient investor with a near-term obligation still has a short horizon.
