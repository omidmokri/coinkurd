Gambler's Fallacy

Simple definition

Expecting a run to even itself out soon. After five losses, the sixth trade is not more likely to win unless something real connects them.

Professional definition

The belief that independent outcomes must balance in the short run. It follows from reading a small sample as though it had to resemble the long-run distribution, which Tversky and Kahneman described as the law of small numbers, a consequence of judging by representativeness.

Technical definition

For independent draws the conditional probability equals the unconditional one, so no sequence of past results changes the next distribution. The fallacy is not simply wrong about markets, it is wrong about the model: it assumes independence and then denies it. Where dependence genuinely exists — volatility clustering, autocorrelation, a strategy whose edge varies with regime — the next outcome's distribution does change, but then the reasoning must come from that measured dependence rather than from the idea that things are due.

Academic definition

The erroneous inference that short sequences of independent trials must exhibit local balance, attributed to judgement by representativeness rather than to a mistaken estimate of the underlying probability.

Mechanism

Representativeness judges a sequence by how much it looks like the process that generated it. Five losses in a row does not look like a fair process, so a correction feels owed. Nothing in an independent process stores that debt. The long-run average is reached by the sample growing large, not by short runs compensating each other.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone sizing the next trade with reference to the last few results, and anyone building a martingale-style recovery rule.
Why it matters
It separates two distinct questions: whether results are independent, which is testable, and whether a correction is owed, which is not a property of anything.
Limitations
Market returns are not perfectly independent, so the correct answer is not always no. Dependence has to be measured; invoking it after a losing run is the fallacy wearing a statistical coat.

Common mistakes

  • Raising size after a losing run because a win is due.
  • Treating volatility clustering as evidence that returns mean-revert trade by trade.
  • Reading a long streak as proof that a strategy is broken, with no test.

Examples

numeric

A rule wins half the time, independently. After four losses, the chance the next trade wins is still one half; the chance of five losses in a row from the start was one in thirty-two, but four of them have already happened.

Given
  • independent trades, p(win) = 0.5 each
  • four consecutive losses already observed
Steps
  1. P(five losses from the start) = 0.5^5 = 1/32
  2. P(next is a loss | four losses) = 0.5
  3. the first figure is about a sequence not yet begun, the second about the trade in front of you
the next trade's probability is unchanged at 0.5

The two probabilities answer different questions, which is the whole of the fallacy. Real trade outcomes may not be independent; that has to be tested rather than assumed in either direction.

market

An asset has fallen for eight sessions. That alone says nothing about the ninth. A measured tendency to mean-revert, estimated out of sample, would say something — and it would say it in both directions, not only after falls.

Procedure

State the independence assumption explicitly. If it holds, drop the conditioning: the history on the left has no effect and the next outcome's distribution is the unconditional one. If it does not hold, do not reason from streaks; estimate the dependence from data out of sample and use that estimate instead.

Worked example

Given
  • independent trades with p(win) = 0.5
  • observed history: four losses
Steps
  1. conditioning set = {loss, loss, loss, loss}
  2. by independence the conditioning set is irrelevant
  3. P(win next) = 0.5
P(win on the next trade) = 0.5

Interpretation

The identity is a definition of independence, not a claim about markets. Its use here is diagnostic: it shows that any argument from a streak must first assert dependence, and an asserted dependence can be measured or dropped.

Assumptions

  • Trials are independent and identically distributed; this is the assumption under which the fallacy is a fallacy, and it is an assumption about the model, not a finding about any market.
  • Probabilities are known rather than estimated. With an estimated win probability, a long streak is weak evidence about the estimate itself, which is a separate inference from the one the fallacy makes.
Limits of the formula
  • Financial return series show volatility clustering and some autocorrelation, so independence is an approximation at best; where dependence is present the unconditional distribution is the wrong one to use.
  • The identity says nothing about position sizing. Even with constant probabilities, raising size after losses changes the distribution of terminal equity and the probability of ruin.

Commonly confused with

Mean Reversion

Mean reversion is a measurable statistical property that can be estimated and tested. The gambler's fallacy is the belief that a balance is owed regardless of whether any such property exists.
