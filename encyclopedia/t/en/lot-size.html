Lot Size

Simple definition

The smallest quantity a market trades in, and the step between allowed sizes. You can buy one lot or two, but not one and a third.

Professional definition

The quantity increment an instrument trades in: a minimum order size plus a step that all larger sizes must be multiples of. With tick size it quantises the book in both dimensions, and it usually sits alongside a separate minimum notional requirement.

Technical definition

Three distinct venue parameters are commonly bundled under this name: minimum order quantity, the quantity step, and minimum notional value. An order can satisfy the quantity step and still be rejected on notional, which is the usual cause of unexplained rejections on small accounts. In futures the lot is the contract and economic size comes from the contract multiplier; in crypto spot the steps are decimal and are revised as price levels change, so a computed position size must be rounded down to the step.

Academic definition

The minimum tradable quantity and quantity increment specified by a venue, which discretises order size and constrains the attainable precision of position sizing.

Mechanism

Quantising size keeps the book legible and the matching engine cheap: an engine handling arbitrary precision would carry more state per level for no economic gain. The cost falls on the trader, because a risk calculation that produces 1.374 lots has to be rounded, so realised risk never exactly equals planned risk.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone sizing a position from a risk rule, API developers handling rejections, and portfolio systems reconciling quantities.
Why it matters
Planned risk is continuous and tradeable size is not, so the quantity step sets the precision limit on every position-sizing rule.
Limitations
Minimum quantity, quantity step and minimum notional are three separate rules that differ by instrument and are revised; small accounts hit them first.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding a computed size up and so exceeding the risk budget.
  • Confusing lot size with contract size in futures.
  • Ignoring a separate minimum notional requirement.

Examples

crypto

A small order satisfies the quantity step but is rejected anyway, because its value falls under the venue's minimum notional. Nothing is wrong with the size; a second, separate rule was violated.

numeric

A risk budget of 100 with a stop 3.30 away implies 30.30 units. With a step of 0.5, rounding down to 30.0 risks 99; rounding up to 30.5 risks 100.65 and breaks the budget.

Given
  • risk budget 100 in quote currency
  • stop distance 3.30 per unit
  • quantity step 0.5 units
  • fees and slippage excluded
Steps
  1. unconstrained size = 100 / 3.30 = 30.3030 units
  2. round down to the step: 30.0 units, risk = 30.0 x 3.30 = 99.00
  3. round up to the step: 30.5 units, risk = 30.5 x 3.30 = 100.65
30.0 units and 99.00 of risk, against 30.5 units and 100.65 which exceeds the budget

Rounding down is the conservative convention: it keeps realised risk inside the plan at the cost of slightly less exposure.

Commonly confused with

Contract Size

Lot size is how many units you may order and in what steps. Contract size is how much economic exposure one unit carries. A one-lot order can be a small or a very large position depending on it.
