Hedge Ratio

Simple definition

How much of a hedging instrument to hold against an exposure. It is rarely one for one: if the hedge moves more than what you are hedging, you need less of it.

Professional definition

The size of the hedge per unit of exposure. The minimum-variance ratio is the correlation between spot and hedge returns times the ratio of their volatilities, which is also the slope of a regression of spot changes on hedge changes. Multiplying by the exposure and dividing by contract size gives the number of contracts.

Technical definition

Minimising the variance of the hedged position yields the ratio as the covariance of spot and futures changes divided by the variance of futures changes, equivalently the correlation times spot volatility over futures volatility. The residual variance of the hedged position is one minus the squared correlation times the unhedged variance, so the correlation alone determines how much risk the hedge can remove. Estimates depend on the return interval and window, and a tailed hedge additionally discounts for the timing of margin cash flows.

Academic definition

The variance-minimising exposure of a hedging instrument relative to the hedged position, equal to the regression coefficient of spot on hedge returns, with residual variance proportional to one minus the coefficient of determination.

Mechanism

A hedge works by holding an offsetting exposure whose moves cancel part of the original. How much offsets depends on two things: how closely the two move together, which is the correlation, and how large each one's moves are, which is the ratio of volatilities. Correlation sets the ceiling on risk removed; the volatility ratio only scales the size needed to reach it.

In practice

Who uses it
Treasury and hedging desks, miners and producers hedging output, funds hedging an index exposure, and crypto holders hedging spot with futures or perpetuals.
Why it matters
It gives the size of the hedge rather than a guess, and the squared correlation states in advance how much risk the hedge can actually remove.
Limitations
The ratio is an estimate from a chosen window and interval, it minimises variance rather than loss, and it leaves basis risk that no ratio can hedge away.

Common mistakes

  • Hedging one for one when the hedge instrument is more volatile than the exposure.
  • Estimating the ratio on daily data and applying it to an intraday hedge.
  • Ignoring the margin cash flows the hedge leg will generate.

Examples

numeric

Spot volatility is 3 percent per month, futures volatility 4 percent, correlation 0.8. The minimum-variance ratio is 0.6, so hedging 10 units of spot needs 6 units of futures, and 36 percent of the original variance remains.

Given
  • sigma_spot = 0.03 per month
  • sigma_futures = 0.04 per month
  • rho = 0.8
  • exposure = 10 units, contract size = 1 unit
Steps
  1. h = 0.8 x (0.03 / 0.04) = 0.8 x 0.75 = 0.60
  2. contracts = 0.60 x 10 / 1 = 6
  3. residual variance fraction = 1 - rho^2 = 1 - 0.64 = 0.36
  4. variance eliminated = 64 percent, so residual volatility = sqrt(0.36) = 0.6 of the unhedged level
h = 0.6, six contracts, and 36 percent of the variance left unhedged.

A one-for-one hedge of ten contracts would overhedge here and add variance rather than remove it, because the futures leg moves more than the spot.

crypto

A holder hedges spot with a perpetual contract on the same asset. The correlation is near one, so the ratio is close to the volatility ratio, but the hedge introduces funding payments and a liquidation threshold that the spot position did not have.

Procedure

Fix the hedging horizon and compute spot and hedge price changes over intervals of that length. Estimate the two volatilities and the correlation from the same paired sample, or equivalently regress spot changes on hedge changes and take the slope. Multiply the ratio by the exposure and divide by contract size, then round to whole contracts and record the residual exposure. Re-estimate periodically, since the inputs drift.

Worked example

Given
  • sigma_S = 0.03, sigma_F = 0.04 over the same monthly interval
  • rho = 0.8
  • Q_A = 10 units, Q_F = 1 unit per contract
Steps
  1. sigma_S / sigma_F = 0.03 / 0.04 = 0.75
  2. h = 0.8 x 0.75 = 0.60
  3. N = 0.60 x 10 / 1 = 6 contracts
  4. residual variance = (1 - 0.8^2) = 0.36 of the unhedged variance
h = 0.60, six contracts, 64 percent of variance eliminated.

Interpretation

The ratio is a size, and the squared correlation is the honest statement of what the hedge can do. At a correlation of 0.8 the best possible hedge still leaves 36 percent of the variance, so a hedge is a reduction of risk and never its removal unless the instrument matches the exposure exactly.

Assumptions

  • Volatilities and the correlation are estimated from paired price changes over the same interval as the hedging horizon. Mixing intervals produces the wrong ratio.
  • The relationship is linear and stable over the hedge's life. Both assumptions weaken for options, for cross hedges and across regime changes.
  • Variance is the objective. Minimising variance also removes favourable moves; it is not the same as minimising loss or maximising utility.
  • Contract size and any multiplier are handled explicitly, and rounding to whole contracts leaves a residual exposure that should be recorded.
  • A tailed hedge discounts the futures position for the timing of margin cash flows. The plain ratio above omits that adjustment.
Limits of the formula
  • Basis risk remains by construction whenever the hedge instrument is not identical to the exposure, and the residual share is one minus the squared correlation.
  • The estimate is backward-looking. A ratio fitted in a calm window will underhedge or overhedge once the volatility ratio changes.
  • The hedge leg has its own operational consequences: margin calls, funding payments on perpetuals, roll costs on expiring futures and, in crypto, a liquidation threshold the hedged exposure did not previously have.

Commonly confused with

Beta

Beta is the regression slope of an asset on a market index, used to describe exposure. The minimum-variance hedge ratio is the same arithmetic applied in the other direction, to size an offsetting position. Related construction, different purpose.
