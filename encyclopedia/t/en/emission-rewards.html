Emission Rewards

Simple definition

New tokens a protocol hands out to attract deposits or trading. They are a subsidy paid by issuing more supply, not interest earned from anyone.

Professional definition

Newly issued protocol tokens distributed to users for a targeted behaviour, usually supplying liquidity or borrowing. The quoted rate annualises a current issuance schedule at a current token price, and both inputs are under pressure from the rewards themselves.

Technical definition

Three features separate emissions from economic return. They are dilutive: the value comes from existing holders, not from users of the service. They are self-defeating at the margin: the same issuance spread over more deposits lowers the per-unit rate, while harvested rewards are routinely sold, pushing the price down. And they are correlated with exactly the conditions in which the recipient wants to exit. A protocol whose fee income cannot replace emissions when the schedule ends was renting deposits, not earning them.

Academic definition

Protocol-issued token distributions used to subsidise participation, representing a transfer from existing holders through dilution rather than a return generated by the protocol's economic activity.

Mechanism

Emissions buy a behaviour at a price the protocol sets in its own currency, which is why the rate is highest where organic demand is weakest. Capital arrives, the per-unit reward falls, and sellers of the reward token push its price down, so the advertised figure decays from both sides. What remains afterwards is whatever the fees alone would have supported.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocols bootstrapping liquidity, depositors chasing advertised rates, and analysts separating subsidised yield from fee-based yield.
Why it matters
It is the fastest way to attract capital to a new market, and for a recipient it is a real, if temporary, source of return.
Limitations
The reward is paid in an asset the protocol creates at will, its value is not contractual, and a published schedule can be changed by governance.

Common mistakes

  • Adding emissions to an interest rate and presenting one combined yield.
  • Valuing rewards at the current price without checking the exit liquidity for that token.
  • Treating an emissions schedule as a commitment rather than a parameter.

Examples

numeric

A pool emits 50,000 tokens a week at a price of 0.40 against deposits of 20,000,000. The emission rate annualises to about 5.2 percent, and halves if deposits double or if the token price halves.

Given
  • emission = 50,000 tokens per week
  • reward token price = 0.40
  • deposits valued at 20,000,000
  • 52 weeks per year
Steps
  1. weekly value = 50,000 x 0.40 = 20,000
  2. annualised value = 20,000 x 52 = 1,040,000
  3. emission rate = 1,040,000 / 20,000,000 = 5.2%
  4. if deposits reach 40,000,000: 1,040,000 / 40,000,000 = 2.6%
5.2% annualised, falling to 2.6% if deposits double at an unchanged token price.

The figure assumes the token price holds. Rewards are commonly sold on receipt, which works against that assumption.

market

A pool advertises a high combined rate, most of it emissions. When the schedule steps down, deposits leave within days and the fee-only rate that remains shows what the market was actually worth.

Mathematical foundation

r_{\text{emission}} = \frac{E \, p_E \, N}{V}

Variables

Procedure

Value the tokens emitted to the pool over one period at the reward token's market price, annualise by the number of periods, and divide by the value of deposits sharing them. Report the result separately from fee income; adding the two produces a single number whose parts have completely different reliability.

Worked example

Given
  • E = 50,000 tokens per week
  • p_E = 0.40
  • N = 52
  • V = 20,000,000
Steps
  1. E x p_E = 20,000 per week
  2. x N = 1,040,000 per year
  3. / V = 1,040,000 / 20,000,000 = 0.052
Emission component = 5.2% annualised.

Interpretation

The number is the current rate of a subsidy, valued at today's reward-token price. It is not interest, it is not contractual, and it tells you nothing about whether the protocol will still exist when the schedule runs out. Its only honest use is beside the fee-based return, never merged into it.

Assumptions

  • The reward token price holds for the full year, which is the strongest and least defensible assumption in the calculation.
  • The deposit base sharing the emission stays constant; inflows dilute the per-unit rate immediately.
  • The published schedule is followed. Emissions are a governance parameter and can be raised, cut or ended.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity values a subsidy, not an economic return, and says nothing about dilution borne by existing token holders.
  • It ignores the exit liquidity for the reward token, which is what determines whether the quoted value can be realised.
  • Annualising a weekly schedule assumes continuation that no protocol is obliged to deliver.
