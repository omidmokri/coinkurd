Liquidation

Simple definition

When the exchange closes your leveraged position for you, because the collateral behind it is no longer enough to cover the loss. You do not choose the moment or the price.

Professional definition

The forced closure of a margined position once equity falls below the maintenance requirement. It is executed by the venue under its own rules, at its own mark price, often with a fee, and it converts an unrealised loss into a realised one at the worst available moment.

Technical definition

A venue-side risk action, not a market event. The trigger is a comparison of equity against a maintenance requirement computed on the venue's mark or index price, so a position can be closed on a mark that no public trade ever printed. Mechanics differ materially between venues: partial liquidation, tiered maintenance rates, insurance funds and auto-deleveraging all change where and how a position actually dies.

Academic definition

The compulsory termination of a leveraged position by the counterparty extending credit, triggered when posted equity falls below a specified maintenance threshold.

Mechanism

Liquidation exists because the venue cannot allow a client's loss to exceed the collateral securing it. The maintenance requirement is set so that closure happens while some equity remains, giving the venue room to exit. The side effect is that forced closes cluster: many positions reach their thresholds on the same move, and the resulting flow pushes price further in the same direction.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone holding a margined or perpetual position, venue risk engines, and analysts reading liquidation data as a crowding signal.
Why it matters
Knowing roughly where the threshold sits tells a holder how much adverse movement the position can absorb before the decision is taken away from them.
Limitations
The estimate is approximate and venue-specific. Tiered rates, cross margin, funding payments, fees and partial liquidation all move the real level away from any closed-form figure.

Common mistakes

  • Placing a stop beyond the estimated liquidation level.
  • Computing a liquidation price from last traded price instead of the venue's mark.
  • Assuming cross margin behaves like isolated margin.

Examples

market

A fast move clears a band of long positions. Each forced close is a market sell, which pushes the mark lower and brings the next band into range. The sequence is visible as a cascade, and it ends only where resting bids are deep enough to absorb it.

numeric

An isolated long opened at 100 with ten times leverage and a maintenance rate of half a percent is liquidated at roughly 90.45. That is an adverse move of about 9.55 percent, not the 10 percent the leverage figure might suggest.

Given
  • entry P0 = 100
  • initial leverage L = 10, so initial margin is 10 percent of notional
  • maintenance margin rate m = 0.005
  • isolated margin, no added collateral, fees and funding excluded
Steps
  1. P_liq = P0 x (1 - 1/L) / (1 - m)
  2. = 100 x (1 - 0.10) / (1 - 0.005)
  3. = 100 x 0.90 / 0.995
  4. = 90.4523
approximately 90.45, an adverse move of about 9.55 percent

The maintenance buffer is why the level sits above the 90 that a naive reading of ten times leverage suggests. Fees and funding move it higher still, and a venue with tiered maintenance rates will use a larger m on a large position.

Mathematical foundation

P_{\text{liq}} \approx P_0 \, \frac{1 - 1/L}{1 - m} \ \text{(long)}, \qquad P_{\text{liq}} \approx P_0 \, \frac{1 + 1/L}{1 + m} \ \text{(short)}

Procedure

Start from the condition that equity equals the maintenance requirement. For an isolated long, equity is the initial margin plus the mark-to-market change, and the requirement is the maintenance rate times current notional. Setting them equal and solving for price gives the expressions above. Both are approximations: each venue's exact rule, fee treatment and margin tier must be applied to get the figure it will actually use.

Worked example

Given
  • P0 = 100
  • L = 10
  • m = 0.005
  • isolated margin, no fees, no funding, no added collateral
Steps
  1. 1 - 1/L = 1 - 0.10 = 0.90
  2. 1 - m = 1 - 0.005 = 0.995
  3. P_liq = 100 x 0.90 / 0.995 = 90.4523
  4. adverse move = (100 - 90.4523) / 100 = 9.55 percent
  5. short side check: P_liq = 100 x 1.10 / 1.005 = 109.4527
long liquidation near 90.45 and short liquidation near 109.45 under these assumptions

Interpretation

The result is an estimate of where the decision stops being the holder's. It is always closer to entry than a naive one-over-leverage reading, because the maintenance buffer and every fee eat into the distance before the equity actually reaches zero.

Assumptions

  • Isolated margin: only the collateral allocated to this position backs it. Under cross margin the entire account equity and every other position enter the calculation, and no single-position formula applies.
  • A constant maintenance margin rate m. Most crypto venues use tiered rates that rise with position size, so the relevant m depends on the notional.
  • Fees, funding payments, borrowing interest and any liquidation penalty are excluded; each brings the level closer to entry.
  • A linear contract settled in the quote currency and quantity held constant. Inverse contracts, where the position is denominated in the base asset, have different algebra.
  • The trigger is evaluated on the venue's mark or index price, which is not the last traded price, and no collateral is added or removed before the threshold.
  • A single, full closure. Many venues liquidate partially, run insurance funds, or auto-deleverage counterparties, all of which change the realised outcome.
Limits of the formula
  • The expression is an approximation. Each venue publishes its own liquidation rule, and the exact level it will use differs from this figure, sometimes materially.
  • A liquidation price is not a floor on loss: in a fast market the close can execute well past the trigger, and some venues can leave an account with a negative balance absent a protection fund.
  • Because the trigger uses a mark price, a position can be liquidated on a mark no public trade ever printed, which also means that chart-based estimates can be wrong in both directions.

Commonly confused with

Stop-Loss

A stop-loss is the holder's own exit at a chosen level. Liquidation is the venue's exit at a level its rules choose. Reaching the second means the first was either absent or set too far away.

The formula is an approximation derived from the condition that equity equals the maintenance requirement under isolated margin with a constant maintenance rate, no fees and no funding. It is not any venue's published rule. Real mechanics are exchange-specific: tiered maintenance rates, cross versus isolated margin, mark-price construction, partial liquidation, insurance funds, auto-deleveraging and negative-balance protection all differ, so any figure a trader relies on should come from their own venue's documentation.
