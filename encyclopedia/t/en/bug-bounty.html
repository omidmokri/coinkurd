Level
Professional

Simple definition

A standing offer to pay anyone who reports a vulnerability instead of using it. It runs continuously, unlike an audit, which looks once.

Professional definition

A published programme with a scope, rules and a reward scale that pays external researchers for responsibly disclosed vulnerabilities. It complements an audit: open-ended rather than a fixed review of one commit, and paid for results rather than for hours.

Technical definition

The economics are the point. A reward must plausibly compete with the value of exploiting the bug, or a rational finder has no reason to report, which makes low caps awkward for protocols holding far more than the maximum payout. Programmes also need a credible payment history, clear scope, a safe-harbour commitment and a real response process, or researchers disengage. Like an audit, a bounty lowers residual risk without bounding it, and neither should be read as an assurance of safety.

Academic definition

An incentive mechanism that purchases vulnerability information from external researchers, aligning disclosure with reward so as to reduce the expected cost of undiscovered defects.

Mechanism

A finder chooses between reporting and exploiting. Reporting wins only when the reward, the reputation and the legal safety together outweigh the expected proceeds of an exploit net of the risk of being traced. A programme that pays slowly, argues about severity or threatens researchers moves that calculation the wrong way.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol and exchange security teams, independent researchers, and users assessing how seriously a project treats security.
Why it matters
It extends review beyond launch and prices security information before an attacker does.
Limitations
Coverage is open in theory and limited in practice by the reward cap, scope exclusions and the team's responsiveness. A live programme is not evidence that the code is sound.

Common mistakes

  • Capping the reward far below the value held in the contracts.
  • Writing scope so narrowly that real findings fall outside it.
  • Treating a bounty as a substitute for review before launch.

Examples

numeric

A protocol holds 400 million units of deposits and caps its bounty at 50,000, which is 0.0125% of the value at risk. For a finder weighing disclosure against exploitation, that ratio is the whole argument.

Given
  • Value in the contracts = 400,000,000 units
  • Maximum bounty = 50,000 units
  • Finder assumed rational and risk-aware
Steps
  1. Ratio = 50,000 / 400,000,000
  2. = 0.000125, or 0.0125%
The reward is about one eight-thousandth of the value at risk

Illustrative figures. A cap below the exploit value does not make disclosure irrational on its own — reputation, legal exposure and the difficulty of laundering proceeds all enter the finder's decision — but it is the first thing a researcher reads.

security

An outside researcher reports a flaw through a published programme. The team pauses the affected contract, migrates deposits and pays the reward. Nothing was exploited, and nothing about the code was proven safe.

Commonly confused with

Smart Contract Audit

A bounty buys findings from anyone, at any time, in live code. An audit buys a defined number of expert hours against one commit.
