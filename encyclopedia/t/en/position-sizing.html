Position Sizing

Simple definition

Deciding how many units to buy or sell so that, if the trade fails at your stop, the loss is an amount you decided in advance instead of whatever the market hands you.

Professional definition

Converting a chosen risk budget into a quantity, by dividing the amount of capital you are willing to lose on the trade by the risk per unit between entry and stop. Size becomes an output of the risk decision rather than an input based on conviction.

Technical definition

The mapping from a risk fraction of equity to an instrument quantity, given a defined invalidation level. It is the only step that connects a risk policy to the order ticket, and it is sensitive to three things that are easy to get wrong: the unit the instrument is quoted in, the contract multiplier, and the cost terms that widen the effective stop distance beyond the chart distance.

Academic definition

The determination of transaction quantity such that the ex-ante loss implied by a predetermined invalidation level equals a specified fraction of portfolio equity.

Mechanism

Sizing works because loss is the product of two separable things: distance and quantity. Fix the distance with a stop and the account can only be hurt through quantity, which is fully under the trader's control. That is also why a tighter stop permits a larger quantity for the same money at risk, and why cost per unit must enter the distance rather than be forgotten.

In practice

Who uses it
Every trader placing an order, plus risk functions that set per-trade and per-portfolio limits independently of the people trading.
Why it matters
It is the one control that keeps a losing streak survivable, because it fixes the loss per trade before the market has any say in it.
Limitations
It bounds loss only if the stop behaves. Correlated positions sized separately can each be within policy while the portfolio is not.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing from notional exposure instead of from risk per unit.
  • Ignoring the contract multiplier or the quote currency.
  • Keeping the risk fraction constant while equity is falling fast.

Examples

market

Two traders both risk one percent of equity. One uses a wide weekly stop and holds a small quantity; the other a tight intraday stop and a large quantity. Their risk is identical, their sensitivity to slippage is not.

numeric

Equity 20,000 in account currency, risk fraction one percent, entry 100, stop 96. Risk budget is 200 and stop distance 4 per unit, so size is 50 units, a notional of 5,000. Adding 0.40 per unit of costs cuts the size to 45 units.

Given
  • account equity A = 20,000 (account currency)
  • risk fraction f = 0.01
  • entry E = 100 (account currency per unit)
  • stop S = 96
  • round-trip fees plus expected slippage c = 0.40 per unit
  • contract multiplier = 1 unit of the asset per contract
Steps
  1. risk budget = A x f = 20,000 x 0.01 = 200 account currency
  2. stop distance d = E - S = 100 - 96 = 4 account currency per unit
  3. Q = 200 / 4 = 50 units
  4. notional = 50 x 100 = 5,000 account currency, which is 25 percent of equity
  5. effective distance with costs = 4 + 0.40 = 4.40
  6. Q with costs = 200 / 4.40 = 45.45, rounded down to 45 units
50 units ignoring costs, 45 units once 0.40 per unit of costs is included

Notional and risk are different quantities: a 5,000 notional here carries only 200 of planned risk. Confusing the two is the most common sizing error.

Mathematical foundation

Q = \frac{A \times f}{d}, \qquad d = \left| E - S \right| + c

Procedure

Multiply equity by the risk fraction to get the money you are prepared to lose. Measure the distance from entry to stop in account currency per unit, then add round-trip fees and expected slippage per unit. Divide the risk budget by that effective distance and round the quantity down. For contracts, divide again by the multiplier to get the number of contracts.

Worked example

Given
  • A = 20,000 account currency
  • f = 0.01
  • E = 100 account currency per unit
  • S = 96
  • c = 0.40 per unit
  • multiplier = 1
Steps
  1. A x f = 20,000 x 0.01 = 200 account currency of risk budget
  2. |E - S| = 4 account currency per unit
  3. d = 4 + 0.40 = 4.40 account currency per unit
  4. Q = 200 / 4.40 = 45.45 units
  5. round down: Q = 45 units
  6. check: 45 x 4.40 = 198, inside the 200 budget
  7. notional = 45 x 100 = 4,500 account currency
Q = 45 units, planned worst loss 198 account currency, notional 4,500

Interpretation

The output is a quantity, in units of the instrument, whose loss at the stop matches the risk policy. Notional exposure is a separate number and is usually much larger, which is why a small risk fraction can still sit on a large position.

Assumptions

  • The stop is reachable and fills near its level; a gap through it breaks the loss bound, not the arithmetic.
  • A linear instrument whose quote currency is the account currency. Inverse contracts and cross-currency quotes need an extra conversion term.
  • The cost term c is an estimate. Fees are known, slippage is not, so d is a forecast and the resulting loss bound is only as good as it.
  • Positions are treated one at a time; the formula says nothing about correlation across open trades.
Limits of the formula
  • A fixed risk fraction applied to falling equity shrinks position size automatically, which slows recovery as well as losses; variants that keep risk constant in money terms behave very differently.
  • Rounding, minimum order sizes and lot steps mean the realised risk is rarely exactly A x f.

Commonly confused with

Leverage

Sizing asks how much can be lost if the stop is hit. Leverage asks how large the notional is relative to posted equity. A high leverage ratio with a very tight stop can carry less risk than low leverage with a wide one.
