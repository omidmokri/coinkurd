Insider Trading

Simple definition

Trading on important information that the public does not have yet, when you were not supposed to use it. It is prohibited in regulated markets.

Professional definition

Prohibited dealing, or procuring dealing, on the basis of precise non-public information that a reasonable participant would expect to affect price, where the person is under a duty not to use it. Unlawful disclosure of the same information to others is usually prohibited alongside the dealing.

Technical definition

Liability turns on three things: the quality of the information, the duty the person owes, and the use made of it. Information must be specific enough to be acted upon and material enough to move a price; a duty must arise from position, contract, relationship or the way the information was obtained. Scope depends on whether the asset and venue are inside a regime at all, which in digital-asset markets is frequently unsettled, and the conduct is detected by trade-timing analysis against disclosure events rather than by confession.

Academic definition

Dealing on material non-public information in breach of a duty of trust or confidence, prohibited because it transfers wealth from uninformed counterparties and raises the adverse-selection cost borne by all participants.

Mechanism

Someone must be on the other side of every informed trade. Liquidity providers cannot tell an informed order from an uninformed one, so they price for the average and widen the spread to cover expected loss. Insider dealing therefore does not only harm the direct counterparty: it raises the cost of trading for everyone and, if believed to be endemic, drives depth out of the market altogether.

In practice

Who uses it
Issuers and their staff, listing venues, surveillance and enforcement teams, and anyone handling pre-announcement information such as a listing decision.
Why it matters
It defines when information must not be traded on, and underpins restricted lists, blackout periods and insider registers inside regulated firms.
Limitations
Coverage is uneven: whether an asset or venue falls inside a regime, and what counts as a duty, differ by jurisdiction and are frequently unsettled for digital assets.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the prohibition reaches only company officers and not anyone else under a duty.
  • Treating a lucky trade before news as proof of inside information.
  • Believing an unregulated venue removes the duty a person already owes.

Examples

regulatory

An employee who knows a listing decision before it is announced is typically under a duty regardless of job title. Surveillance compares the timing of accounts against the announcement rather than asking who was senior.

numeric

If five per cent of flow is informed and moves two per cent against the quoting side, expected loss per unit traded is ten basis points. The spread widens to recover it, so uninformed traders pay for the insider.

Given
  • Probability an incoming order is informed = 0.05
  • Average adverse price move when it is = 2%
  • Loss is zero on uninformed flow
Steps
  1. Expected adverse cost = 0.05 x 2% = 0.10%
  2. 0.10% = 10 basis points
  3. A quoting participant must earn at least this much from the spread to break even
About 10 basis points of spread is needed purely to cover informed flow

A stylised adverse-selection calculation in the spirit of the Glosten-Milgrom framework, using invented inputs. It illustrates why the harm is spread across all participants, not why any real spread is any particular width.

Commonly confused with

Informed Trading

Informed trading is any trading on superior analysis and is entirely lawful. Insider dealing requires non-public information and a duty not to use it.

Commonly confused with

Front-Running Regulation

Front-running uses knowledge of another participant's order. Insider dealing uses non-public information about the asset or issuer itself.
