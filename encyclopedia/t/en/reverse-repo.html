Reverse repurchase agreement

Simple definition

A short-term loan backed by securities, seen from the lender's side: you hand over cash, take bonds as security, and both legs unwind at an agreed price, usually the next day.

Professional definition

The cash-lending side of a repurchase agreement. Economically it is secured lending: the collateral protects the lender, and the repurchase price above the sale price is the interest. The same trade is a repo to the counterparty borrowing cash and a reverse repo to the one lending it.

Technical definition

The label depends on who is speaking, which is a frequent source of confusion. In a central-bank reverse repurchase facility, the central bank is the cash taker and the counterparty the cash lender, so the facility absorbs cash from money markets and puts a floor under short rates. Interest accrues on a money-market day-count basis, and haircuts and margining determine how much credit risk actually remains with the lender.

Academic definition

A collateralised money-market transaction viewed from the perspective of the cash provider, in which securities are purchased with a simultaneous agreement to resell at a specified price and date.

Mechanism

Because the cash is secured by collateral, the rate on it sits close to the risk-free overnight rate, and an official facility available to a wide set of counterparties becomes an effective floor: no one lends unsecured below a rate they can get risk-free. Usage of such a facility therefore rises when other short-term options pay less, not because anything has broken.

In practice

Who uses it
Money-market funds, bank treasuries, dealers financing inventory, and central banks implementing a rate floor.
Why it matters
It is the standard way to lend cash overnight against high-quality collateral, and official facility usage is read as a gauge of surplus cash in the system.
Limitations
It is secured but not riskless: collateral can be hard to value or liquidate under stress, and the legal treatment of title and close-out differs by jurisdiction.

Common mistakes

  • Using 'repo' and 'reverse repo' without saying whose side you mean.
  • Reading high facility usage as a crisis signal.
  • Ignoring haircuts when computing the real exposure.

Examples

numeric

A fund lends 100 million overnight at 4 percent against government collateral on an actual over 360 basis. It receives back the principal plus about 11,111 in interest the next morning.

Given
  • principal = 100,000,000
  • rate = 4.00% per annum
  • term = 1 day
  • day-count convention = actual/360
Steps
  1. interest = 100,000,000 x 0.04 x (1 / 360)
  2. = 100,000,000 x 0.000111111
  3. = 11,111.11
repurchase amount = 100,011,111.11

The rate is invented. Day-count conventions differ by market and currency; actual/365 would give a different interest amount for the same quoted rate.

market

A money fund can either buy a short bill or place cash in an official reverse repurchase facility. It chooses whichever pays more, so facility balances swell when bill yields sit below the facility rate.

Mathematical foundation

C_{repurchase} = C_{principal} \left( 1 + r \times \frac{d}{B} \right)

Variables

C_{repurchase}Cash returned to the lender at unwindcurrency
C_{principal}Cash advanced at the start, after any haircut on the collateralcurrency
rQuoted repo rate, annualiseddecimal per annum
dNumber of days the cash is outstandingdays
BDay-count basis: 360 or 365 depending on market conventiondays

Procedure

Agree the cash amount after applying the haircut to the collateral's market value, the rate and the term. Accrue simple interest on the money-market day-count basis for that currency, and settle the repurchase at principal plus accrued interest on the unwind date.

Worked example

Given
  • principal = 100,000,000
  • r = 0.04
  • d = 1
  • B = 360
Steps
  1. r x d / B = 0.04 / 360 = 0.000111111
  2. interest = 100,000,000 x 0.000111111 = 11,111.11
  3. repurchase = 100,000,000 + 11,111.11
repurchase amount = 100,011,111.11

Interpretation

The difference between the two legs is interest on secured cash, nothing more. It is quoted annualised, so a one-day trade earns roughly one three-hundred-sixtieth of the quoted rate under an actual over 360 convention.

Assumptions

  • Simple interest on a money-market basis; term repo of more than a year, or compounded structures, need a different formula.
  • The day-count basis is a market and currency convention, not a universal constant, and must be stated with the rate.
  • The principal is the post-haircut cash, so the lender's exposure to the collateral is smaller than its market value by the haircut.
Limits of the formula
  • The formula prices the cash leg only; it says nothing about collateral quality, re-use, or the right of substitution.
  • Under stress the realisable value of collateral, not the agreed rate, determines the lender's actual loss.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

The term is direction-dependent: the same trade is a repo for the cash borrower and a reverse repo for the cash lender, and in a central-bank facility the central bank is the cash taker. The entry states this explicitly because the two usages are routinely conflated. Facility eligibility, counterparty lists, haircuts and the legal treatment of collateral are jurisdiction-specific and change; no facility rate, balance or counterparty is quoted. The reading of facility usage as surplus cash rather than distress is stated as a mechanism, not as a measured relation. Rates and amounts in the examples are invented. Kurdish practitioners retain the English term, hence english-retained; native-expert review is still required.
