Logarithmic Returns

Simple definition

The natural logarithm of the ratio of two prices. Log returns add up across time, so a sequence of them can simply be summed to get the total.

Professional definition

The continuously compounded return, equal to the natural log of one plus the simple return. It is additive across time but not across the assets in a portfolio; the simple return is the exact opposite, and that single asymmetry decides which to use.

Technical definition

For prices P at t minus one and t, the log return is ln of P_t over P_t-1, identically equal to ln of one plus the simple return. Time-additivity follows from the logarithm of a product, so a multi-period log return is the sum of its single-period parts. Portfolio aggregation fails because the log of a weighted sum is not the weighted sum of logs. Log returns are also unbounded below, whereas a simple return cannot fall past minus one.

Academic definition

The continuously compounded rate of change of a price series, defined as the first difference of the logarithm of price, additive under time aggregation and non-additive under cross-sectional aggregation.

Mechanism

Compounding multiplies gross returns, and the logarithm turns multiplication into addition. That single property makes log returns the natural scale for time: sums, time-scaling of variance and continuous-time models all become straightforward. A portfolio, by contrast, adds value-weighted gross returns, and no logarithm distributes over a sum — hence the asymmetry.

In practice

Who uses it
Quantitative researchers building return series, volatility modellers, option pricers, and anyone aggregating performance over time.
Why it matters
Summing log returns gives an exact multi-period figure, and their variance scales linearly in time, which simple returns do not do.
Limitations
They cannot be weighted into a portfolio return, they are not what a client's statement shows, and for large moves they differ materially from simple returns.

Common mistakes

  • Weighting log returns by portfolio weights to get a portfolio return.
  • Reporting a log return to a client as the realised percentage gain.
  • Mixing log and simple returns inside one performance table.

Examples

numeric

Up 50% then down 50%: log returns +0.405465 and -0.693147 sum to -0.287682, and e to that power is 0.75 — a 25% loss. Adding the simple returns gives zero, which is wrong.

Given
  • period 1 simple return = +50%
  • period 2 simple return = -50%
Steps
  1. ln(1 + 0.50) = +0.405465
  2. ln(1 - 0.50) = -0.693147
  3. sum = -0.287682, which equals ln(0.75)
  4. exp(-0.287682) = 0.75, so the simple total return is -25%
  5. by contrast 0.50 + (-0.50) = 0, which is not the realised result
total log return -0.287682; total simple return -25%

Verified arithmetic. This is the clearest demonstration that simple returns are not additive across time while log returns are.

pitfall

An equally weighted pair: one asset +10%, the other -10%. The portfolio ends flat, and the weighted simple returns give exactly 0%. Weighting the log returns gives -0.50%, a loss that never happened.

Mathematical foundation

r^{\log}_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) = \ln\!\left(1 + r^{\text{simple}}_t\right), \qquad r^{\log}_{1:T} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} r^{\log}_t

Procedure

Divide each closing price by the previous one and take the natural logarithm, or equivalently take the log of one plus the simple return. To aggregate over time, add the log returns. To convert back to a percentage, exponentiate the sum and subtract one. To aggregate across assets, convert back to simple returns first and only then apply portfolio weights.

Worked example

Given
  • period 1: +50% simple
  • period 2: -50% simple
Steps
  1. r_log,1 = ln(1.50) = 0.405465
  2. r_log,2 = ln(0.50) = -0.693147
  3. sum = -0.287682
  4. ln(0.75) = -0.287682, so the two agree exactly
  5. exp(-0.287682) - 1 = -0.25
cumulative log return -0.287682; cumulative simple return -25%

Interpretation

A log return of zero means no change, a positive one a gain and a negative one a loss, but the number is not a percentage. Only after exponentiating does it become the figure an account statement would show.

Assumptions

  • Prices are strictly positive, so the logarithm is defined. A price reaching zero gives a log return of minus infinity.
  • The identity with ln of one plus the simple return is exact, not an approximation; the familiar claim that log and simple returns are 'about the same' holds only for small moves.
  • Dividends, funding and fees must be built into the price series first, otherwise both return measures are incomplete in the same way.
Limits of the formula
  • Not additive across assets: a portfolio log return is not the weighted average of component log returns.
  • Not the figure reported to clients or used in most performance standards, which use simple returns.
  • Unbounded below, so distributional assumptions that look harmless on log returns can imply impossible simple returns.

Commonly confused with

Compounding versus Simple Returns

Log returns are the measurement scale; compounding is the arithmetic that makes that scale useful. The log return is the continuously compounded rate, so the two topics are two views of one fact.
