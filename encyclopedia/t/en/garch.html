Simple definition

A formula for tomorrow's expected variance built from today's surprise and today's variance. It makes calm and turbulent periods persist instead of resetting every day.

Professional definition

A model in which conditional variance is a weighted sum of a constant, the latest squared shock and the previous conditional variance. The two weights separate how fast variance reacts to news from how long that reaction lasts, which is why one lag of each usually fits long return series well.

Technical definition

The generalisation of ARCH that adds lagged conditional variance to the variance equation. In the first-order case the weights on the squared shock and on the lagged variance sum to the persistence of the process: the sum must be below one for the unconditional variance to exist, and the long-run variance is the constant divided by one minus that sum. Shock decay is geometric at a rate given by the same sum.

Academic definition

A conditional-variance specification in which current variance is linear in lagged squared innovations and lagged conditional variances, yielding covariance stationarity when the coefficient sum is less than unity.

Mechanism

The squared-shock term makes variance jump when something happens; the lagged-variance term makes that jump fade slowly rather than at once. Together they turn a single surprise into a decaying path of elevated risk, which is what observed clustering looks like. The sum of the two weights controls how long the memory is, and as it approaches one the process barely forgets.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk teams producing variance forecasts, option desks comparing realised with implied volatility, and researchers who need a variance model before testing anything else.
Why it matters
It gives a one-step variance forecast and a long-run anchor from the same fitted parameters, which is what margin and position-size rules need.
Limitations
It forecasts magnitude only, the fit is sensitive to the sample and frequency, and a persistence sum near one means forecasts revert so slowly that they are effectively trend-following.

Common mistakes

  • Using a fitted model across a regime change without refitting.
  • Reading a volatility forecast as a direction forecast.
  • Ignoring whether the estimated persistence sum is below one.

Examples

numeric

With a constant of 0.000002, a shock weight of 0.08 and a variance weight of 0.90, persistence is 0.98. Long-run daily standard deviation is 1%, and half of any shock to variance is still present about 34 days later.

Given
  • omega = 0.000002
  • alpha = 0.08
  • beta = 0.90
Steps
  1. persistence = alpha + beta = 0.98, which is below 1 so the unconditional variance exists
  2. long-run variance = 0.000002 / (1 - 0.98) = 0.000002 / 0.02 = 0.0001
  3. long-run sd = sqrt(0.0001) = 0.01
  4. half-life = ln(0.5) / ln(0.98) = 34.3 periods
long-run daily sd = 1%, shock half-life about 34 days

Parameters are illustrative. Had alpha plus beta been 1 or more, no long-run variance would exist and the division above would be meaningless.

market

A desk refits the model each quarter. After a turbulent quarter the shock weight rises and the forecast mean-reverts more slowly, so margin requirements derived from it stay elevated long after prices have steadied.

Mathematical foundation

\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2, \qquad \omega > 0,\ \alpha \ge 0,\ \beta \ge 0,\ \alpha + \beta < 1

Variables

\sigma_t^2Conditional variance for period tsquared return
\omegaConstant term; fixes the level of the long-run variancesquared return
\alphaReaction: weight on the latest squared innovation, how sharply variance responds to newsdimensionless
\betaPersistence: weight on the previous conditional variance, how long a shock lingersdimensionless
\alpha + \betaTotal persistence; must be below one for a finite unconditional variancedimensionless
\bar{\sigma}^2Long-run (unconditional) variance, equal to omega divided by one minus alpha minus betasquared return

Procedure

Specify a mean equation and take its residuals as the innovations. Estimate the three variance parameters jointly by maximum likelihood. Check that the sum of the two weights is below one, then compute the long-run variance and the shock half-life from the fitted values and report the sample used.

Worked example

Given
  • omega = 0.000002
  • alpha = 0.08
  • beta = 0.90
Steps
  1. alpha + beta = 0.98 < 1
  2. long-run variance = 0.000002 / 0.02 = 0.0001
  3. long-run sd = 0.01
  4. half-life = ln(0.5) / ln(0.98) = 34.3
long-run sd 1% per period; half-life about 34 periods

Interpretation

Alpha is how hard variance reacts and beta is how long it remembers. Their sum is the memory of the series: near one, variance forecasts drift for months; well below one, they snap back to the long-run level within weeks.

Assumptions

  • The first-order form shown here is the common working case; higher orders and asymmetric variants exist and give different fitted persistence.
  • Non-negativity of the coefficients and omega above zero keep the variance positive; the sum below one is required for covariance stationarity and for the long-run variance formula to be meaningful.
  • The innovation distribution must be specified for likelihood estimation. A normal assumption is common but leaves residual excess kurtosis, so heavier-tailed choices change the estimates.
Limits of the formula
  • The model captures the magnitude of risk and says nothing about direction, so it cannot generate an entry signal on its own.
  • Fitted parameters are unstable: the same series estimated on different windows or frequencies can give materially different persistence, and a half-life computed from one fit should not be treated as a property of the asset.

Commonly confused with

Implied Volatility

GARCH estimates variance from realised returns, looking backwards. Implied volatility is extracted from option prices and is forward-looking under a pricing model. The two can disagree for long stretches without either being wrong.
