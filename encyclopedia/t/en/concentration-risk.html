Concentration Risk

Simple definition

The risk from having too much of your money in one thing: one asset, one venue, one issuer or one theme. If that thing fails, the loss is not recoverable from the rest.

Professional definition

Exposure that is not spread, measured along whichever dimension can fail as a unit: a single asset, a counterparty, a custodian, a chain, a sector or a factor. Equal nominal weights do not remove it when several holdings share the same failure mode.

Technical definition

Quantified on a weight vector, most simply by the sum of squared weights, whose reciprocal is the effective number of positions. A portfolio of five equal weights has an effective number of five; shifting to sixty percent in one name drops it to two and a half while the position count stays at five. The dimension chosen for the weights matters as much as the arithmetic, because concentration in custody or venue is invisible in an asset-level calculation.

Academic definition

The degree to which portfolio exposure is unevenly distributed across the units capable of independent failure, commonly summarised by a Herfindahl-type index and its reciprocal.

Mechanism

A loss is recoverable in proportion to what remains. Ninety percent in one asset means a total loss there cannot be offset, no matter how the rest performs. The arithmetic of squared weights captures this: large weights contribute to the index quadratically, so the index is driven by the biggest positions and barely notices the small ones.

In practice

Who uses it
Portfolio managers and risk committees setting position limits, crypto holders spread across venues and chains, and anyone reviewing where a book would fail first.
Why it matters
It turns diversification from a count of names into a measurable property, and it forces the question of which dimension the weights should be taken on.
Limitations
A weight-based index says nothing about correlation or about the probability of failure, and it depends entirely on how exposures are grouped.

Common mistakes

  • Counting the number of holdings as a measure of diversification.
  • Measuring only on assets while all of them sit at one exchange.
  • Raising the largest position because it has performed best.

Examples

numeric

Five equal positions give a squared-weight sum of 0.20 and an effective number of five. Move to 60 percent in one name with 10 percent in each of four others and the index rises to 0.40, halving the effective number to two and a half.

Given
  • case A weights: 0.20 each across five positions
  • case B weights: 0.60, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10
Steps
  1. case A: 5 x 0.20^2 = 5 x 0.04 = 0.20, effective number = 1 / 0.20 = 5
  2. case B: 0.60^2 = 0.36 and 4 x 0.10^2 = 0.04
  3. case B index = 0.36 + 0.04 = 0.40, effective number = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5
Five positions in both cases; effective diversification of five versus two and a half.

The position count is unchanged. Only the distribution of weights changed, and that is what the measure is for.

crypto

A holder splits across eight tokens and feels diversified, but every token sits in one exchange account. On the asset dimension the concentration index is low; on the custody dimension it is one, and a venue failure takes everything.

Mathematical foundation

C = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i^2, \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{C}

Procedure

Choose the dimension that can fail as a unit: asset, issuer, counterparty, custodian, chain, sector or factor. Express each exposure as a fraction of the relevant total, square each fraction, and sum. Take the reciprocal for the effective number of exposures. Repeat on every dimension that matters, since a portfolio can be diversified on one and concentrated on another.

Worked example

Given
  • weights: 0.60, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10
  • all weights sum to 1.00
Steps
  1. 0.60^2 = 0.36
  2. 0.10^2 = 0.01, and four of them give 0.04
  3. C = 0.36 + 0.04 = 0.40
  4. N_eff = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5
C = 0.40 and an effective number of 2.5 exposures from five positions.

Interpretation

The index runs from one over the number of exposures, for perfectly even weights, to one, for everything in a single exposure. The reciprocal is easier to read: it is the number of equally weighted positions that would carry the same concentration.

Assumptions

  • Weights are non-negative and sum to one. Short and levered books need gross exposures defined explicitly before the index means anything.
  • Each exposure is treated as a separate unit of failure. Holdings that fail together should be grouped before the weights are computed.
  • The measure is weight-based only. It assumes nothing about volatility, correlation or probability of loss.
Limits of the formula
  • Two portfolios with the same index can carry very different risk if their constituents differ in volatility or dependence.
  • The grouping choice dominates the result. Eight tokens at one custodian are eight exposures on the asset dimension and one on the custody dimension.
  • The index gives no threshold. What counts as too concentrated is a policy decision, not an output of the formula.

Commonly confused with

Position Concentration Limits

Concentration risk is the exposure itself, measured. Position concentration limits are the policy rules written to cap it. The measure describes the book; the limits constrain it.
