Face value

ENGLISHFace valuereference

Financial Foundations
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

The amount printed on the instrument — what the issuer promises to repay at maturity. It is the base for interest, not a statement of what the instrument is worth today.

Professional definition

The principal amount repayable at maturity and the base on which coupons are calculated. In bond markets it is used interchangeably with par; in insurance and some contracts it names the stated benefit amount instead.

Technical definition

A denominational constant. Because the face repayment and the coupon are both fixed in currency, a change in required yield can only be absorbed by price: this is why price and yield move inversely, by arithmetic necessity rather than by market psychology. Face value also anchors notional-based calculations where no principal is ever exchanged.

Academic definition

The nominal principal amount stated on a financial instrument, constituting the contractual basis for interest computation and terminal repayment.

Mechanism

The repayment at maturity pulls the price toward face as the final date approaches, regardless of what happened in between. Combined with a fixed coupon, that convergence is what makes the price-yield relationship mechanical: there is simply nothing else in the contract left to move.

In practice

Who uses it
Settlement and operations teams converting quotes to cash, treasury teams sizing an issue, and derivatives desks working in notional terms.
Why it matters
Interest, redemption and most exposure figures are all calculated from it, so getting it wrong misstates every downstream number.
Limitations
Says nothing about recovery: in default, holders receive what the estate pays, which can be far below face even though the contract says otherwise.

Common mistakes

  • Reading face value as market value.
  • Using face value as exposure for a swap where no principal is exchanged.
  • Overlooking a different face in a different currency tranche.

Examples

market

A deeply distressed bond with face 1,000 changes hands at 220. The contract still promises 1,000 at maturity; the market is pricing the chance that it never arrives, which the face amount cannot express.

numeric

A zero-coupon bond with face 1,000 due in five years, bought at 747.26, pays nothing until maturity. The entire return is the climb from price to face, equivalent to 6% a year.

Given
  • F = 1,000
  • n = 5 years
  • price = 747.26
  • no coupons
Steps
  1. (1,000 / 747.26) = 1.33822
  2. 1.33822^(1/5) = 1.06
annual yield = 6%

With no coupons there is no reinvestment assumption, so the quoted yield is realised if held to maturity and the issuer pays.

Commonly confused with

Par value

In bond markets the two words are used for the same amount. Par value additionally has a separate meaning for shares, where it is a legal capital figure with no pricing role.
