Simple definition

How good your actual fill was compared with the price you were aiming for, once every cost is counted.

Professional definition

The measurement of what execution really cost against a stated benchmark. The honest measure is implementation shortfall: the gap between the price at which the decision was taken and the price realised, including fees and the cost of whatever never got filled.

Technical definition

A family of measures, each defined by its benchmark: quoted spread, effective spread, arrival price, interval volume-weighted average price, or the decision price. Implementation shortfall is the most complete because it charges the strategy for delay, for market impact, for explicit costs and for unexecuted quantity. Benchmarks are not interchangeable: an algorithm can beat the interval average price and still show a large shortfall if it waited while price ran away. Measurement needs timestamps most retail platforms never expose.

Academic definition

The evaluation of realised transaction prices against a specified benchmark, with implementation shortfall measuring the total difference between a paper portfolio struck at the decision price and the portfolio actually obtained.

Mechanism

Every execution choice trades impact against delay. Trading fast pays impact; trading slowly pays the risk that price moves away. Shortfall is the only common currency in which those two costs can be added together, which is why it is the benchmark that cannot be flattered by simply trading less.

In practice

Who uses it
Execution desks, algorithm developers, compliance and best-execution reporting, and any serious backtester.
Why it matters
It is the only honest answer to whether an entry was cheap, because it prices delay and non-execution, which spread-based measures ignore entirely.
Limitations
It needs a timestamped decision price, which many platforms never record, and the opportunity-cost term depends on a chosen end-of-horizon price.

Common mistakes

  • Judging execution by the fill price alone.
  • Comparing shortfall figures computed against different benchmarks.
  • Excluding unfilled quantity, which flatters a patient algorithm.

Examples

market

An algorithm reports beating the interval average price by a few basis points. Measured from the decision price it is far worse: it waited two hours while the market moved away, and that delay never appeared in its chosen benchmark.

numeric

An intention to buy 1,000 at a decision price of 100.00 ends as 800 filled at 100.25, with 40.10 of fees, while the unfilled 200 would now cost 101.00. Shortfall is 440.10, or 44 basis points.

Given
  • intended quantity Q = 1,000, decision price 100.00
  • filled Q_f = 800 at a weighted average of 100.25
  • explicit costs 40.10 (0.05% of traded notional)
  • end-of-horizon price for the unfilled 200 is 101.00
Steps
  1. execution cost = 800 x (100.25 - 100.00) = 200.00
  2. explicit costs = 40.10
  3. opportunity cost = 200 x (101.00 - 100.00) = 200.00
  4. IS = 200.00 + 40.10 + 200.00 = 440.10
  5. as a fraction of intended notional = 440.10 / (1000 x 100.00) = 0.004401
implementation shortfall 440.10, or 44.0 basis points of the intended notional

Nearly half the shortfall comes from the quantity that never traded. A measure that ignores unfilled size would have reported roughly 24 basis points.

Mathematical foundation

IS = Q_f \left( \bar{P}_{exec} - P_d \right) + C + \left( Q - Q_f \right) \left( P_{end} - P_d \right)

Variables

Q_fQuantity actually filledunits or lots

Procedure

Record the decision price with a timestamp before the order is sent. After the horizon, compute the weighted average fill price on the executed quantity and multiply the difference by that quantity. Add the explicit costs. Price the unexecuted quantity at the end-of-horizon reference and add that too. Divide by the intended notional to express the result in basis points.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 1000, Q_f = 800
  • P_d = 100.00, average fill 100.25
  • C = 40.10
  • P_end = 101.00
Steps
  1. 800 x (100.25 - 100.00) = 200.00
  2. C = 40.10
  3. (1000 - 800) x (101.00 - 100.00) = 200.00
  4. IS = 200.00 + 40.10 + 200.00 = 440.10
  5. 440.10 / 100000 = 0.004401
IS = 440.10, i.e. 44.0 basis points of the intended notional

Interpretation

A positive value is a cost: the paper portfolio struck at the decision price would have done better by that amount. The decomposition matters more than the total, because delay, impact, fees and missed quantity call for different fixes.

Assumptions

  • The decision price must be defined and timestamped before the order is sent. Using the arrival price or the previous close instead gives a different number that is not comparable with it.
  • The sign convention here makes a positive value a cost for a buy order; for a sell, both price differences reverse.
  • The opportunity-cost term requires a stated end-of-horizon price, which is a choice, so two desks can report different shortfalls on the same order.
Limits of the formula
  • It evaluates one order against one benchmark and says nothing about whether the underlying decision was any good.
  • Delay between the decision and order entry is charged to execution here, although it is often an upstream organisational cost rather than a trading one.

Commonly confused with

Slippage

Slippage compares the fill with the price visible when the order was sent. Implementation shortfall compares it with the price when the decision was taken, and also charges for fees and for quantity never filled.
