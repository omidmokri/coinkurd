Stop-Limit Order

Simple definition

A stop that turns into a limit order instead of a market order. It protects you from a terrible price by accepting that you may not trade at all.

Professional definition

Two prices: a trigger that activates the order and a limit that bounds the fill. It removes the open-ended slippage of a plain stop and replaces it with non-execution risk — the position stays on if price runs straight through the limit.

Technical definition

On trigger the venue submits a limit order at the stated limit, which then behaves like any resting limit and fills only while the opposite side is at or better than that price. In a gap or a cascade the market trades through the limit in a single print, the order rests unfilled on the wrong side of the move, and the exposure the stop existed to end carries on. The failure mode is therefore correlated with the very events the order was placed for.

Academic definition

A doubly-conditioned instruction whose activation and execution constraints are specified separately, bounding realised price at the cost of a non-execution probability that rises with the speed of the triggering move.

Mechanism

The gap between trigger and limit is the slippage you agree to accept in advance. Set it narrow and the order is a precise exit in orderly markets and a dead letter in disorderly ones; set it wide and it converges on a plain stop. No setting delivers both guarantees at once.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders exiting illiquid instruments, desks with a maximum acceptable exit price, and anyone who has been filled into a vacuum by a plain stop.
Why it matters
It caps the price concession on exit, which matters most in thin books where a plain stop can fill several percent away from the level.
Limitations
It can fail precisely when it is most needed: in a gap it never fills, and the loss it was meant to bound keeps growing.

Common mistakes

  • Setting the limit at the trigger price, so any fast move leaves it unfilled.
  • Treating it as a strictly safer stop rather than a different trade-off.
  • Using it as the only exit on a leveraged position.

Examples

market

A trader replaces a stop at 48.00 with a stop-limit triggering at 48.00 and limited to 47.90. In an orderly slide it exits at 47.92. In an overnight gap to 41 it never fills, and the loss continues to run.

numeric

On a 500-unit position, a plain stop filled at 47.63 realises a loss of 1,185 from entry at 50. The stop-limit at 47.90 does not fill in the gap, and by 41 the open loss is 4,500.

Given
  • long 500 units, entry 50.00
  • plain stop average fill 47.63
  • stop-limit: trigger 48.00, limit 47.90; market gaps to 41.00 without trading at or above 47.90
Steps
  1. plain stop loss = (50.00 - 47.63) x 500 = 2.37 x 500 = 1185
  2. stop-limit: no fill, position still open
  3. open loss at 41.00 = (50.00 - 41.00) x 500 = 4500
1,185 realised with the plain stop versus 4,500 still open with the stop-limit

Illustrative, not a general ranking. In an orderly market the stop-limit would have been the cheaper of the two. The point is that its failure is correlated with gaps.

Commonly confused with

Stop Order

A stop order becomes a market order and always trades, at an unknown price. A stop-limit becomes a limit order and trades only inside your price, or not at all.
