Foundation

Simple definition

A contract giving its buyer the right to sell the underlying at a fixed price until a set date. It gains value when the underlying falls.

Professional definition

The right, not the obligation, to sell a stated quantity of the underlying at the strike price on or before expiry. Payoff at expiry is max(K−S,0). The holder's loss is capped at the premium; the writer is obliged to buy at the strike however far price has fallen.

Technical definition

A contingent claim whose terminal value is max(K−S,0), with K the strike and S the underlying price at expiry. A long put carries negative delta, positive gamma and negative theta. Its maximum value is bounded by the strike, so unlike a call it has a finite ceiling, and a written put's worst case is the strike less the premium received, reached only if the underlying goes to zero.

Academic definition

A contingent claim conferring the unilateral right to sell a specified underlying at a predetermined strike on or before a stated expiry, with terminal payoff equal to the positive part of the strike less the underlying price.

Mechanism

A put is priced insurance on a level. Below the strike each unit the underlying loses is a unit the put gains, so the buyer converts an open downside into a known cost. The writer sells that protection and is left long the asset at the strike exactly when nobody else wants it, which is why written puts behave like underwriting, not like income.

In practice

Who uses it
Holders hedging a position they do not want to sell, funds buying tail protection, and writers collecting premium against cash they are willing to deploy.
Why it matters
It places a floor under a long position without closing it, leaving the upside intact and the cost of the floor known in advance.
Limitations
Protection is rented, not owned. Each expiry has to be paid for again, and the cost rises precisely when fear is already priced in.

Common mistakes

  • Buying puts only after the fall, when the premium already contains the move.
  • Writing puts on size that cash on hand cannot absorb.
  • Reading a put's premium as the maximum cost of a repeated hedge.

Examples

market

A holder who wants to keep an asset through a known event date can buy puts expiring after it. The position survives the event with a floor at the strike, and the premium is the price of not having to sell beforehand.

numeric

A put with strike 100 costs 5. At expiry at 88 it pays 12, a profit of 7. At 97 it pays 3, a loss of 2. At 105 it pays nothing and the loss is 5. Breakeven is 95.

Given
  • K = 100
  • premium p = 5
  • European exercise, one unit of underlying, fees ignored
Steps
  1. S_T = 88: payoff = max(100 - 88, 0) = 12, profit = 12 - 5 = 7
  2. S_T = 97: payoff = 3, profit = -2
  3. S_T = 105: payoff = 0, profit = -5
breakeven at expiry = K - p = 95; maximum payoff = K = 100, reached only at S_T = 0

A put's gain is bounded by the strike, because the underlying cannot fall below zero.

Mathematical foundation

\text{Payoff}_{\text{put}} = \max(K - S_T,\ 0), \qquad C - P = S - K e^{-rT}

Procedure

For the payoff, subtract the underlying price at expiry from the strike and floor the result at zero. For parity, discount the strike at the risk-free rate over the remaining life, subtract it from today's underlying price, and the result must equal the call premium minus the put premium for the same strike and expiry.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 100
  • K = 100
  • r = 0.05 per year, continuously compounded
  • T = 0.5 years
  • observed call premium C = 6.00
Steps
  1. rT = 0.05 x 0.5 = 0.025
  2. e^(-0.025) = 0.97531
  3. K e^(-rT) = 100 x 0.97531 = 97.531
  4. C - P = 100 - 97.531 = 2.469
  5. P = 6.00 - 2.469
P = 3.531; a put quoted materially away from 3.53 is inconsistent with the quoted call, not necessarily mispriced in isolation

Interpretation

Parity is an arbitrage relation, not a model: it ties the two premiums together without any assumption about volatility. It is how desks check quotes for internal consistency and how a synthetic long or short is built from the other three legs.

Assumptions

  • Parity as written holds for European-style options on a non-dividend-paying, non-carrying underlying, with the same strike and the same expiry on both legs.
  • The rate r is continuously compounded. With simple or periodic compounding the discount factor changes form and the arithmetic differs.
  • An underlying with a dividend or a carry yield q requires replacing S with S e^{-qT}. For a futures-settled contract the forward price replaces S and both legs are discounted.
  • Frictionless markets: no fees, no bid-ask spread, no borrow cost and free short selling. Observed quotes deviate inside those costs without implying arbitrage.
  • For American-style options the equality becomes a pair of inequalities, because early exercise has value.
Limits of the formula
  • Parity constrains the pair of premiums; it does not price either one. Both can be expensive or cheap together and still satisfy it.
  • The payoff identity describes expiry only. Before expiry a put's value also depends on the volatility input and the remaining time, which parity says nothing about.
