Simple definition

The Federal Reserve sets a short-term interest rate for the United States. Moving it changes the cost of borrowing across the economy, and that is how monetary policy reaches prices and jobs.

Professional definition

The Federal Open Market Committee sets a target range for the federal funds rate and keeps the effective rate inside it using administered rates and open-market tools. Its statutory goals are maximum employment and price stability. The target range was 3-3/4 to 4 percent as of 28 October 2026.

Technical definition

Policy is implemented in an ample-reserves regime: the rate paid on reserve balances and the overnight reverse repurchase rate bracket the effective federal funds rate inside the Committee's target range, rather than open-market operations rationing scarce reserves. Transmission runs through bank funding costs, the yield curve, credit conditions, asset prices and the exchange rate, with long and variable lags. The target range was 3-3/4 to 4 percent as of 28 October 2026.

Academic definition

The administered short-term interest rate through which a central bank with a dual mandate conditions financial conditions, aggregate demand, and ultimately inflation and employment outcomes.

Mechanism

A policy rate matters only through what it does to everything priced off it. A higher rate raises funding costs, tightens credit supply, lowers the present value of distant cash flows and tends to firm the currency. Expectations of the future path, not merely today's level, do most of the work, because long rates are built from expected short rates plus a premium.

In practice

Who uses it
Rate and currency desks, bank treasuries, borrowers and lenders, and any analyst choosing a discount rate.
Why it matters
Almost every valuation discounts future cash flows, so the expected path of the policy rate is embedded in the price of nearly everything.
Limitations
This entry describes the United States. Other central banks have different mandates, instruments and operating frameworks, and the stance quoted here carries its as-of date.

Common mistakes

  • Reading one meeting's decision as the whole policy path.
  • Assuming a rate change reaches the real economy immediately.
  • Applying the Fed's framework to another country's central bank.

Examples

market

When markets reprice the expected path rather than the current level, long-dated bonds and long-duration equities move first. The policy rate itself may not have changed at all that day.

numeric

A cash flow of 100 due in five years is worth 82.19 today at a 4 percent discount rate and 78.35 at 5 percent. One extra point of rate removes about 4.7 percent of its present value.

Given
  • single cash flow = 100
  • horizon = 5 years
  • discount rate r1 = 4 percent, r2 = 5 percent
  • annual compounding, no credit or liquidity premium
Steps
  1. 100 / 1.04^5 = 100 / 1.21665 = 82.19
  2. 100 / 1.05^5 = 100 / 1.27628 = 78.35
  3. (78.35 - 82.19) / 82.19 = -0.0467
present value falls from 82.19 to 78.35, about 4.7 percent

Illustrative discount rates, not quoted market rates. The same arithmetic is why long-duration assets react most to rate repricing.

Commonly confused with

Quantitative Easing

The policy rate is a price set for overnight money. Quantitative easing works on the quantity and maturity of assets held, and was used when the rate itself was near its lower bound.
