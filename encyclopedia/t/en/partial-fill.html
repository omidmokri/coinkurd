Partial Fill

Simple definition

When only part of your order trades. The rest either keeps waiting or is cancelled, depending on the instruction you attached.

Professional definition

An execution of less than the full order quantity. What happens to the remainder is set by the time in force: a good-till-cancelled limit keeps resting, an immediate-or-cancel cancels it, a fill-or-kill would have prevented the partial fill entirely. The realised price is the quantity-weighted average of the fills.

Technical definition

Partial execution is the normal case, not an exception: one order commonly produces many fills at several prices, each with its own timestamp, maker or taker label and fee. Average price must therefore be computed as a quantity-weighted mean, and positions, stops and hedges must be reconciled against filled quantity rather than requested quantity. Systems that assume one order equals one fill break here, and a stop sized to the requested quantity can leave residual exposure or reverse the position.

Academic definition

Execution of a strict subset of an order's quantity, with the residual either persisting as a resting order or being cancelled according to the order's validity condition.

Mechanism

An order is matched against discrete resting quantities, and there is no reason for those to sum to exactly what was asked for. Partial fills are therefore a direct consequence of depth being lumpy, and they become more frequent as order size approaches the size of a single price level.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone trading size relative to depth, plus reconciliation and risk systems and anyone building against an exchange API.
Why it matters
Average price, fees and remaining exposure are all computed from the fills rather than from the order, so every post-trade number depends on them.
Limitations
Some venues report fills aggregated per order and others per execution, and the maker or taker role can differ between fills of a single order.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing a stop to the requested quantity rather than the filled quantity.
  • Averaging fill prices without weighting by quantity.
  • Reading a cancelled remainder as a failed order.

Examples

market

A resting limit for 1,000 is filled 200 in the morning and 300 in the afternoon, and the trader cancels the remaining 500. The position is 500, not 1,000, and any hedge written against 1,000 is now wrong.

numeric

Three fills of 120 at 100.00, 80 at 100.10 and 50 at 100.25 give a quantity-weighted average of 100.082. The simple average of the three prices, 100.117, overstates the cost.

Given
  • fills: 120 at 100.00, 80 at 100.10, 50 at 100.25
  • total filled 250
  • fees excluded
Steps
  1. cost = 120 x 100.00 + 80 x 100.10 + 50 x 100.25 = 12000 + 8008 + 5012.5 = 25020.5
  2. quantity-weighted average = 25020.5 / 250 = 100.082
  3. unweighted average of prices = (100.00 + 100.10 + 100.25) / 3 = 100.1167
weighted 100.082 against unweighted 100.117, a difference of 3.5 basis points

The unweighted figure is simply wrong for cost purposes; it ignores that most of the quantity traded at the best price.

Mathematical foundation

\bar{P} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} q_i P_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} q_i}

Variables

\bar{P}Quantity-weighted average fill price of the orderquote currency
q_iQuantity of fill iunits or lots
P_iPrice of fill iquote currency
kNumber of fills produced by the ordercount

Procedure

Multiply each fill's quantity by its price and sum. Divide by the total filled quantity, not by the requested quantity. Record fees separately per fill, because the maker or taker rate can differ between fills of the same order.

Worked example

Given
  • q = (120, 80, 50)
  • P = (100.00, 100.10, 100.25)
Steps
  1. sum q_i P_i = 12000 + 8008 + 5012.5 = 25020.5
  2. sum q_i = 250
  3. 25020.5 / 250 = 100.082
average fill price 100.082 on 250 units filled

Interpretation

The output is the single price that reproduces the cash actually paid for the quantity actually received. It is the correct basis for profit and loss, for comparing against a benchmark, and for sizing the remaining order.

Assumptions

  • Fees are excluded from this average; adding them per fill gives the all-in cost used in execution analysis.
  • All fills are in the same instrument and the same quote currency, with no contract-size differences between them.
Limits of the formula
  • The average price hides the dispersion of the fills, which is itself information about depth and impact.
  • Where a venue aggregates reporting per order rather than per fill, the individual q and P are not recoverable from the report.

Commonly confused with

Slippage

Slippage is the price difference between what was expected and what was obtained. A partial fill is about quantity: how much traded. A single order can show either, or both at once.
