Relative Strength Index

Simple definition

A 0 to 100 gauge of how one-sided recent price moves have been. High readings mean up-days have dominated, low readings mean down-days have.

Professional definition

Wilder's momentum oscillator, comparing the smoothed average size of up-closes with that of down-closes over a lookback, usually 14 periods, mapped onto 0 to 100. Above 70 is conventionally called overbought and below 30 oversold.

Technical definition

A normalised ratio of Wilder-smoothed average gain to average loss. Being bounded and smoothed, it measures the recent distribution of one-period changes rather than trend direction. In a strong trend it saturates and stays at an extreme while price continues, which is why level-based reversal signals fail there; its better-studied use is divergence against price.

Academic definition

A bounded momentum oscillator that normalises the ratio of smoothed positive to smoothed negative price changes over a fixed lookback onto the interval [0, 100].

Mechanism

Because gains and losses pass through the same smoothing window, RSI reports the balance of recent one-period changes. Saturation follows from the bound: as average loss approaches zero the ratio explodes and the map compresses everything into the top few points, so additional strength cannot register.

In practice

Who uses it
Technical analysts, systematic strategy developers, and anyone screening for momentum exhaustion or divergence.
Why it matters
It turns a price series into a bounded, comparable momentum figure that can be screened across instruments and timeframes.
Limitations
Levels are not comparable across different lookbacks or smoothing implementations, and overbought does not mean a reversal is due.

Common mistakes

  • Shorting a market only because RSI is above 70.
  • Comparing RSI(14) daily with RSI(7) hourly as if they were one scale.
  • Trading divergence on a low timeframe with no higher-timeframe context.

Examples

market

In a strong uptrend, daily RSI can hold above 70 for weeks while price keeps rising. Traders who shorted each 70 reading were fighting the trend; those who waited for RSI to make a lower high while price made a higher high were reading divergence.

numeric

Over 14 periods, average gain is 1.2% and average loss 0.8%. RS is 1.5, so RSI is 60 — recent up-moves have been half again as large on average as down-moves.

Given
  • n = 14
  • average gain = 1.2%
  • average loss = 0.8% (entered as a positive magnitude)
Steps
  1. RS = 1.2 / 0.8 = 1.5
  2. RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + 1.5) = 100 - 40
RSI = 60

A reading of 60 describes the recent balance of changes. It is not a probability of any future move.

Mathematical foundation

RSI = 100 - \frac{100}{1 + RS}, \qquad RS = \frac{\overline{U}_n}{\overline{D}_n}

Variables

RSRelative strength: average gain divided by average lossratio (dimensionless)
\overline{U}_nWilder-smoothed average of up-closes over n periodsprice units or %
\overline{D}_nWilder-smoothed average of down-closes over n periods, as positive magnitudesprice units or %
nLookback length; Wilder used 14periods

Procedure

Split each period's close-to-close change into a gain or a loss. Smooth each series with Wilder's method: the first value is a simple mean of n periods, then each later value is (previous x (n-1) + current) / n. Divide average gain by average loss to get RS, then map it into 0-100 with the formula above.

Worked example

Given
  • n = 14
  • average gain over the window = 1.2%
  • average loss over the window = 0.8%
Steps
  1. RS = 1.2 / 0.8 = 1.5
  2. 1 + RS = 2.5
  3. 100 / 2.5 = 40
  4. RSI = 100 - 40
RSI = 60

Interpretation

The output is a position on a fixed 0-100 scale, where 50 means up and down moves have been equal in average size. It describes what has already happened to the distribution of changes; it carries no forecast.

Assumptions

  • Wilder's smoothing, not a simple moving average. Platforms that smooth with an SMA produce different numbers from the same price data.
  • Losses enter the average as positive magnitudes.
  • If the average loss over the window is zero, RS is undefined and RSI is set to 100 by convention.
Limits of the formula
  • Bounded at 0 and 100, so it compresses information in strong trends and can sit above 70 for long stretches without a reversal.
  • The lookback n and the input series (close versus typical price) both change the output; a level is only comparable within one fixed configuration.

Commonly confused with

Stochastic Oscillator

RSI compares the average size of gains with losses. The stochastic compares the close with the high-low range. Similar scale, different question.
