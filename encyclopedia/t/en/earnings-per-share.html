Earnings Per Share

Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A company's profit divided by the number of shares it has issued. It says how much of the period's profit belongs to each single share.

Professional definition

Profit attributable to ordinary shareholders, after tax and after any preferred dividend, divided by the weighted average number of ordinary shares outstanding. Basic EPS counts shares actually issued; diluted EPS adds the shares that options, convertibles and awards would create.

Technical definition

The numerator is an accrual profit after interest, tax, depreciation and preferred claims, so it inherits every estimate in the accounts. The denominator is time-weighted, which is why a mid-year buyback lifts EPS with no operating improvement. Diluted EPS applies the if-converted and treasury-stock methods; the gap between basic and diluted measures how much of the per-share claim is contingent on instruments not yet exercised.

Academic definition

Profit or loss attributable to ordinary equity holders of the parent for a period, divided by the weighted average number of ordinary shares outstanding during that period.

Mechanism

EPS is a ratio, so it moves when either part moves. Management controls the denominator directly through buybacks and issuance, and influences the numerator through accounting estimates. This is why per-share growth and business growth can point in opposite directions, and why analysts read EPS beside the share count and the cash flow statement.

In practice

Who uses it
Equity analysts, index and screening providers, boards setting incentive targets, and retail investors reading headline results.
Why it matters
It puts profit on a per-share basis, which is the only basis on which a shareholder's claim can be compared with a share price.
Limitations
It can be raised by shrinking the share count rather than earning more, and adjusted or non-GAAP EPS is defined by the company itself.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing EPS across companies with very different share counts.
  • Using basic EPS when heavy option grants are outstanding.
  • Treating management's adjusted EPS as the audited figure.

Examples

market

A company buys back a tenth of its shares while profit is unchanged. Reported EPS rises by roughly eleven per cent and headlines call it earnings growth, although the business earned exactly what it earned the year before.

numeric

Profit after tax is 120, preferred dividends are 8, and the weighted average ordinary share count is 40. Basic EPS is 2.8. If 5 further shares would be created by in-the-money options, diluted EPS falls to about 2.49.

Given
  • Profit after tax = 120
  • Preferred dividends = 8
  • Weighted average ordinary shares = 40
  • Potential dilutive shares = 5
Steps
  1. Numerator = 120 - 8 = 112
  2. Basic EPS = 112 / 40 = 2.8
  3. Diluted share count = 40 + 5 = 45
  4. Diluted EPS = 112 / 45 = 2.489
Basic EPS = 2.8; diluted EPS = 2.49 (rounded)

The 11 per cent gap between the two is the part of each share's claim that is contingent on instruments not yet exercised.

Mathematical foundation

EPS_{basic} = \frac{NI - D_p}{WASO}, \qquad EPS_{diluted} = \frac{NI - D_p + adj}{WASO + \Delta S}

Variables

D_pDividends declared on preferred sharescurrency

Procedure

Take profit after tax attributable to the parent, subtract preferred dividends, and divide by the weighted average ordinary share count — weighting each tranche by the fraction of the period it was outstanding. For diluted EPS, add the shares dilutive instruments would create and any interest or dividend the conversion would save.

Worked example

Given
  • Profit after tax attributable to the parent = 120
  • Preferred dividends = 8
  • Weighted average ordinary shares = 40
  • Dilutive shares from in-the-money options = 5, with no interest add-back
Steps
  1. NI - D_p = 120 - 8 = 112
  2. Basic EPS = 112 / 40 = 2.8
  3. WASO + delta S = 40 + 5 = 45
  4. Diluted EPS = 112 / 45 = 2.489
Basic EPS = 2.8; diluted EPS = 2.49

Interpretation

EPS states the period's accounting profit attached to one share. It is a claim on reported earnings, not a payment: what reaches the shareholder in cash is the dividend, which is usually a fraction of EPS.

Assumptions

  • The denominator is a weighted average, not the year-end count. A buyback or issue part-way through the period changes the weight as well as the count.
  • Diluted EPS depends on the method used and on whether the instruments are in the money at the measurement date, so it is not a single mechanical number.
  • Adjusted, underlying or non-GAAP EPS is defined by the reporting company and is not the figure the auditor signed.
Limits of the formula
  • Rises when shares are repurchased even if profit is flat, so per-share growth is not evidence of operating growth.
  • Not comparable across companies with different capital structures, accounting policies or share-based pay practices.
