Simple definition

Edges wear out. As more capital trades the same idea, the prices it depended on move earlier, and the returns that were there at the start shrink.

Professional definition

The decline in a strategy's expected return as it becomes known and as capital allocated to it grows. Two mechanisms run together: dissemination, which brings competitors who act sooner, and capacity, where the strategy's own size moves the prices it is trying to capture.

Technical definition

Decay is the expected consequence of competition, not a failure of implementation, and it applies to every rule documented in public, including those in this encyclopedia. It is hard to measure: declining performance is equally consistent with a regime change, ordinary sampling variability, or an original result that was data-snooped and never real. Separating them needs prior estimates of edge and capacity plus enough post-publication data, and usually all three are missing. What is not contested is the incentive: a published edge attracts capital, and capital competes it away.

Academic definition

The attenuation of a strategy's expected excess return following dissemination and capital inflow, driven by competitive entry and by the price impact of the strategy's own growing size.

Mechanism

An edge is a price wrong by enough to pay for the trade. Acting on it pushes the price toward correctness, so harvesting an edge consumes it. Dissemination multiplies the harvesters, and capacity limits how much each can take before impact exceeds the mispricing. The equilibrium is not zero return but zero excess return net of the cost of competing.

In practice

Who uses it
Fund allocators, strategy researchers, and any trader whose rule came out of a book, a course or a public repository.
Why it matters
It sets the expectation that a documented edge is a depreciating asset, which makes continuous monitoring part of the strategy rather than an extra.
Limitations
Decay cannot be separated from regime change or from an original result that was never real, so attribution after the fact is usually impossible.

Common mistakes

  • Blaming regime change for a decline that was never an edge.
  • Scaling a strategy straight into its own capacity limit.
  • Assuming a published rule still performs as its write-up claimed.

Examples

crypto

A simple price difference between two early crypto venues paid anyone with accounts on both. As participants, routing and infrastructure improved, the same trade became a latency contest and the difference narrowed toward the cost of competing for it.

numeric

Measured gross edge per round trip falls from ten basis points to eight, five and three across four quarters while cost stays at four. Net goes six, four, one, minus one: the rule stops being tradeable before it stops being profitable gross.

Given
  • gross edge by quarter: 10, 8, 5, 3 bps per round trip
  • all-in cost held constant at 4 bps per round trip
Steps
  1. net = gross - cost
  2. Q1: 10 - 4 = 6
  3. Q2: 8 - 4 = 4
  4. Q3: 5 - 4 = 1
  5. Q4: 3 - 4 = -1
net edge reaches zero between quarters three and four

Illustrative figures. The structural point is that a fixed cost floor makes a decaying edge unusable well before it vanishes, so the retirement decision comes earlier than the gross numbers suggest.

Commonly confused with

Overfitting

An overfitted rule never had an edge, so its live failure is immediate and total. A decaying rule did have one and loses it gradually as competition and size catch up. The time profile of the decline is the main way to tell them apart.

Marked contested: the direction of the mechanism is widely accepted, but how fast published edges decay, and how much observed decay is instead regime change or an original result that was never real, is empirically unsettled. No decay formula is given because the registry supports none, and inventing a half-life would be false precision. The prediction applies to strategies documented in this encyclopedia as much as to any other. Kurdish headword is a descriptive proposal by this project.
