Custody Rule

Simple definition

Rules that force a firm holding customers' assets to keep them separate from its own, so they are still the customers' if the firm fails.

Professional definition

Requirements on an authorised custodian covering segregation of client assets from proprietary assets, accurate and reconciled records of each client's entitlement, restrictions on use or rehypothecation, independent verification, and arrangements intended to keep client property outside the firm's insolvency estate.

Technical definition

The protection is legal, not technical: segregation determines whether a customer is an owner with a proprietary claim or an unsecured creditor ranking alongside everyone else. Omnibus holding is common and compatible with segregation only if records reliably attribute entitlements. For digital assets the hard questions are key control, whether an on-chain address is legally dedicated to clients, and whether records survive disorderly failure. Scope, and whether crypto custody is a regulated activity at all, differ by jurisdiction.

Academic definition

The body of requirements governing segregation, record keeping, reconciliation and permissible use of client assets held by an authorised intermediary, directed at preserving proprietary claims on insolvency.

Mechanism

Insolvency law does not ask who paid for an asset; it asks whose property it legally is. If client holdings are commingled with the firm's own, they look like the firm's assets and the customer joins the queue of creditors. Segregation and records are what let a court separate the two after the fact, which is why the rule is about documentation and control as much as about storage.

In practice

Who uses it
Custodians and exchanges holding client balances, auditors and reconciliation teams, insolvency practitioners, and supervisors.
Why it matters
It decides whether a customer owns their balance or merely has a claim against a failed firm, which is the difference between recovery and loss.
Limitations
Whether crypto custody is regulated, and what segregation means for keys and addresses, differ by jurisdiction. A stated policy is not evidence that records would survive failure.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a platform's segregation statement as a legal conclusion about insolvency treatment.
  • Assuming an omnibus wallet is automatically a breach, or automatically compliant.
  • Confusing technical key security with legal protection of ownership.

Examples

regulatory

Two platforms hold identical balances in identical wallets. One keeps reconciled per-client records in a legally dedicated structure; the other does not. The technology is the same; the outcome on failure may not be.

numeric

A customer holds 100 on a failed platform. If segregated, the claim is proprietary and recovery can approach 100. If commingled and the estate pays unsecured creditors 40 per cent, the same customer receives about 40.

Given
  • Customer entitlement = 100 units
  • Segregated case: proprietary claim, assets identifiable
  • Commingled case: unsecured claim, estate recovery rate = 40% (illustrative)
Steps
  1. Segregated recovery = 100 x 1.00 = 100 (subject to shortfalls and costs)
  2. Commingled recovery = 100 x 0.40 = 40
  3. Difference attributable to legal characterisation = 60
100 against 40 on the same balance

The recovery rate is invented for illustration. Real outcomes depend on the insolvency law applied, the quality of records, actual shortfalls and administration costs, and segregated claims are not guaranteed to be paid in full.

JURISDICTION WARNING: rules differ by country and change. There is no single custody rule: the term covers different requirements in different regimes, and whether crypto custody is a regulated activity at all is itself jurisdiction-dependent. The EU regime is cited as one example of a safekeeping framework; no article, rule number or threshold is stated, because none could be cited from the closed source registry. Insolvency outcomes depend on the insolvency law applied, not only on the conduct rules, and segregated claims are not guaranteed to be paid in full; the 40-per-cent recovery rate in the numeric example is invented.
