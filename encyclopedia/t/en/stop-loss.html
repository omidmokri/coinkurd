Category
Risk Management
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A pre-set exit that closes a losing position once price reaches a level you chose in advance, so a single trade cannot keep costing you more.

Professional definition

An order resting away from the market that becomes an exit once a trigger price trades, capping the loss on a position. It fixes risk per unit before entry, which is what makes position size computable; the level it actually fills at is not guaranteed.

Technical definition

A conditional exit instruction whose trigger is a price event and whose execution is an order type: a stop-market guarantees that an exit is attempted, not the price obtained, while a stop-limit guarantees a price band and may therefore not fill at all. Effective risk is the distance from entry to the realised fill, widened by spread, slippage and fees, so the planned loss and the booked loss differ.

Academic definition

A contingent instruction that liquidates a position upon realisation of a predetermined adverse price threshold, bounding ex-ante risk while leaving execution price subject to prevailing market conditions.

Mechanism

A stop works by handing the exit decision to a price level instead of to the trader in the moment. Its weakness is the same mechanism: the trigger is a condition on price, so in a gap or a thin book the exit fills wherever liquidity happens to be, which can be far past the level. The protection is on the decision, not on the price.

In practice

Who uses it
Discretionary and systematic traders, execution desks, and anyone sizing a position from a defined risk per unit.
Why it matters
It fixes the loss side of a trade before capital is committed, which is the input that every sizing and reward-to-risk calculation needs.
Limitations
It bounds the decision, not the fill. Gaps, trading halts, thin books and venue outages all mean the realised loss can exceed the planned one.

Common mistakes

  • Placing the stop on a round number where resting liquidity clusters.
  • Widening the stop after entry so the planned loss no longer applies.
  • Choosing the size first and then fitting a stop to it.

Examples

market

A long is opened at 100 with a stop at 96. Weekend news gaps the market open to 92; the stop triggers and fills near 92, not 96. The decision was honoured, the price was not.

numeric

Risk per unit is 100 minus 96, so 4. A trader risking 200 can hold 50 units. If round-trip fees and slippage add 0.40 per unit, effective risk is 4.40 and only 45 units keep the loss inside 200.

Given
  • entry E = 100 (account currency per unit)
  • stop S = 96
  • risk budget = 200
  • round-trip fees plus slippage = 0.40 per unit
Steps
  1. planned risk per unit = 100 - 96 = 4
  2. units at planned risk = 200 / 4 = 50
  3. effective risk per unit = 4 + 0.40 = 4.40
  4. units at effective risk = 200 / 4.40 = 45.45, rounded down to 45
45 units, worst planned loss 45 x 4.40 = 198

This assumes the stop fills at 96. A gap through the level breaks the 4.40 figure entirely, which is why the planned loss is a floor on optimism, not a guarantee.

Procedure

Take the absolute distance between entry and stop in price units, then multiply by the position size. For a long the stop sits below entry, for a short above it, so the sign convention differs but the magnitude is the same quantity: risk per unit times units.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 50 units
  • E = 100
  • S = 96
Steps
  1. E - S = 100 - 96 = 4
  2. L = 50 x 4
L_planned = 200 in account currency

Interpretation

The output is the loss the trade is designed to produce if it is wrong and the stop behaves. It is a planning figure, not a worst case: the worst case is unbounded by gaps and outages.

Assumptions

  • The stop fills exactly at S. In practice it fills at the first available price after the trigger.
  • Fees, funding and currency conversion are excluded; add them separately as cost per unit.
  • A linear instrument quoted in the account currency, with no partial fills.
Limits of the formula
  • Gaps, limit moves and halts can carry the fill well past S, so realised loss exceeds the formula.
  • A stop-limit may not fill at all, in which case the position and its loss stay open.

Commonly confused with

Liquidation

A stop is your own order, set where you choose. Liquidation is the venue closing your position when margin fails, at a level the venue's rules decide.
