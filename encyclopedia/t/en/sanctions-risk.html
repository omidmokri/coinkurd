Simple definition

The risk that a transaction, counterparty or asset turns out to be restricted under someone's sanctions rules, leaving funds frozen, accounts closed or penalties due.

Professional definition

Exposure to legal and financial consequences arising from restrictive measures imposed by states or international bodies on named persons, entities, sectors or jurisdictions. It affects institutions directly, through enforcement, and indirectly, through the de-risking decisions of their banks and service providers.

Technical definition

Sanctions regimes are jurisdiction-specific, overlapping and frequently amended, and several apply simultaneously to a cross-border transaction. Screening obligations usually attach to the institution rather than the customer, which is why compliance programmes screen names, addresses and ownership chains. For transferable digital assets, published designation of addresses and the traceability of chain history mean exposure can persist in an asset after it changes hands.

Academic definition

The compliance and financial risk created by restrictive measures of extraterritorial reach, whose incidence depends on jurisdictional nexus, designation scope and the screening duties imposed on intermediaries.

Mechanism

Enforcement reaches institutions through the jurisdictional links of a transaction: the currency used, the correspondent bank, the location of a server or counterparty. Banks therefore price legal uncertainty as cost and often withdraw from an entire category of clients rather than assess each one, which is why the practical effect is felt as lost access rather than as a penalty.

In practice

Who uses it
Compliance and legal teams at banks, exchanges and payment firms; cross-border investors; and custodians assessing the provenance of assets.
Why it matters
It can block settlement or freeze holdings regardless of market value, so it is a precondition to transacting rather than one risk among several.
Limitations
Lists, thresholds and extraterritorial reach differ by jurisdiction and change frequently; general descriptions cannot substitute for current legal advice.

Common mistakes

  • Screening only the direct counterparty and not the ownership chain.
  • Assuming one jurisdiction's list is the only one that applies.
  • Treating a one-off check as an ongoing control.

Examples

market

A payment is routed through a correspondent bank in a third country. That bank's own obligations, not the sender's or receiver's, determine whether the transfer settles or is held for review.

crypto

An exchange screens deposits against published designated addresses and the history of the funds. A transfer with a designated address in its chain of custody can be held even if the depositor has no connection to it.

Commonly confused with

Know Your Customer and Anti-Money Laundering

Anti-money-laundering rules ask who a customer is and where funds came from. Sanctions rules ask whether dealing with that person or asset is permitted at all. An institution can satisfy one and breach the other.
