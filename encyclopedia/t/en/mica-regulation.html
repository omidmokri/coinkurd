Simple definition

The European Union's single rulebook for crypto-assets: it sets out who may issue them, who may offer services in them, and what must be disclosed.

Professional definition

Regulation (EU) 2023/1114, establishing a harmonised European framework covering issuance and offering of crypto-assets, authorisation and conduct of crypto-asset service providers, specific regimes for asset-referenced and e-money tokens, disclosure obligations and market-abuse provisions for in-scope assets.

Technical definition

Its significance is structural: it replaces a patchwork of member-state regimes with one framework, so authorisation in one member state can be used across the Union rather than being sought separately in each. Scope is defined by asset and activity, and assets already covered by other European financial legislation are addressed by that legislation instead. Supervision is shared between national authorities and the European bodies, with technical standards and guidance continuing to develop after the core text.

Academic definition

The European Union's harmonising regulation for crypto-asset markets, allocating authorisation, conduct, disclosure and market-integrity obligations across issuers and service providers within a single-market framework.

Mechanism

A single market with twenty-seven supervisors produces either duplicated applications or the weakest regime setting the standard. Harmonisation answers both: one framework, one authorisation usable across borders, and one supervisory language. The cost is that a single text must cover very different assets, which is why it carves out separate regimes for the token types that behave like payment instruments.

In practice

Who uses it
Firms serving European customers, issuers preparing disclosure documents, national supervisors and the European authorities, and European retail users.
Why it matters
It decides who may lawfully serve European customers, what must be published before an offer, and which conduct and custody duties apply.
Limitations
It binds in the European Union and not elsewhere, and it does not make any crypto-asset safe. Technical standards and guidance continue to develop, so the detail dates quickly.

Common mistakes

  • Reading authorisation under the regime as approval of a product or its returns.
  • Assuming it applies to firms and users outside the European Union.
  • Treating a published white paper as a guarantee rather than a liability-bearing disclosure.

Examples

regulatory

A firm authorised in one member state can serve customers in others under the single framework, instead of applying separately in each. The same firm serving customers outside the Union is governed by those countries' own rules.

numeric

A firm targeting ten European markets faces one authorisation process under the single framework rather than ten. The reduction is in applications, not in substantive obligation: the same conduct rules then apply in all ten.

Given
  • Target member-state markets = 10
  • Harmonised framework: one authorisation usable across member states
  • Pre-harmonisation counterfactual: one permission per market
Steps
  1. Applications before harmonisation = 10
  2. Applications under the single framework = 1
  3. Reduction = 9 applications
One authorisation in place of ten, with obligations unchanged in all ten

A structural illustration with an invented market count. No procedure, fee, timetable or document requirement is stated; those are set by the regulation and its technical standards, which continue to develop.
