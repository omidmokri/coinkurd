Expectancy

Simple definition

The average result you should expect from one trade of a strategy, worked out from how often it wins and how big its wins and losses are.

Professional definition

The mean outcome per trade of a rule set: win rate times average win, minus loss rate times average loss. Expressed in account currency or in multiples of the risked amount. A positive figure is the minimum condition for a strategy to be worth running; it says nothing about the path.

Technical definition

The sample mean of the per-trade profit and loss distribution, decomposed into frequency and magnitude. Because it is a mean it is dominated by the tails of the magnitude distribution: one outlier can carry the whole figure, so expectancy should always be reported with the trade count and a dispersion measure. Costs, slippage and funding must be inside the average win and average loss or the number is not the one you can trade.

Academic definition

The first moment of the empirical per-trade return distribution of a trading rule, decomposed into outcome frequencies and conditional mean magnitudes.

Mechanism

Frequency and magnitude trade off against each other, and expectancy is the only place they meet. A rule that wins three times in ten can carry a positive mean if its winners are large enough relative to its losers, and a rule that wins eight times in ten can still lose money. This is why win rate quoted on its own tells you nothing about profitability.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone evaluating a rule set from its own record: discretionary traders with a trade journal, systematic developers reading backtest output, and risk reviewers.
Why it matters
It is the single number that decides whether a method has an edge at all, and it is the input position sizing formulas need.
Limitations
It is an estimate from a finite sample, highly sensitive to outliers, and stable only while the market regime that produced the sample persists.

Common mistakes

  • Computing it before fees, slippage and funding.
  • Quoting it from twenty trades as if it were settled.
  • Treating a positive figure as a guarantee of no long losing run.

Examples

numeric

A rule wins 40 percent of the time. Winners average 2.5 times the risked amount, losers average one times it. Expectancy is 0.4 units of risk per trade, so a strategy that is wrong six times in ten still carries a positive mean.

Given
  • win rate = 0.40, loss rate = 0.60
  • average win = 2.5R
  • average loss = 1.0R
  • R = the amount risked per trade
Steps
  1. 0.40 x 2.5 = 1.00
  2. 0.60 x 1.0 = 0.60
  3. E = 1.00 - 0.60
E = 0.40R per trade

Over 100 trades the expected total is 40R, but the realised total will differ: the standard deviation of the sum grows with the trade count.

market

Two desks report an identical 55 percent win rate. The first averages 1.0 won against 1.2 lost and bleeds; the second averages 1.3 won against 0.9 lost and compounds. The win rate was never the variable that mattered.

Mathematical foundation

E = (W \times \overline{G}) - (L \times \overline{P})

Variables

EExpectancy: mean result per tradeaccount currency or R
WWin rate: winning trades divided by all tradesfraction, 0 to 1
\overline{G}Average gain on winning trades, after costsaccount currency or R
LLoss rate, equal to 1 - W when there are no scratch tradesfraction, 0 to 1
\overline{P}Average loss on losing trades, after costs, as a positive magnitudeaccount currency or R

Procedure

Take a closed set of trades from one unchanged rule set. Count winners and losers and divide by the total for the two rates. Average the winning results and, separately, the losing results, with fees, slippage and funding already deducted. Multiply each rate by its average and subtract. Report the trade count beside the result.

Worked example

Given
  • 120 trades
  • 48 winners, so W = 48 / 120 = 0.40 and L = 0.60
  • average win = 500 units of account currency
  • average loss = 200 units of account currency
Steps
  1. 0.40 x 500 = 200
  2. 0.60 x 200 = 120
  3. E = 200 - 120
E = 80 units of account currency per trade, which is 9,600 over the 120 trades.

Interpretation

A positive expectancy means the mean of the recorded distribution was above zero. It is a property of a sample, not a promise about the next trade or the next hundred, and it says nothing about how deep the drawdown on the way will be.

Assumptions

  • All trades come from one unchanged rule set. Mixing rule versions, instruments or position sizes makes the mean meaningless.
  • Losses are entered as positive magnitudes and all costs are already inside the two averages.
  • Scratch trades at exactly zero must be assigned to a category or counted separately, otherwise the two rates do not sum to one.
  • The sample is treated as representative of the process. That assumption fails whenever volatility or liquidity regime changes.
Limits of the formula
  • A mean is not robust: a single very large winner can turn a negative figure positive, which is why trade count and dispersion must be reported with it.
  • Expectancy in R units is only comparable between strategies when R is defined identically, normally as the initial risk per trade.
  • A positive expectancy is compatible with ruin if position size is too large relative to the dispersion of outcomes.

Commonly confused with

Expected Value

Expected value is the general probability-weighted mean of any random outcome. Expectancy is that mean applied to one trading rule's own record, estimated from realised trades rather than from assumed probabilities.

Commonly confused with

Win Rate

Win rate is only the frequency term. Expectancy multiplies that frequency by the size of the outcomes, which is why a high win rate and a negative expectancy coexist comfortably.
