Sortino Ratio

Simple definition

A risk-adjusted return figure that counts only the bad kind of volatility. Upside swings do not penalise the score; shortfalls below a chosen target do.

Professional definition

Excess return over a minimum acceptable return, divided by downside deviation — the root mean square of shortfalls below that same target. It is the Sharpe ratio with the denominator restricted to the outcomes an investor actually objects to.

Technical definition

Numerator is the mean return minus the minimum acceptable return. Denominator is the square root of the mean of squared negative deviations from the same target, with every non-shortfall entering as zero. Two conventions compete over the divisor, the full observation count or only the count of shortfalls, and they give materially different numbers. Because both the target and the divisor are choices, a Sortino ratio is comparable only against another computed the same way.

Academic definition

A reward-to-downside-risk measure expressing excess return over a target rate per unit of lower partial moment of order two, evaluated at that same target.

Mechanism

Standard deviation treats a large gain and a large loss as equally undesirable, which misprices any strategy whose distribution is asymmetric. Replacing it with the second lower partial moment measures only the side that hurts, so a positively skewed return stream stops being punished for its best months. The price of that fix is a target parameter that the analyst must choose.

In practice

Who uses it
Allocators screening funds with skewed return profiles, strategy developers comparing asymmetric systems, and risk teams reporting beside the Sharpe ratio.
Why it matters
It separates a strategy that is volatile upwards from one that is volatile downwards, a distinction the Sharpe ratio cannot make.
Limitations
With few shortfalls the denominator is estimated from a handful of observations, so the ratio is unstable and can look spectacular on a short sample.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing two Sortino ratios computed with different targets.
  • Not stating whether the divisor counts all periods or only shortfalls.
  • Reading a very high value as skill rather than as too few losing periods.

Examples

numeric

Six monthly returns +3, -2, +4, -5, +1, +2 percent. With a target of zero the ratio is 0.227. With a target of 0.5%, the mean exactly equals the target and the ratio collapses to zero on identical data.

Given
  • monthly returns = [3, -2, 4, -5, 1, 2] in %
  • mean return = 0.5% per month
  • divisor convention: all n = 6 observations
Steps
  1. MAR = 0: shortfalls are -2 and -5, so DD = sqrt((4 + 25)/6) = sqrt(4.8333) = 2.1985%
  2. Sortino = (0.5 - 0) / 2.1985 = 0.2274
  3. MAR = 0.5%: shortfalls are -2.5 and -5.5, so DD = sqrt((6.25 + 30.25)/6) = sqrt(6.0833) = 2.4664%
  4. Sortino = (0.5 - 0.5) / 2.4664 = 0.000
0.227 at a zero target, 0.000 at a 0.5% target

Same returns, two defensible targets, two incompatible verdicts. With the alternative divisor of two shortfalls instead of six periods, the zero-target ratio becomes 0.131.

market

A short-volatility strategy delivers many small gains and rare large losses. Its Sharpe ratio looks respectable because the small gains are also smooth; its Sortino ratio drops sharply once the rare losses enter the denominator alone.

Mathematical foundation

S = \frac{\bar{R} - \mathrm{MAR}}{\mathrm{DD}}, \qquad \mathrm{DD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[\min\!\left(R_t - \mathrm{MAR},\, 0\right)\right]^2}

Variables

\bar{R}Mean periodic return of the portfolio% per period
\mathrm{MAR}Minimum acceptable return, the target the investor sets; often zero or the risk-free rate% per period
\mathrm{DD}Downside deviation: root mean square shortfall below the MAR% per period
R_tReturn in period t% per period
nNumber of periods in the samplecount

Procedure

Fix and disclose the minimum acceptable return before looking at the data. Subtract it from every period return. Replace each positive difference with zero, square what is left, average the squares over the chosen divisor and take the square root. Divide the mean excess return by that figure. Annualise numerator and denominator consistently or not at all.

Worked example

Given
  • R = [3, -2, 4, -5, 1, 2] percent monthly
  • n = 6
  • MAR = 0, then MAR = 0.5%
Steps
  1. mean R = (3 - 2 + 4 - 5 + 1 + 2) / 6 = 0.5%
  2. MAR = 0: squared shortfalls = 4 and 25; DD = sqrt(29/6) = 2.1985%
  3. S = 0.5 / 2.1985 = 0.2274
  4. MAR = 0.5: squared shortfalls = 6.25 and 30.25; DD = sqrt(36.5/6) = 2.4664%
  5. S = 0 / 2.4664 = 0.000
S = 0.227 with MAR 0; S = 0.000 with MAR 0.5%

Interpretation

The figure is units of excess return per unit of shortfall risk. Higher is better only within one convention: the target, the divisor and the periodicity must match before two numbers can be compared at all.

Assumptions

  • The minimum acceptable return is a free parameter; zero, the risk-free rate and an explicit liability target are all in use and give different answers.
  • The divisor is contested: dividing by all n periods and dividing by the number of shortfalls are both published practice, and the worked example shows both.
  • Annualisation of downside deviation by the square root of the period count assumes serially independent returns.
Limits of the formula
  • Sensitive to very few shortfalls: with one or two losing periods the denominator is almost unmeasured.
  • Says nothing about the size of the worst single loss or the length of a drawdown.
  • Like every ratio computed from a sample, it is inflated by selection when the best of many tested strategies is reported.

Commonly confused with

Sharpe Ratio

Sharpe divides by total standard deviation and penalises upside volatility too. Sortino divides only by shortfall risk below a stated target, so the two rank asymmetric strategies differently.
