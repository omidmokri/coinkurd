Sanctions Screening

Simple definition

Checking a customer, a counterparty or an address against official lists of people and entities that a firm is forbidden to deal with.

Professional definition

A control that matches names, identifiers and, for digital assets, addresses against sanctions lists issued by the authorities whose law applies to the firm, at onboarding and continuously thereafter, blocking or freezing on a confirmed match and escalating on a possible one.

Technical definition

Distinct from risk-based diligence because sanctions obligations are typically absolute: in many jurisdictions a prohibited dealing is a breach regardless of intent or knowledge. Matching is fuzzy by necessity — transliteration, name order and partial identifiers generate false positives — so screening systems trade recall against alert volume. For on-chain activity the obligation extends to addresses and, in some regimes, to indirect exposure through intermediate hops, which no list can enumerate completely.

Academic definition

The control process by which obliged entities compare customers, counterparties and transaction identifiers against authoritative restricted-party lists, with strict-liability consequences for prohibited dealings in many legal systems.

Mechanism

A list is a legal instrument, not a database key. The same person appears under several transliterations, an entity hides behind ownership layers, and an address is reused by thousands of users of one service. So screening cannot be exact matching; it must be probabilistic matching with a human adjudication step, and the threshold a firm sets decides whether it fails by over-blocking customers or by missing a designated party.

In practice

Who uses it
Compliance operations, payment and withdrawal review teams, custodians, and any firm with customers or counterparties abroad.
Why it matters
A single prohibited dealing can trigger penalties that dwarf the economics of the transaction, so the control is run before value moves, not after.
Limitations
Lists differ between authorities and are updated constantly; matching is fuzzy; indirect on-chain exposure cannot be fully enumerated. Which lists apply depends on the firm's own jurisdictional connections.

Common mistakes

  • Screening once at onboarding and never rescreening as lists are updated.
  • Assuming one country's list is the only list that applies to the firm.
  • Reading a possible name match as a confirmed designation.

Examples

regulatory

A withdrawal is held because the destination address previously received value, two hops away, from an address a list names. The customer is not designated; the exposure is indirect, and the firm must still decide.

numeric

Screening 50,000 names at a loose threshold flags 2 per cent, or 1,000 cases, of which perhaps two are true. Tightening the threshold cuts the queue to 200 but risks letting a transliterated name through.

Given
  • 50,000 names screened
  • Loose threshold flags 2% of names
  • Tight threshold flags 0.4% of names
Steps
  1. Loose alerts = 50,000 x 0.02 = 1,000
  2. Tight alerts = 50,000 x 0.004 = 200
  3. Review burden falls by 800 cases
1,000 alerts loose against 200 tight, for the same list

Rates are illustrative, not measured. The trade-off is real and unavoidable: the threshold that reduces review volume also reduces recall, and the firm bears the legal consequence of a miss.

Commonly confused with

Anti-Money Laundering

Anti-money laundering is risk-based: more risk, more diligence. Sanctions are typically absolute: a prohibited party is prohibited at any size.
