Simple definition

Quoting both a buy price and a sell price continuously, and earning the gap between them by trading with whoever comes. The maker supplies the liquidity others take.

Professional definition

Continuously posting two-sided quotes and earning the spread, while managing the inventory the fills leave behind. Profit comes from many small round trips, and the main costs are adverse selection and the price risk of unbalanced inventory.

Technical definition

A liquidity-provision strategy whose profit is the spread captured minus losses to informed flow and the cost of carrying inventory. Quotes are skewed away from the side the maker is already long and widened when the probability of trading against information rises. Because the maker is systematically on the wrong side at the moment of an informed trade, the spread must be wide enough to pay for those fills, which is why liquidity thins exactly when volatility spikes.

Academic definition

The continuous provision of two-sided quotes in return for the bid-ask spread, with profitability determined by the balance between spread revenue, adverse selection costs and inventory holding risk.

Mechanism

A maker is a dealer in immediacy. Every fill hands them inventory they did not want, so they quote asymmetrically to attract the offsetting trade and widen when being picked off becomes more likely. Two risks dominate: adverse selection, where the counterparty knows something, and inventory risk, where the position moves before it can be offset.

In practice

Who uses it
Exchange-designated makers, proprietary high-frequency firms, crypto desks earning maker rebates, and liquidity providers in automated pools in a different form.
Why it matters
It earns a spread from order flow rather than from direction, and venues subsidise it with fee rebates because a tight book attracts volume.
Limitations
Thin margins demand low fees, fast cancellation and tight risk limits. A maker without a speed or information edge is the one being selected against, and a gap through the quotes can erase months of spread capture.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a fixed spread through a volatility spike.
  • Letting inventory build on one side instead of skewing quotes.
  • Counting spread revenue without the losses to informed flow.

Examples

market

A maker quotes 100.00 bid and 100.10 offer, earning 0.10 on each completed round trip. News arrives and its bid is filled repeatedly as price falls to 99.00: it has bought a large inventory from traders who knew more, and one such episode can cost many hundreds of round trips.

numeric

Five hundred round trips at a 0.10 spread earn 50. One adverse episode fills 200 units on the bid before price drops 1.00, costing 200. The net is a loss of 150, even though the spread was captured on every single round trip.

Given
  • bid 100.00, offer 100.10, so the spread is 0.10 (10 basis points)
  • 500 completed round trips of 1 unit each
  • one adverse episode: 200 units bought on the bid, then price falls 1.00
  • no hedge, no fee rebate
Steps
  1. spread revenue = 500 x 0.10 = 50
  2. adverse selection loss = 200 x 1.00 = 200
  3. net = 50 - 200 = -150
a loss of 150 despite capturing the spread on all 500 round trips

Simplified: it ignores fee rebates, partial fills and any hedge. The point is the shape of the payoff -- small frequent gains against rare large losses -- not these particular figures.

Commonly confused with

Automated Market Maker

An automated market maker prices from a fixed formula over a pool's reserves, with no quotes to pull. A human or algorithmic market maker chooses, skews and cancels its quotes continuously.
