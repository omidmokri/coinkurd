Smart Order Routing

Simple definition

Choosing which exchange each piece of an order is sent to, so it fills at the best price after fees rather than just the best price on the screen you happen to be watching.

Professional definition

A layer that scans quotes, depth, fee schedules and expected fill probability across venues and dispatches child orders accordingly. In fragmented markets, and in crypto where one pair trades on many unconnected exchanges, the best headline quote and the best net price are often on different venues.

Technical definition

Routing minimises net cost: the quoted price adjusted for taker fee or maker rebate, the expected impact against that venue's depth, the latency to reach it, and the probability the quote survives until arrival. Scheduling and routing are separate problems — when and how much versus where — and a router adds its own failure modes: stale quotes, inventory stranded on a thin venue, and transfer or settlement friction between accounts that the quote comparison never sees.

Academic definition

The algorithmic allocation of child orders across competing execution venues so as to minimise net transaction cost, conditional on posted liquidity, fee schedules, latency and fill-probability estimates.

Mechanism

Fragmentation creates the opportunity and the problem at once. Depth sits on several books, so no venue can fill size cheaply; prices on those books are linked only by arbitrageurs, so they differ until someone acts. A router harvests that difference while racing other routers, which is why latency and fee tiers end up inside what looks like a pricing decision.

In practice

Who uses it
Brokers with best-execution obligations, prime and OTC desks in crypto, market makers hedging across venues, and funds running accounts on several exchanges.
Why it matters
In a fragmented market the cheapest fill is usually spread across books; routing is what turns several shallow books into one usable one.
Limitations
Net price depends on fee tier, account balances and withdrawal paths, so a router that compares only quoted prices can route to the more expensive venue.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing gross quotes and ignoring taker fees and rebates.
  • Routing size to a venue whose quote has no depth behind it.
  • Ignoring that funds must already sit on the venue chosen.

Examples

crypto

The same pair quotes one basis point tighter on a venue charging ten basis points taker than on a venue charging two. For a taker order the wider quote is cheaper, and a router that reads only the book sends the order to the wrong place.

numeric

Venue A quotes 100.00 with a 0.10 percent taker fee; venue B quotes 100.05 with 0.02 percent. Net cost is 100.10 against 100.07, so the worse-looking quote wins by three basis points.

Given
  • buy order, fully filled at the quote on one venue
  • venue A: price 100.00, taker fee 0.10%
  • venue B: price 100.05, taker fee 0.02%
  • sufficient depth assumed on both
Steps
  1. A: 100.00 x (1 + 0.0010) = 100.10
  2. B: 100.05 x (1 + 0.0002) = 100.0700...
  3. difference = 100.10 - 100.07 = 0.03, i.e. about 3 bps
venue B is cheaper net, despite the worse quoted price

The comparison assumes both quotes hold for the full size. Once depth is finite the ranking can reverse, which is why routers price against the book and not against the top level.

Mathematical foundation

P^{\,buy}_{eff} = P_{venue}\,(1 + f),\qquad P^{\,sell}_{eff} = P_{venue}\,(1 - f)

Variables

P_{venue}Executable price on the venue for the intended sizequote currency
fFee rate for the order's role on that venue; negative for a maker rebatefraction
P_{eff}Effective net price used for ranking venuesquote currency

Procedure

For each venue, price the intended quantity against that venue's book rather than its top level, add the applicable fee for the role the order will take, and rank by effective net price. Then check feasibility: are funds already there, is the quote fresh enough given latency, and would the fill strand inventory that must later be moved?

Worked example

Given
  • buy
  • A: 100.00 at f = 0.0010
  • B: 100.05 at f = 0.0002
Steps
  1. A: 100.00 x 1.0010 = 100.10
  2. B: 100.05 x 1.0002 = 100.0700 (to 4 dp)
  3. rank by lower effective price
route to B; saving about 3 bps

Interpretation

The effective price is what the account is actually debited per unit. Ranking on it rather than on the quote is the minimum standard for a routing decision.

Assumptions

  • Fee tiers are account-specific and change with volume, so the same comparison can rank venues differently for two traders at the same instant.
  • The formula prices only fee and quote. Funding, transfer, withdrawal and settlement costs are real and sit outside it.
  • The quoted price is assumed available for the full size; with finite depth the venue's book must be walked instead.
Limits of the formula
  • It is a static snapshot. Quotes can be gone by the time an order arrives, which is why fill probability and latency belong in a production router.
  • In crypto, net price comparison across venues assumes the asset is fungible and movable between them, which bridges, withdrawal halts and listing differences can break.

Commonly confused with

Arbitrage

Arbitrage is a strategy that exists to capture a price difference between venues. Routing is an execution service that takes a trade you already decided on and finds the cheapest place to do it.
