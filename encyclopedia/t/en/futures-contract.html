Futures Contract

Simple definition

A binding agreement to buy or sell a set quantity of something at a fixed price on a set future date, traded on an exchange rather than arranged privately.

Professional definition

An exchange-traded, standardised contract specifying quantity, quality, delivery date and settlement method, cleared through a central counterparty. Both sides post margin, and gains and losses are settled in cash daily rather than only at expiry.

Technical definition

A standardised forward obligation with exchange-set specifications, novated to a clearing house that becomes counterparty to each side. Daily mark-to-market variation margin removes most of the credit exposure of a bilateral forward, which is why a futures price and a forward price on the same underlying can differ when interest rates are stochastic. Settlement is physical delivery or cash against a published reference.

Academic definition

A standardised, centrally cleared contract conferring the obligation to exchange a specified underlying at a predetermined price on a stated delivery date, with daily variation margin settling intermediate gains and losses.

Mechanism

Standardisation and central clearing are what make the contract fungible: because every contract is identical and the clearing house stands between the parties, a position can be closed by taking the opposite trade with anyone. Daily margin calls, not trust in the counterparty, keep the system solvent.

In practice

Who uses it
Producers and consumers hedging price risk, asset managers adjusting exposure cheaply, and speculators who want leverage and a liquid two-sided market.
Why it matters
A futures position changes exposure without moving the underlying asset, and the clearing house removes the need to assess each counterparty's credit.
Limitations
Contracts expire, so a long-term view must be rolled, and each roll pays or receives the basis. Margin can be called at the worst moment.

Common mistakes

  • Treating contract size as the amount at risk instead of margin and leverage.
  • Forgetting that the position expires and must be rolled.
  • Assuming the futures price is a forecast of the spot price at expiry.

Examples

market

A mining company sells futures against next quarter's output. If the price falls, the loss on the physical sale is offset by the gain on the short futures position. The hedge fixes revenue, and in exchange gives up the upside.

numeric

A contract covers 5 units and the futures price is 2,000, so the notional is 10,000. Initial margin of 800 is 8% of notional, about 12.5 times leverage: a 1% move in the futures price changes the margin balance by 12.5%.

Given
  • contract size = 5 units
  • futures price = 2,000 per unit
  • initial margin = 800 per contract
Steps
  1. notional = 5 x 2,000 = 10,000
  2. margin / notional = 800 / 10,000 = 8%
  3. leverage = 10,000 / 800 = 12.5x
  4. 1% of notional = 100, which is 12.5% of the 800 posted
notional 10,000; leverage 12.5x; a 1% adverse move consumes 12.5% of initial margin

Contract specifications and margin rates are set by the exchange and change over time. These are illustrative figures, not a live quote.

Mathematical foundation

F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - q) T}

Variables

F_0Futures price agreed now for delivery at Tprice units
S_0Spot price of the underlying nowprice units
rRisk-free interest rate, continuously compoundedper year
qContinuous income or convenience yield on the underlying, net of storage costper year
TTime to deliveryyears

Procedure

Take the spot price and compound it forward at the net cost of carry: the financing rate you pay to hold the asset, less any income or convenience yield it produces. The result is the futures price that leaves no riskless profit available to someone who buys spot, funds it and sells the future.

Worked example

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • r = 5% per year, continuously compounded
  • q = 0
  • T = 0.5 years
Steps
  1. (r - q) T = 0.05 x 0.5 = 0.025
  2. e^0.025 = 1.02532
  3. F_0 = 100 x 1.02532
F_0 = 102.53

Interpretation

The futures price is the spot price carried forward at the cost of financing minus the yield on holding the asset. It is a no-arbitrage boundary, not an opinion about where spot will be at delivery.

Assumptions

  • Continuous compounding and a known, constant interest rate. With stochastic rates futures and forward prices are no longer equal, because daily margin cash flows are reinvested at a rate correlated with the price.
  • No transaction costs, no cost of funding margin and unrestricted short selling of the underlying.
  • The underlying can be stored and delivered. For commodities a convenience yield enters q and the relation becomes a band rather than an identity.
  • Cash-settled crypto futures have no storage or delivery, so the carry term is driven by financing cost and demand for leverage rather than physical carry.
Limits of the formula
  • It is a no-arbitrage boundary, not a forecast of the spot price at delivery.
  • Where the underlying cannot be borrowed or shorted, only one side of the arbitrage is executable and the market price can sit away from the theoretical level for long periods.

Commonly confused with

Perpetual Contract

A futures contract has a delivery date and converges to spot at it. A perpetual contract never expires and is pulled toward spot by funding payments instead.
