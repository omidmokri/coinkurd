Market Maker versus Taker

Simple definition

Two opposite businesses. The market maker earns the spread for standing ready to trade; the taker pays the spread in order to trade right now.

Professional definition

A contrast of economic roles rather than of order flags. The maker's profit is many small spreads minus losses to informed flow and inventory risk; the taker's profit must come from the view, because for them spread, fees and impact are all costs. One account can be either, on different fills.

Technical definition

The maker's expected revenue per round trip is the captured spread less the adverse-selection loss and the cost of carrying unbalanced inventory. The taker's expected cost is roughly half the spread plus market impact plus the taker fee. The two risk profiles are mirror images: many small gains against occasional large losses for the maker, and the reverse for the taker. A strategy that cannot say which side it is on usually pays both costs at once.

Academic definition

A dichotomy between liquidity supply, remunerated by the spread and exposed to adverse selection, and liquidity demand, which pays for immediacy in order to act on information or on a view.

Mechanism

Immediacy is what is actually being traded. The taker buys it, the maker sells it, and the spread plus the fee asymmetry is its price. Because the maker is systematically on the wrong side at the moment an informed order arrives, the price of immediacy must rise with the probability that the next order knows something.

In practice

Who uses it
Desks choosing a business model, strategy designers, and any trader deciding whether to post a quote or cross the spread.
Why it matters
It decides where the edge must come from: a maker needs volume, queue position and fee tiers; a taker needs a forecast good enough to pay for immediacy.
Limitations
Market making needs infrastructure, inventory capacity and fee tiers most individuals do not have. The role is not a style that can simply be chosen.

Common mistakes

  • Calling passive limit orders market making without inventory management or two-sided quoting.
  • Assuming maker rebates will rescue a weak strategy.
  • Ignoring that a taker's forecast must beat spread, fees and impact together.

Examples

market

Two accounts trade the same instrument all day. One posts both sides, ends flat and keeps a thin margin per round trip. The other crosses the spread on signals; its results depend entirely on whether the signals were right.

numeric

On a two-tick spread with a one-tick capture, a maker needs its adverse-selection loss per fill to stay under one tick. If a tenth of fills lose five ticks, the loss is half a tick and the edge halves.

Given
  • spread 2 ticks, maker captures 1 tick per completed round trip
  • 10% of fills are adversely selected and lose 5 ticks each
  • fees and inventory carry excluded
Steps
  1. gross capture per fill = 1 tick
  2. expected adverse-selection loss = 0.10 x 5 = 0.5 tick
  3. net expected capture = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5 tick
net half a tick per fill, so a 20% adverse-selection rate at the same severity would erase the edge entirely

Illustrative arithmetic on assumed parameters, not a measurement. Real adverse-selection rates must be estimated from the desk's own fills.
