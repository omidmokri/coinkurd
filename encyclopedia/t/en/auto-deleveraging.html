Auto-Deleveraging

Simple definition

When a venue cannot cover a bankrupt trader's losses, it closes other traders' winning positions to balance the books. You can be right and still be taken out.

Professional definition

A venue mechanism that force-closes profitable positions on the opposite side of a bankrupt one when the insurance fund cannot absorb the shortfall. Counterparties are selected from a ranked queue, closed at the bankrupt position's bankruptcy price, and are not compensated for the gains they are removed from.

Technical definition

The last layer of a derivatives venue's loss waterfall. A liquidation executed worse than the bankruptcy price leaves a deficit; the insurance fund is drawn first, and any residual is passed to the system by reducing opposing positions. Ranking is typically a function of unrealised profit and effective leverage, but the formula, the trigger and even the existence of the mechanism are venue rules rather than market properties.

Academic definition

A clearing-of-last-resort procedure in which a trading venue extinguishes profitable counterparty exposures to restore the solvency identity between winners and losers after a default exceeds mutualised resources.

Mechanism

On a venue where every position is matched by an opposite one, a loss larger than the loser's collateral has nowhere to come from. The insurance fund absorbs that gap, and when it cannot, the only remaining source is the winners' unrealised gains. Auto-deleveraging makes that transfer explicit instead of letting accounts go negative, and it appears in the fast, gapping moves that created the deficit.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone holding a leveraged derivatives position on a venue that operates this mechanism, and risk teams measuring venue risk rather than market risk.
Why it matters
It is a real path by which a correct, profitable position is closed without the holder's consent, so it belongs in the risk budget alongside liquidation.
Limitations
It cannot be hedged on the venue that imposes it, and the queue position is a venue computation that the trader can influence only by holding less leverage and less size.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a profitable position is safe from forced closure.
  • Ignoring a venue's ADL ranking indicator while sitting at high leverage.
  • Treating the insurance fund balance as a permanent guarantee.

Examples

market

A violent move liquidates a large leveraged short. The liquidation fills far above the price at which its margin was exhausted, the deficit exceeds the insurance fund, and the venue closes the highest-ranked profitable longs at the bankruptcy price — ending their trades for them.

numeric

A bankrupt position of 100 units has a bankruptcy price of 20,000 but is closed at 19,500: a deficit of 50,000. With 30,000 available in the insurance fund, 20,000 must still be found, and auto-deleveraging takes it from ranked profitable counterparties.

Given
  • position size Q = 100 units
  • bankruptcy price P_bk = 20,000
  • actual close price P_close = 19,500
  • insurance fund available F = 30,000
Steps
  1. shortfall = 100 x |20,000 - 19,500| = 50,000
  2. covered by the fund = 30,000
  3. residual = 50,000 - 30,000 = 20,000
20,000 must be recovered by auto-deleveraging profitable counterparties

Illustrative figures. Real venues differ in how the bankruptcy price is defined, how much of the fund may be used, and how the queue is ordered.

Mathematical foundation

\text{Shortfall} = Q \times \left|P_{\text{bk}} - P_{\text{close}}\right|, \qquad \text{ADL required} = \max\!\left(\text{Shortfall} - F,\ 0\right)

Variables

QSize of the bankrupt position, in contract unitscontracts or units of underlying
P_{\text{bk}}Bankruptcy price: the price at which the position's posted margin is exactly exhaustedprice units
P_{\text{close}}Price at which the liquidation actually executedprice units

Procedure

Compare the price at which the position was actually closed with the price at which its margin was exhausted, and multiply the gap by the size: that is the deficit. Draw the insurance fund against it. Whatever remains is the amount the venue must recover by reducing opposing positions, selected from its own ranking queue.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 100
  • P_bk = 20,000
  • P_close = 19,500
  • F = 30,000
Steps
  1. |20,000 - 19,500| = 500
  2. 100 x 500 = 50,000
  3. 50,000 - 30,000 = 20,000
20,000 of residual loss to be passed on by auto-deleveraging

Interpretation

The arithmetic shows that the deficit is not created by the winners and is still paid by them. Auto-deleveraging is therefore a venue credit mechanism, and the exposure it creates is counterparty risk wearing the clothes of a market event.

Assumptions

  • The shortfall identity is arithmetic, but every input is a venue definition. How the bankruptcy price is computed, which mark or index price it is measured against, and how much of the insurance fund may be drawn are all rulebook matters.
  • The ranking that selects which counterparties are deleveraged is venue-specific. Profit and effective leverage are commonly cited inputs, but no formula is standard and none is reproduced here.
  • Some venues socialise the residual loss across accounts instead of closing positions, and some combine both. The mechanism described is not universal.
  • Figures are per-contract and ignore fees, funding and any clawback or compensation policy a venue may apply afterwards.
Limits of the formula
  • The trigger is not observable in advance: a trader cannot know the insurance fund will be exhausted until it is, and queue indicators are venue-published estimates rather than guarantees.
  • It is concentrated in exactly the conditions where exit liquidity is worst, so it correlates with liquidation cascades rather than occurring independently of them.
  • This entry describes a venue mechanism, not a regulated clearing procedure. Rules differ by venue and jurisdiction and change without notice.

Liquidation
