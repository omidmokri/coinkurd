Simple definition

A fake message or website built to look official so you hand over a password, a code, or a recovery phrase. No real support agent ever asks for a recovery phrase.

Professional definition

Social engineering that imitates a trusted party — an exchange, a wallet, a support channel, a known project — to capture credentials or an authorising signature. In crypto the payload is usually one of three: a recovery phrase, a login plus a one-time code, or a transaction or token approval the victim signs willingly.

Technical definition

The attack works on the person, not the protocol, so cryptography offers no defence. Vectors include lookalike domains and paid search results, cloned wallet applications, fake support staff in chat groups, and airdrop or security-alert messages that end in a signature request. The common signature is an inbound channel plus urgency. The defences are therefore procedural: reach sites from your own bookmarks, never enter a recovery phrase anywhere at all, verify what you are signing on a hardware device screen, and treat any unsolicited contact about your funds as hostile.

Academic definition

A class of social-engineering attack in which an adversary impersonates a trusted counterparty over a communications channel to obtain authentication material or an authorising action from the victim.

Mechanism

Every control a user holds can be handed over voluntarily, so the cheapest attack is simply to ask. Impersonation plus time pressure suppresses verification, and a phrase typed by the owner or a signature they approved is indistinguishable from a legitimate one. This is why procedure beats judgement: rules that never depend on the message being genuine.

In practice

Who uses it
Every user; and also the support, community and security teams who are themselves impersonated.
Why it matters
It is the most common route to retail loss and it defeats strong keys, because the victim performs the action themselves.
Limitations
Awareness decays and the stories adapt. Only habits that hold under pressure help: fixed bookmarks, no phrase entry anywhere, and on-device verification.

Common mistakes

  • Entering a recovery phrase into any website, form, photo or support chat.
  • Reaching a wallet or exchange through a search advert or a message link.
  • Approving a signature request without reading it on the device.

Examples

security

A helpful stranger replies in a public wallet chat, moves to a private message, and asks the user to validate the wallet by entering the twelve words. That request is the attack; there is no situation in which it is legitimate.

everyday

A caller says they are from your bank's fraud desk and need the code they just sent you. The same script, older medium. Hanging up and calling the number on the card is the whole defence.

Commonly confused with

Approval Exploit

Two statements are absolute by design and must not be qualified in review: a recovery phrase or private key is never entered into a website, chat, support ticket, photo or spreadsheet, and no legitimate support agent asks for one. The vector list reflects patterns current as at 2026-11-05 and is not exhaustive; the cited sources are US investor alerts and the mechanism is general.
