Dollar-Cost Averaging

Category
Professional Investment
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Buying a fixed amount of money's worth at regular intervals instead of all at once. The fixed cash buys more units when the price is low and fewer when it is high.

Professional definition

A schedule that commits equal cash amounts at fixed intervals regardless of price. Because quantity varies inversely with price, the average cost per unit is the harmonic mean of the purchase prices, which is at or below their arithmetic mean.

Technical definition

A deployment rule, not a risk control. It lowers the variance of the entry price relative to a single-date purchase, which reduces the dispersion of outcomes attributable to entry timing. It does not reduce exposure to a persistently falling asset, and since capital is idle for part of the schedule, expected terminal value is lower than lump sum whenever expected returns are positive.

Academic definition

A periodic, fixed-expenditure acquisition rule whose realised unit cost equals the harmonic mean of transaction prices, trading expected terminal wealth for reduced dispersion of entry price.

Mechanism

Fixed cash plus a varying price means quantity moves inversely with price, so the schedule mechanically buys more of the cheap and less of the dear. That is arithmetic and always holds. What does not hold is any claim about direction: the rule shapes when capital enters, and nothing about entering slowly changes what the asset then does.

In practice

Who uses it
Retirement and savings schemes, treasury programmes deploying cash over time, and individuals with income arriving periodically rather than in a lump.
Why it matters
It removes the single-date entry decision, which is both a dispersion reduction and a behavioural one: a schedule is easier to keep than a judgement call.
Limitations
It is not protection. Against a persistently falling asset it simply buys more of a declining position, and lump sum has the higher expected value when expected returns are positive.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a schedule a hedge against the asset falling.
  • Averaging into a position with no plan for stopping.
  • Comparing a schedule with lump sum only over periods that suit the comparison.

Examples

everyday

A saver commits the same amount each payday for a year. In the months when the price has fallen the same cash buys more units; in the strong months it buys fewer. The schedule never required a view on which month would be which.

numeric

Three purchases of 300 each at prices of 100, 150 and 75 buy 3, 2 and 4 units. That is 900 for 9 units, so an average cost of 100, against an arithmetic mean price of 108.33.

Given
  • three equal purchases of A = 300 account currency
  • prices P = 100, 150, 75
  • fees ignored, fractional units allowed
Steps
  1. units bought = 300/100 + 300/150 + 300/75 = 3 + 2 + 4 = 9
  2. total invested = 3 x 300 = 900
  3. average cost per unit = 900 / 9 = 100
  4. harmonic mean check = 3 / (1/100 + 1/150 + 1/75) = 3 / 0.03 = 100
  5. arithmetic mean of prices = (100 + 150 + 75) / 3 = 108.33
average cost 100 per unit against an arithmetic mean price of 108.33

The gap between 100 and 108.33 is a property of fixed cash amounts, not evidence of a better outcome. If the price had risen steadily, buying all 9 units at the start would have cost less than 900.

Procedure

Divide each cash instalment by the price paid to get the units bought, and add them. Divide total cash by total units for the average cost. When every instalment is the same size, that average is the harmonic mean of the prices, which equals their arithmetic mean only when all prices are identical and is lower otherwise.

Worked example

Given
  • A = 300 per instalment, n = 3
  • P1 = 100, P2 = 150, P3 = 75
  • no fees
Steps
  1. units = 300/100 + 300/150 + 300/75 = 3 + 2 + 4 = 9
  2. total cash = 900
  3. average cost = 900 / 9 = 100
  4. harmonic mean = 3 / (0.01 + 0.006667 + 0.013333) = 3 / 0.03 = 100
  5. arithmetic mean price = 108.33
average cost 100, which is 8.33 below the arithmetic mean price of 108.33

Interpretation

The harmonic mean result is a certainty about cost accounting, and it is often mistaken for a certainty about returns. It says the schedule paid less than the average quoted price; it says nothing about whether buying at all was profitable.

Assumptions

  • Equal cash per interval and purchases at the quoted price, with fractional units allowed; integer lot sizes change the arithmetic slightly.
  • Fees are excluded. A fixed fee per purchase penalises many small instalments relative to one large one.
  • Prices are the actual fills, not the period averages; a schedule executed at a fixed time of day inherits whatever that time's prices are.
  • The comparison with lump sum assumes the same total capital and the same end date.
Limits of the formula
  • It reduces the variance of the entry price, not the exposure to the asset. Once invested, every unit carries full market risk.
  • When expected returns are positive, holding cash for part of the schedule lowers expected terminal value relative to lump sum; the schedule trades that away for a narrower spread of outcomes.
  • Against an asset in a sustained decline it increases the position while the position loses value, which is the opposite of protection.

Commonly confused with

Position Sizing

Averaging is a schedule for deploying capital over time with no reference to an invalidation level. Position sizing fixes the loss on one trade from a defined stop. One spreads entries, the other bounds losses.

The harmonic-mean cost result is arithmetic and uncontested. The comparison with lump-sum investing is where honest disagreement sits: lump sum has the higher expected terminal value whenever expected returns are positive, while averaging narrows the dispersion of outcomes attributable to entry timing, and which of those matters more is a question about an investor's objective rather than a fact about markets. The entry states both and recommends neither.
