Fully Diluted Valuation

Simple definition

Today's price multiplied by every unit that could ever exist, not just the units trading now. It answers a hypothetical question: what would the whole supply be worth at this price?

Professional definition

A scenario figure equal to the current price times maximum or total supply. It is not a market value, because the un-issued units have never traded and no buyer has committed capital at that price for them. Its only honest use is as an upper bound on dilution, read beside the emission schedule that would produce it.

Technical definition

The construction embeds an assumption that is almost always false: that price is invariant to the arrival of the remaining supply. Two projects can share an identical figure while one issues the remainder over a year and the other over fifteen, so the quantity is not comparable across schedules without discounting for time and price impact. Published figures also differ depending on whether the denominator is maximum supply, total minted supply, or supply net of burns.

Academic definition

A hypothetical aggregate value obtained by applying a prevailing unit price to the maximum issuable supply of an asset, with no adjustment for the time profile of issuance or for the price elasticity of incremental supply.

Mechanism

The figure is a product, so it inherits the weakness of both factors: a price set by the thin float, and a supply that may not arrive for a decade. Multiplying them treats future units as if the market had already priced them, which is why the result is a construction rather than an observation. Its use is the ratio to market value: how much supply is still coming.

In practice

Who uses it
Token analysts and allocators, listing and index committees, and project teams setting an initial float and a release schedule.
Why it matters
The ratio of this figure to current market value shows how much of eventual supply has yet to reach the market, which market value alone hides.
Limitations
It is a hypothetical, not a valuation. It assumes a price that would not survive the supply it assumes, and it is not comparable between assets with different emission schedules.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a token cheap or expensive on this figure alone.
  • Comparing it across projects without comparing their release timetables.
  • Mixing maximum supply and total supply between two assets being ranked.

Examples

numeric

Price 2 units, circulating 100 million, maximum 1 billion. Market value is 200 million and the fully diluted figure is 2 billion: nine tenths of eventual supply has never traded.

Given
  • price = 2 units of account
  • circulating supply = 100,000,000
  • maximum supply = 1,000,000,000
Steps
  1. market value = 2 x 100,000,000 = 200,000,000
  2. fully diluted = 2 x 1,000,000,000 = 2,000,000,000
  3. ratio = 2,000,000,000 / 200,000,000 = 10
market value 200 million; fully diluted 2 billion; ratio 10 to 1

Hypothetical figures. The 2-unit price was set by demand for 100 million units; it is not evidence of what the remaining 900 million would fetch.

comparison

Two tokens report the same fully diluted figure. One releases its remaining supply over twelve months, the other over twelve years. The number is identical and the dilution pressure is not remotely comparable.

Procedure

Take the current price and multiply by the supply figure whose definition you intend — maximum supply, total minted, or total net of burns — and state which you used. Divide the result by market capitalisation to get the dilution multiple, which is the part of the calculation that carries information.

Worked example

Given
  • P = 2 units of account
  • S_total = 1,000,000,000 tokens
  • S_circ = 100,000,000 tokens
Steps
  1. FDV = 2 x 1,000,000,000 = 2,000,000,000
  2. MC = 2 x 100,000,000 = 200,000,000
  3. FDV / MC = 10
FDV = 2,000,000,000; dilution multiple = 10x

Interpretation

The output is explicitly hypothetical: what the full supply would be worth if price were unchanged, which it would not be. The ratio to market value is the usable part, and it states how many times current float still has to be absorbed.

Assumptions

  • Price is held constant while the remaining supply arrives. This is the central assumption and it is unrealistic by construction.
  • The supply input is whichever of maximum, total minted or net-of-burn supply the publisher chose; figures are not comparable unless the same definition is used on both sides.
  • Locked and unissued units are treated as identical to circulating ones, ignoring the time value of a release that may be years away.
Limits of the formula
  • It is not a market value and should never be reported as one: no capital has been committed at that price for the un-issued units.
  • Cross-project comparison is misleading whenever emission schedules differ, because the same figure can represent a year of dilution or a decade of it.
  • The identity says nothing about demand, revenue or whether the remaining supply will ever be issued at all.

Commonly confused with

Market Capitalisation

Market capitalisation uses the supply that trades; this figure uses supply that mostly does not exist yet. One is an aggregate of actual prices paid, the other a scenario.
