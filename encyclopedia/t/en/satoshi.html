Simple definition

The smallest amount of bitcoin the network can record: one hundred-millionth of one BTC. All balances are really whole numbers of satoshi, and BTC is just a convenient way of writing them.

Professional definition

The base unit of account in Bitcoin. One BTC equals 100,000,000 satoshi, so the protocol stores every amount as an integer and never needs decimal arithmetic. Block subsidies, fees and transaction outputs are all denominated in satoshi internally.

Technical definition

An integer unit, not a currency subdivision added afterwards: amounts are stored as 64-bit integers of satoshi, which is why the halving schedule terminates rather than continuing forever, and why fee rates are quoted in satoshi per virtual byte. The name honours the pseudonymous author of the Bitcoin whitepaper, and the unit itself is a naming convention rather than something the whitepaper defines.

Academic definition

The indivisible integer unit of the Bitcoin ledger, equal to 10 to the power of minus eight bitcoin, in which all protocol-level quantities are represented.

Mechanism

Integer arithmetic removes rounding disputes from consensus: every node computing a fee or a subsidy gets exactly the same number, so no node disagrees about the ledger for arithmetic reasons. The choice of eight decimal places also fixes the floor of divisibility, which is why very small on-chain payments stop being expressible long before they stop being wanted.

In practice

Who uses it
Wallet and node developers, fee-estimation tools, Lightning and payment engineers, and traders reading sat-denominated prices for small-cap pairs.
Why it matters
It is the unit the protocol actually uses, so fee rates, dust limits and accounting reconcile exactly only when expressed in satoshi.
Limitations
It is a unit, not a separate asset: holding satoshi is holding bitcoin. Divisibility also has a floor, so sub-satoshi amounts exist only off-chain.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing satoshi per byte with satoshi per transaction when setting a fee.
  • Thinking satoshi are a different coin from BTC.
  • Mixing BTC and satoshi in one spreadsheet column.

Examples

numeric

A balance of 0.00125 BTC is 125,000 satoshi. A fee quoted at 20 satoshi per virtual byte on a 140-vbyte transaction costs 2,800 satoshi, about 2.2 percent of that balance.

Given
  • 1 BTC = 100,000,000 satoshi
  • balance = 0.00125 BTC
  • fee rate = 20 sat/vB
  • transaction size = 140 vB
Steps
  1. 0.00125 x 100,000,000 = 125,000 sat
  2. 20 x 140 = 2,800 sat
  3. 2,800 / 125,000 = 0.0224
125,000 sat balance; 2,800 sat fee; about 2.24 percent of the balance

Fee rates vary with mempool demand. The arithmetic is exact because both sides are integers of satoshi.

everyday

Pricing a coffee in BTC gives a number with many leading zeros. Quoting the same coffee in satoshi gives a readable figure, which is why small-payment interfaces often switch the unit rather than the asset.

Procedure

Multiply a BTC figure by 100,000,000 to get satoshi, or divide by the same factor to go back. The protocol only ever stores the integer, so any BTC figure with more than eight decimal places is not representable on-chain.

Worked example

Given
  • a = 0.5 BTC
  • 1 BTC = 100,000,000 sat
Steps
  1. 0.5 x 100,000,000 = 50,000,000
0.5 BTC = 50,000,000 satoshi

Interpretation

The output is the same quantity of bitcoin written in the protocol's own unit. Nothing about value changes; only the scale of the number does.

Assumptions

  • The 10 to the power of eight ratio is a protocol constant of Bitcoin; other chains divide their native units differently.
  • Amounts are integers of satoshi, so sub-satoshi precision exists only in off-chain accounting such as payment-channel balances.
Limits of the formula
  • The conversion is a change of unit and says nothing about purchasing power or price.
  • Quoting portfolio sizes in satoshi can make a small holding look large; the unit does not change the position.

The 1 BTC = 100,000,000 satoshi relation is a protocol constant. The name 'satoshi' for the unit is a community convention that post-dates the whitepaper, which is stated rather than attributed to the paper. The 64-bit integer representation and the sat/vB fee convention follow implementation practice as described in Mastering Bitcoin. Kurdish keeps the transliterated English word because that is what practitioners use.
