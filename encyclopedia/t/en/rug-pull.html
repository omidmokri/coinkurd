Rug Pull

ENGLISHRug Pullreference
فارسیراگ‌پولEnglish term retained
کوردیڕاگ پوولEnglish term retained
Category
Fundamental Analysis
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

A project collects money from buyers and then the people behind it take the value out and disappear. Buyers are left holding a token nobody will buy.

Professional definition

An exit fraud in which insiders convert other people's capital into their own and abandon the project. The usual routes are withdrawing the pooled liquidity that made the token tradable, selling a large retained allocation into thin depth, or minting new supply for themselves.

Technical definition

The pattern is defined by who holds the levers rather than by any one transaction. Where pool liquidity is unlocked and insider-held, where a mint or transfer-rule function remains callable by a controlling key, or where a large share of supply sits in a handful of linked addresses, the economic exit exists regardless of marketing. Slow versions are harder to label, since insiders can withdraw gradually while the project continues to publish.

Academic definition

A class of exit fraud in which parties controlling a crypto-asset's issuance or its trading liquidity extract value from secondary-market participants and cease to support the asset.

Mechanism

A newly listed token is usually priced only against a single pool, so its quoted price depends entirely on what sits in that pool. Remove the other side of the pool and there is no price at all, only an unsellable balance. Nothing has to be hacked, because the control needed for the exit was granted at launch and left in place.

In practice

Who uses it
Buyers of newly launched tokens, listing and risk teams, and researchers classifying on-chain fraud.
Why it matters
The warning signs are visible before the loss: who holds supply, whether pool liquidity is locked, and which functions a single key can still call.
Limitations
The same signs appear in honest early-stage projects, and a failed project is not automatically a fraud. Intent is rarely provable from on-chain data alone.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a rising chart as evidence that the token can be sold.
  • Treating a locked-liquidity claim as verified without checking it.
  • Assuming a named team or an audit badge removes the exit risk.

Examples

numeric

A pool holds 100,000 units of a stable asset against 1,000,000 tokens, so the quoted price is 0.10. An insider allocation of 1,000,000 tokens sold into that same pool leaves 50,000 of the stable asset and a quoted price of 0.025 — a 75% fall caused by one holder, with no news at all.

Given
  • Constant-product pool
  • Reserves: 100,000 stable units and 1,000,000 tokens
  • Quoted price = 100,000 / 1,000,000 = 0.10
  • Insider sells 1,000,000 tokens into the pool
  • Fees ignored for clarity
Steps
  1. k = 100,000 x 1,000,000 = 1 x 10^11
  2. Token reserve after the sale = 2,000,000
  3. Stable reserve after the sale = 1 x 10^11 / 2,000,000 = 50,000
  4. New quoted price = 50,000 / 2,000,000 = 0.025
Price falls from 0.10 to 0.025, a 75% decline; the seller receives 50,000 stable units

This is ordinary pool arithmetic, not an exploit. It shows why concentrated supply beside shallow liquidity is a structural risk whatever the team's intentions.

market

A token trades well for weeks, then in one block the pooled liquidity is withdrawn. Orders still display, but every sell attempt returns almost nothing. The chart does not show the moment the exit became possible; it was possible from launch.

Honeypot

A honeypot blocks selling from the start. A rug pull lets selling work until the insiders take the value out.
