FOMO and FUD

ENGLISHFOMO and FUDreference
فارسیفومو و فادEnglish term retained
کوردیفۆمۆ و فادEnglish term retained
Category
Market Psychology
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

FOMO is buying because a move is already running and you do not want to be left out. FUD is selling because alarming talk has spread. Both start from a feeling rather than a plan.

Professional definition

Two crowd states that travel through markets: FOMO, entry driven by a rising price and the social visibility of other people's gains; FUD, exit driven by circulating fear, uncertainty and doubt. Both push participants toward late entries, late exits and more transactions than their plan called for.

Technical definition

Descriptive labels for herding behaviour under social information. Their measurable cost is not the emotion but the execution it produces: chasing into thin liquidity widens realised slippage, exits under stress cross a wider spread, and the extra round turns multiply fixed costs. The labels are vernacular, not a measured construct, and they are frequently used after the fact to explain a move already known.

Academic definition

Vernacular terms for socially transmitted buying and selling pressure, in which the salience of others' outcomes and of circulating negative information shifts individual trading behaviour away from a stated plan.

Mechanism

Both states work through salience. A rising price and visible winners make action feel urgent; circulating bad news makes waiting feel reckless. Urgency shortens the decision, and a shortened decision is usually executed with a market order into whatever liquidity is there. The cost lands in the fill price, not in the feeling.

In practice

Who uses it
Every discretionary trader, newer participants most of all, and anyone trading while watching social feeds.
Why it matters
Naming the state is useful because it separates the decision from the mood, and because the fix is procedural rather than a matter of willpower.
Limitations
These are descriptive labels, not diagnoses, and not a reason for self-blame. A pre-written entry rule, an order type and a size limit do more than any attempt to feel differently.

Common mistakes

  • Doubling size to catch up after watching a move leave.
  • Market-selling the whole position into the first alarming headline.
  • Judging a plan by one trade's outcome rather than its rules.

Examples

crypto

A token doubles overnight. Entries cluster at the top of the candle, where depth is thinnest, so the average fill is far worse than the price that attracted attention.

numeric

Chasing a move and exiting in panic twice a week, at an all-in round-trip cost of 0.3 percent, costs about 27 percent of equity over a year before any directional result.

Given
  • 2 round trips per week, 52 weeks = 104 round trips
  • all-in cost per round trip = 0.3 percent of position value
  • full-equity positions, costs charged on each round trip
  • no directional profit or loss assumed
Steps
  1. remaining fraction = (1 - 0.003)^104
  2. = 0.997^104 = 0.7316
  3. cost drag = 1 - 0.7316 = 0.2684
equity multiplier of about 0.73, a drag of roughly 27 percent

Illustrative cost assumption. The point is compounding: the drag is multiplicative in the number of round trips, so frequency hurts even when each individual cost looks small.

Related terminology

related
Overtrading Loss Aversion Fear and Greed Index Slippage Stop-Loss
Commonly confused with

Overtrading

FOMO and FUD name the emotional state behind a decision. Overtrading names the measurable pattern of too many transactions, whatever caused it.

FOMO and FUD are vernacular labels, not measured constructs; the entry treats them that way and locates the quantifiable harm in execution cost rather than in the emotion. No study of herding in crypto markets is in this closed registry, so no magnitude of behavioural effect is claimed. The cost figure in the numeric example is an illustrative assumption. Tone is deliberately non-judgemental: the entry describes the behaviour and its cost without shaming the trader.
