Risk of Ruin

Simple definition

The chance that a run of losses takes your account below the level where you can keep trading, even if the strategy has a positive average result.

Professional definition

The probability that cumulative losses reach a defined ruin threshold before the edge has time to compound. Under the classical setup — fixed unit risk, independent trades, constant win probability and symmetric payoffs — it falls geometrically in the number of units of capital at risk, so unit size dominates everything else.

Technical definition

The classical gambler's-ruin result gives the probability of eventual ruin, starting with N units against an unbounded opponent, as the loss-to-win odds ratio raised to the power N when the win probability exceeds one half, and as certainty otherwise. With unequal payoffs, fractional position sizing, serial dependence or a drifting edge there is no comparable closed form, and ruin probabilities are estimated by simulation instead.

Academic definition

The absorption probability of a random walk with a lower absorbing barrier, evaluated for a trading process under fixed-stake betting and independent identically distributed outcomes.

Mechanism

Ruin is a path problem, not an average problem. The mean of a positive-edge process says where many repetitions end up, but a lower barrier removes paths from the sample: once capital is gone, the remaining favourable outcomes never happen. Larger stakes shorten the distance to the barrier far faster than they raise the mean, which is why the formula's exponent is the number of units, not the edge.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk managers setting a maximum risk per trade, systematic developers sizing a strategy, and anyone deciding how much of an account one idea may risk.
Why it matters
It reframes sizing as a survival question rather than a return question, and it shows how steeply survival improves as the unit risk falls.
Limitations
Its assumptions rarely hold in markets: payoffs are asymmetric, trades are correlated, edges decay, and real ruin is usually a funding or psychological limit rather than a zero balance.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the symmetric-payoff formula to a strategy with uneven payoffs.
  • Assuming independence across correlated positions.
  • Treating a small computed figure as safety rather than as a model output.

Examples

numeric

With a 55 percent win rate and symmetric payoffs, an account divided into 20 equal risk units has about a 1.8 percent chance of eventual ruin. Halve the units to 10 and the figure rises to about 13.4 percent — same edge, seven times the risk.

Given
  • p = 0.55, q = 0.45
  • symmetric payoffs, one unit won or lost per trade
  • N = 20 units of capital, then N = 10
Steps
  1. q / p = 0.45 / 0.55 = 0.8182
  2. N = 20: 0.8182^20 = 0.0181
  3. N = 10: 0.8182^10 = 0.1344
About 1.8 percent at 20 units and about 13.4 percent at 10 units.

The edge did not change; only the stake relative to capital did. This is the whole argument for small unit risk.

market

A leveraged crypto account is often ruined long before its balance hits zero: a maintenance margin breach closes positions at the exchange's price, and the remaining equity can be too small to meet the venue's minimum order size. The practical barrier sits above zero.

Mathematical foundation

R = \left( \frac{q}{p} \right)^{N} \quad \text{for } p > q, \qquad R = 1 \quad \text{for } p \le q

Procedure

Express capital in risk units by dividing equity by the amount risked per trade. Estimate the win probability from a sufficiently long record of one unchanged rule set. If the win probability is at or below one half, ruin is certain in the long run under this model. Otherwise raise the loss-to-win odds ratio to the power of the number of units.

Worked example

Given
  • p = 0.55
  • q = 1 - 0.55 = 0.45
  • equity = 10,000, risk per trade = 500, so N = 20
Steps
  1. q / p = 0.45 / 0.55 = 0.818182
  2. ln(0.818182) = -0.200671
  3. 20 x -0.200671 = -4.013
  4. R = exp(-4.013)
R is about 0.0181, that is roughly 1.8 percent.

Interpretation

The output is the probability that an infinitely repeated process with these parameters eventually touches zero. A low figure means the stake is small relative to capital, not that the strategy is sound: a negative-edge strategy has a ruin probability of one however the stake is set.

Assumptions

  • Fixed stake per trade, so capital is a whole number of identical risk units. Fixed fractional sizing instead shrinks the stake as equity falls, which changes the problem and removes exact ruin.
  • Independent, identically distributed outcomes with a constant win probability. Real trade sequences are serially dependent and regime-sensitive.
  • Symmetric payoffs: one unit won or lost. With uneven payoffs this formula does not apply and no simple closed form replaces it.
  • An unbounded horizon and an unbounded counterparty. Over a finite number of trades the probability of touching the barrier is lower than this figure.
  • No fees, slippage or funding. Each of those shifts the effective win probability downward.
Limits of the formula
  • The assumptions rarely hold together in any real trading record, so the number should be read as a sizing sensitivity rather than a forecast.
  • Under fractional sizing, mathematical ruin is replaced by practical ruin: a drawdown past the point where the account can no longer meet margin, minimum size or the trader's own tolerance. That threshold has to be chosen, and simulation is the usual tool.
  • Estimating the win probability from a short sample dominates the result, because the exponent magnifies small errors in the odds ratio.

Commonly confused with

Drawdown

Drawdown measures a fall in equity that already happened and can be recovered from. Risk of ruin is a forward-looking probability of reaching a level from which there is no recovery because the capital to continue is gone.

The formula given is the standard gambler's-ruin result for fixed stakes and symmetric payoffs, and is mathematically uncontested within those assumptions. What is contested is its application to trading: the assumptions of independence, constant edge and symmetric payoffs rarely hold together, which the entry states plainly, and practitioners use several different definitions of the ruin threshold.
