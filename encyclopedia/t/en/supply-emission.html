Supply Emission

Simple definition

The rate at which new units of an asset are created and reach holders. A schedule can be fixed, declining, demand-driven, or set by governance — and the rate matters more than any cap.

Professional definition

The flow of newly issued units per period, set by protocol rules: block subsidies, staking rewards, scheduled releases from allocations, or a mix. Expressed as an annualised percentage of existing supply it is comparable across assets, and it is the quantity that determines dilution for a holder who does nothing.

Technical definition

Emission has two distinct forms that are often conflated. Protocol issuance creates units that did not exist and dilutes every holder proportionally. Release of already-minted units from a lock does not change total supply but does change float, and reaches the market the same way. A third form is net issuance, gross issuance less burns, which under usage-linked burning is demand-dependent and can be negative. Comparing emission rates therefore requires stating which of the three is being measured and over what window.

Academic definition

The time profile of newly created units of a crypto-asset mandated by its consensus or contract rules, expressed as a rate relative to existing supply, and distinct from the release of previously issued units into tradable float.

Mechanism

New units have to be absorbed by new demand or the price falls, all else equal — that is the whole arithmetic of dilution. What differs between designs is who the units go to and what they are being paid for: security, liquidity, or simply a vesting calendar. Issuance paid for something the network needs can be worth its cost; issuance paid for nothing is a transfer.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol designers setting reward budgets, validators and liquidity providers earning emissions, and analysts comparing dilution across assets.
Why it matters
It is the single number that tells a passive holder how much of their share is being given away each year, independent of price.
Limitations
It describes supply only, says nothing about demand, and is not comparable across assets unless the same definition and window are used on both sides.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a staking yield as income when it is paid in the same diluting token.
  • Comparing a gross issuance rate on one asset with a net rate on another.
  • Reading a falling emission rate as a bullish signal by itself.

Examples

numeric

Supply is 80 million and 4.8 million new units are issued over a year. That is a 6 percent annual rate: a holder who neither stakes nor buys ends the year with the same units and a smaller share.

Given
  • supply at the start of the year = 80,000,000 units
  • new units issued during the year = 4,800,000
  • no burns and no change in locked balances
Steps
  1. 4,800,000 / 80,000,000 = 0.06
  2. 0.06 = 6 percent annualised issuance
  3. a holder's share falls by a factor of 80 / 84.8, about 5.66 percent
6 percent annual issuance; a passive holder's proportional share falls about 5.66 percent

Hypothetical figures. The dilution of the share is not the same number as the issuance rate, because the denominator grows too.

comparison

One asset issues on a fixed declining schedule known years ahead. Another issues whatever governance votes each quarter. The first can be modelled; the second is a policy risk that no chart of past emission captures.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Take new units issued over the window and divide by supply at the start of that window, then annualise if the window is shorter than a year. For the dilution a passive holder actually suffers, compare the opening supply with the closing supply rather than reading the issuance rate directly.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 80,000,000 units at the start of the year
  • I = 4,800,000 units issued during the year
Steps
  1. e = 4,800,000 / 80,000,000 = 0.06
  2. closing supply = 84,800,000
  3. d = 1 - 80,000,000 / 84,800,000 = 0.0566
e = 6 percent; d = about 5.66 percent

Interpretation

The issuance rate is a supply flow relative to a stock. The dilution figure is what it costs a holder who takes no action. Neither is a forecast of price, and neither accounts for burns unless they are netted off first.

Assumptions

  • Supply at the start of the window is measured on the same basis as the issuance figure: both gross, or both net of burns.
  • Releases from locks are not protocol issuance. Including them in I measures float growth rather than new creation, and the two should not be mixed in one figure.
  • Annualising a short window assumes the rate persists, which is false for demand-driven or governance-set emission.
Limits of the formula
  • The rate says nothing about demand, so a high emission rate is not by itself evidence of future price weakness.
  • Where burning is usage-linked, the net rate varies with activity and any single figure needs its window stated.
  • Comparison across assets is only valid when both sides use the same definition of supply and the same window.

Commonly confused with

Token Unlock

Issuance creates units that did not exist and dilutes everyone. An unlock moves existing units from locked to tradable. Both increase sellable supply, but only one changes the total.
