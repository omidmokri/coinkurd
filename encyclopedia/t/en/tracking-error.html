Tracking Error

Quantitative Finance

Professional

Simple definition

How much a portfolio's return wobbles around its benchmark's return. Small means it hugs the index; large means it goes its own way.

Professional definition

The standard deviation of active return, where active return is portfolio return minus benchmark return over the same period. It measures the size of deviation from the benchmark, not its direction, and is the denominator of the information ratio.

Technical definition

Compute active return period by period, then take its standard deviation; annualise by multiplying by the square root of the number of periods per year. Ex-post tracking error is the realised sample figure; ex-ante tracking error is a forecast from a risk model and the two routinely disagree. It must not be confused with tracking difference, which is the cumulative return gap between fund and benchmark and has no dispersion in it at all.

Academic definition

The standard deviation of the differential return between a portfolio and its benchmark, used as the measure of active risk in benchmark-relative performance evaluation.

Mechanism

A portfolio differs from its benchmark through position weights, sector tilts, cash holdings and trading costs. Each deviation adds variance to the active return series, and tracking error aggregates all of them into one number. Because it is a standard deviation, offsetting deviations do not cancel: two bets that pull in opposite directions still both raise the figure.

In practice

Who uses it
Index-fund and ETF managers held to a mandate, allocators setting risk budgets, and anyone reporting an information ratio.
Why it matters
It is the unit in which an active risk budget is written, and the only sensible denominator for judging whether active return was earned or stumbled into.
Limitations
It is symmetric, so beating the benchmark raises it just as much as lagging. A fund that outperforms wildly is reported as high risk.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing tracking error with the cumulative return gap to the benchmark.
  • Comparing a monthly figure with an annualised one.
  • Comparing a risk model's forecast with a realised sample figure.

Examples

numeric

Quarterly active returns +3, -1, +3, -1, +1 percent. The mean is +1% and the sample standard deviation is exactly 2%, so quarterly tracking error is 2% and the annualised figure is 4%.

Given
  • active returns = [3, -1, 3, -1, 1] in % per quarter
  • sample standard deviation with divisor n - 1
  • 4 quarters per year
Steps
  1. mean active return = 5 / 5 = 1%
  2. deviations = 2, -2, 2, -2, 0; squares = 4, 4, 4, 4, 0; sum = 16
  3. sample variance = 16 / 4 = 4, so TE per quarter = 2%
  4. annualised TE = 2% * sqrt(4) = 4%
quarterly tracking error 2%, annualised 4%

With the population divisor n the same data give 1.79% per quarter instead of 2%. The divisor must be disclosed.

market

Two index funds both report tracking difference of 0.10% behind the index. One achieved it with steady small shortfalls, the other by alternating plus and minus one percent. Identical difference, very different tracking error.

Mathematical foundation

\mathrm{TE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(a_t - \bar{a}\right)^2}, \qquad a_t = R_{p,t} - R_{b,t}, \qquad \mathrm{TE}_{\text{ann}} = \mathrm{TE}\sqrt{m}

Variables

a_tActive return in period t: portfolio minus benchmark% per period
\bar{a}Mean active return over the sample% per period
R_{p,t}Portfolio return in period t% per period
R_{b,t}Benchmark return in the same period% per period
mNumber of periods per year used for annualisationcount

Procedure

Align portfolio and benchmark returns on identical periods, net of the same fees and in the same currency. Subtract to get the active return series. Take its standard deviation, stating whether the divisor is n or n minus one. Multiply by the square root of the periods per year to annualise, and report the period, the divisor and the benchmark together.

Worked example

Given
  • active returns = [3, -1, 3, -1, 1] % per quarter
  • n = 5
  • divisor n - 1 = 4
  • m = 4 quarters per year
Steps
  1. mean = 1%
  2. sum of squared deviations = 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 0 = 16
  3. variance = 16 / 4 = 4, TE = 2% per quarter
  4. annualised TE = 2 * sqrt(4) = 4%
TE = 2% quarterly, 4% annualised

Interpretation

The number is a typical size of deviation from the benchmark, in the same units as return. A 4% annual figure says active results of roughly plus or minus four points around the benchmark are ordinary, not exceptional.

Assumptions

  • Sample (n minus one) and population (n) divisors are both in use; the figures differ and the choice must be disclosed.
  • Annualisation by the square root of m assumes serially independent active returns; persistent active positions violate this and understate the annual figure.
  • Portfolio and benchmark returns must be measured net of the same costs and in the same currency, otherwise the active series mixes a currency effect into active risk.
Limits of the formula
  • It is a dispersion measure, so outperformance and underperformance both increase it.
  • Ex-ante forecasts from a risk model and ex-post realised figures are different quantities and are frequently reported as if interchangeable.
  • A concentrated portfolio can show low tracking error over a quiet sample and far higher risk in a stressed one.

Commonly confused with

Beta

Beta measures how strongly a portfolio moves with the benchmark. Tracking error measures how large the leftover deviation is. A portfolio can have beta one and still deviate heavily.

Commonly confused with

Information Ratio

Tracking error is the denominator; the information ratio divides mean active return by it. One is a risk figure, the other a reward-per-risk figure.
