Simple definition

Conduct that makes a price or a market look like something it is not, so that other people trade on a false picture. It is prohibited in regulated markets.

Professional definition

The prohibited class of conduct that creates a false or misleading impression of supply, demand or price, or secures a price at an artificial level. The offence lies in the deception of other participants, not in being wrong about value, and it is defined by effect and intention rather than by any particular trading technique.

Technical definition

Regulatory definitions are deliberately open-ended because any closed list of techniques would be designed around. Supervisors therefore reason from harm: did conduct interfere with the price-formation process, and was the resulting price informative or artificial. Surveillance works from patterns — order-to-trade ratios, self-matching, timing around reference prices, concentration at settlement windows — and detection in crypto markets is impaired by fragmented venues, uneven surveillance and the absence of a consolidated audit trail.

Academic definition

Conduct that distorts the price-formation process by creating a false impression of market conditions or by setting prices at artificial levels, prohibited in regulated markets by reference to effect and intent rather than to enumerated techniques.

Mechanism

A price is useful only because it aggregates genuine intention. Manipulation attacks that function: it inserts signals no one means to honour, so other participants update on information that was manufactured. The harm is borne by whoever trades against the false picture, and by the market as a whole through wider spreads and withdrawn liquidity once participants learn the signal cannot be trusted.

In practice

Who uses it
Surveillance and market-integrity teams, exchange compliance, supervisors and enforcement staff, and traders who must document legitimate intent.
Why it matters
It defines the line between aggressive but legitimate trading and a prohibited act, and it is the basis on which venues suspend accounts and authorities bring cases.
Limitations
Definitions, available remedies and whether a venue is covered at all differ by jurisdiction. Thin, fragmented markets are both easier to distort and harder to police.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming unregulated venues cannot give rise to liability anywhere.
  • Treating any large adverse move as evidence that someone manipulated the market.
  • Believing that a legitimate hedge cannot be questioned if its pattern looks abusive.

Examples

regulatory

A venue's surveillance flags concentrated activity in the minutes that set a reference price used to settle derivatives elsewhere. The volume is small; the leverage it influences is not. Impact on the reference, not size, drives the review.

numeric

In a book with 40,000 of resting bids within one per cent, a 120,000 market buy clears three per cent of depth tiers and prints a visible spike. Thin depth is why distortion is cheap in small markets.

Given
  • Resting bid depth within 1% of mid = 40,000 (quote currency)
  • Incoming market buy = 120,000
  • Depth beyond 1% assumed progressively thinner
Steps
  1. Order size / depth within 1% = 120,000 / 40,000 = 3
  2. The order consumes about three times the depth available in the first band
  3. Price must travel into successively thinner bands to fill
An order three times the near-touch depth moves price well beyond one per cent

Illustrative depth figures. This describes why thin books are fragile and why surveillance weights price impact against available depth. It is not a description of how to execute anything.

Commonly confused with

Oracle Manipulation

Oracle manipulation is a technical attack on a protocol's price input. Market manipulation is a legal category about distorting the market itself.

Commonly confused with

Price Discovery

Price discovery is the process manipulation interferes with. Defining the offence by that interference is what makes the category workable.
