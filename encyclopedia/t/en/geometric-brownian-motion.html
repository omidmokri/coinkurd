Geometric Brownian Motion

Simple definition

The standard textbook model of a price: it drifts upward or downward at a steady percentage rate while being pushed around randomly, so it can approach zero but never go negative.

Professional definition

A continuous-time process whose proportional changes are driven by a constant drift plus a constant volatility times Brownian increments. Prices are therefore lognormally distributed at every horizon and log returns are normal, independent and of variance proportional to elapsed time.

Technical definition

The solution of a linear stochastic differential equation in which both drift and diffusion scale with the level, which keeps the process positive and makes percentage changes the natural scale. Its log is a Brownian motion with drift equal to the drift rate minus half the variance rate, so the median grows more slowly than the mean. Real return series contradict the specification in three ways: tails are fatter than normal, volatility is autocorrelated rather than constant, and prices jump.

Academic definition

The positive-valued diffusion solving a linear stochastic differential equation with level-proportional drift and diffusion, whose logarithm is Brownian motion with drift and whose marginal distributions are lognormal.

Mechanism

Making both terms proportional to the level turns the model into a statement about percentage changes, which is why it never reaches zero and why risk is naturally quoted as volatility. Compounding of random proportional changes then makes the sum of log returns normal and the level lognormal, so the distribution is right-skewed: the mean sits above the median by half the variance, which is arithmetic, not optimism.

In practice

Who uses it
Option pricers, risk teams generating scenario paths, structured product desks, and anyone running a Monte Carlo on a price series.
Why it matters
It is the baseline that makes closed-form option prices and simple scenario engines possible, and the reference against which departures from normality are described.
Limitations
It underlies Black-Scholes option pricing, and real returns violate every one of its assumptions: tails are fat, volatility clusters, and jumps occur. Prices from it are a convention, not a measurement.

Common mistakes

  • Reading simulated paths as a realistic picture of tail risk.
  • Confusing the drift parameter with the expected growth of the level.
  • Calibrating volatility on a calm sample and projecting it unchanged.

Examples

numeric

Start at 100 with a drift of 10% and volatility of 40% per year. After one year the mean level is about 110.5 but the median is only about 102.0. The gap is half the variance rate, and it widens as volatility rises.

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • mu = 0.10 per year
  • sigma = 0.40 per year
  • T = 1 year
Steps
  1. E[S_T] = 100 x exp(0.10 x 1) = 100 x 1.1052
  2. median S_T = 100 x exp((0.10 - 0.5 x 0.16) x 1) = 100 x exp(0.02) = 100 x 1.0202
mean about 110.5, median about 102.0

Both figures are consequences of lognormality under assumed parameters, not forecasts. Under fat tails and clustering neither is a reliable description of the real distribution.

market

Option prices quoted through the model imply a different volatility at each strike, forming a smile. That pattern is the market correcting the model's assumption of a single constant volatility and a normal log return.

Mathematical foundation

dS_t = \mu S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma W_T\right]

Variables

S_tLevel of the asset at time t; strictly positiveprice units
\muInstantaneous drift rate of the proportional changeper unit time
\sigmaVolatility: instantaneous standard deviation of the proportional changeper square root of time
dW_tIncrement of a standard Wiener process: independent, mean zero, variance equal to dtsquare root of time
THorizon over which the solution is evaluatedtime

Procedure

Fix the drift and volatility rates and the horizon. The solution says the log of the ratio of final to initial level is normal with mean drift minus half the variance rate times the horizon, and variance the variance rate times the horizon. Simulate by drawing that normal variate and exponentiating, or read off moments directly.

Worked example

Given
  • S_0 = 100
  • mu = 0.10
  • sigma = 0.40
  • T = 1
Steps
  1. drift of the log = 0.10 - 0.5 x 0.40^2 = 0.10 - 0.08 = 0.02
  2. median = 100 x exp(0.02) = 102.0
  3. mean = 100 x exp(0.10) = 110.5
median about 102.0, mean about 110.5

Interpretation

Mu is the growth rate of the mean, not of the typical path. Subtracting half the variance rate gives the growth rate of the median, so higher volatility lowers the typical outcome even with the drift unchanged.

Assumptions

  • Drift and volatility are constant, increments are independent, and the path is continuous with no jumps.
  • Log returns are exactly normal at every horizon, so all moments beyond the second are fixed by the first two.
  • Under the risk-neutral measure used for option pricing the drift is replaced by the risk-free rate; the physical drift and the pricing drift are different objects and must not be mixed.
Limits of the formula
  • This process is the price model behind Black-Scholes option pricing, and empirical return series violate all three of its core assumptions: tails are fatter than normal, volatility is clustered rather than constant, and prices jump.
  • Because the model forbids fat tails and clustering, risk measures computed from it understate the probability of large moves, and the implied volatility smile is the market's visible correction to it.

Commonly confused with

Random Walk

A random walk adds shocks to the level, so it can go negative. Geometric Brownian motion multiplies the level by random factors, so it stays positive; its logarithm, not the level itself, is the walk.
