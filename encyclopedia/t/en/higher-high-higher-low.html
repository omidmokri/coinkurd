Higher High, Higher Low

Simple definition

An uptrend written as a sequence: each swing high above the last one, each swing low above the last one. The downtrend version is lower highs with lower lows.

Professional definition

The labelling convention for trend in swing-based price action. An uptrend requires both a higher high and a higher low on confirmed swing points; a downtrend requires lower highs and lower lows. Mixed sequences, such as a higher high with a lower low, are left unlabelled or called a range.

Technical definition

Given a confirmed swing series from a fixed fractal rule, the state is up while the latest swing high exceeds the previous swing high and the latest swing low exceeds the previous swing low. The condition is retrospective and inherits the k-bar confirmation lag of the swing rule. Because it compares only the last two swings of each kind, a single deep pullback can flip the label while a longer-horizon trend is untouched.

Academic definition

A deterministic state-labelling rule for trend direction based on the monotonicity of successive local extrema in a filtered price series.

Mechanism

The rule is true by construction, not by evidence: a series of rising extremes is what the word uptrend denotes, so the label can never contradict the price it describes. What it buys is comparability — every chart gets the same state machine. What it cannot do is tell you whether the next swing continues the sequence.

In practice

Who uses it
Price-action traders describing trend state, and developers building a trend filter from confirmed swing points rather than from a moving average.
Why it matters
It gives a trend definition with no smoothing parameter and no lag beyond the swing rule, and it names the exact event that would end the trend.
Limitations
The label depends entirely on the swing rule beneath it, and no peer-reviewed study establishes an edge in trading the sequence itself.

Common mistakes

  • Reading one broken low as a trend reversal.
  • Using different swing rules on different timeframes.
  • Labelling a range as a trend by ignoring the lows.

Examples

market

A market prints swing lows at 90, then 96, and swing highs at 108, then 114. Both sequences are rising, so the state is an uptrend. A later swing low at 93 breaks the sequence of lows and the label is withdrawn even though the last high still stands.

numeric

On the four-hour chart the last two lows are 96 and 93, a lower low. On the daily the last two lows are 78 and 90, a higher low. Both labels are correct on their own series; only the timeframe differs.

Given
  • 4h swing lows: 96 then 93
  • daily swing lows: 78 then 90
  • same instrument, same moment
Steps
  1. 4h: 93 < 96, so lower low, uptrend label fails
  2. daily: 90 > 78, so higher low, uptrend label holds
Opposite structure labels on one instrument at one moment.

Neither reading is wrong. A structure claim is meaningless until the timeframe and the swing rule are stated with it.

Commonly confused with

Trendline

A trendline is a drawn line whose slope and anchors are chosen by hand. The higher-high higher-low sequence is a comparison of numbers and needs no drawing at all.
