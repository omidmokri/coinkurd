Simple definition

Short-term government borrowing, usually for a year or less. There is no interest coupon: you buy below the face amount and are repaid the face amount at maturity.

Professional definition

Zero-coupon government obligations issued at a discount with maturities typically from a few weeks to a year. Sold at auction, highly liquid, and widely used as collateral and as the practical reference for short-dated government credit in their currency.

Technical definition

Quoting conventions matter: a discount rate is computed on face value and understates the actual return, while the bond-equivalent or investment yield is computed on the price paid and is the figure comparable with coupon bonds. Both exist for the same bill, and converting between them requires the day-count basis. Price sensitivity to yield is small because maturity is short, but reinvestment risk is correspondingly large.

Academic definition

Short-maturity sovereign discount instruments, conventionally quoted on a discount basis and convertible to a bond-equivalent yield for comparison with coupon-bearing debt.

Mechanism

With no coupons, the whole return is the gap between purchase price and face value, so the price falls further below face the higher the required yield. Because the horizon is weeks or months, a yield shock moves the price only slightly — but the holder must reinvest soon, at whatever rate then prevails, which is where the real exposure sits.

In practice

Who uses it
Money funds, corporate treasuries, banks managing liquidity buffers, and collateral desks in repo markets.
Why it matters
They are the standard short-dated parking place for cash in their currency and the usual empirical stand-in for a short risk-free rate in that currency.
Limitations
The discount quote is not the return, no instrument is literally risk-free, and rolling bills repeatedly exposes the holder to whatever rates arrive at each roll.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a quoted discount rate as the yield earned.
  • Mixing a 360-day and a 365-day basis in one comparison.
  • Calling a bill risk-free without naming the currency.

Examples

numeric

A 91-day bill with face 100 is quoted at a 4 percent discount rate. The price is 98.989, and the return actually earned on that price, annualised over 365 days, is about 4.10 percent.

Given
  • face value F = 100
  • quoted discount rate d = 4.00% on a 360-day basis
  • days to maturity t = 91
Steps
  1. price = 100 x (1 - 0.04 x 91/360) = 100 x (1 - 0.0101111) = 98.98889
  2. gain = 100 - 98.98889 = 1.01111
  3. BEY = (1.01111 / 98.98889) x (365 / 91) = 0.0102144 x 4.010989
price = 98.989; bond-equivalent yield = about 4.10%

The discount rate is invented. The discount quote is always below the bond-equivalent yield for the same bill, because one divides by face value and the other by the lower purchase price.

market

A treasurer rolling three-month bills for a year faces four reinvestment decisions. If short rates fall through the year, the realised return is well below the first bill's yield, with no price loss anywhere.

Mathematical foundation

P = F \left( 1 - d \times \frac{t}{360} \right), \qquad BEY = \frac{F - P}{P} \times \frac{365}{t}

Procedure

From a discount quote, multiply the rate by days over the discount basis and subtract from one to get the price per unit of face. To compare with a coupon bond, divide the gain by the price actually paid, then annualise on the yield basis that market uses, stating which basis you used.

Worked example

Given
  • F = 100
  • d = 0.04 on a 360-day basis
  • t = 91 days
  • BEY annualised on 365 days
Steps
  1. 0.04 x 91 / 360 = 0.0101111
  2. P = 100 x (1 - 0.0101111) = 98.98889
  3. (100 - 98.98889) / 98.98889 = 0.0102144
  4. x 365 / 91 = 0.0102144 x 4.010989 = 0.040950
P = 98.989 per 100 face; BEY = about 4.10%

Interpretation

Two numbers describe one bill. The discount rate is a quoting convention on face value; the bond-equivalent yield is the return on money actually invested and is always the larger of the two. Any comparison with other instruments must use the second.

Assumptions

  • Day-count bases are market conventions: discount quotes commonly use 360 days and yield conversions 365, and a leap-year or alternative basis changes the result.
  • The bill is held to maturity and the issuer pays face value; no credit event and no accrued coupon are involved.
  • Auction and secondary-market conventions, settlement timing and tax treatment are jurisdiction-specific.
Limits of the formula
  • The formula gives the yield on one bill only; a programme of rolling bills has a realised return that depends on future rates, not on today's quote.
  • Calling the result a risk-free rate is a convention tied to one issuer and one currency, and ignores inflation and currency risk.

Commonly confused with

Risk-free rate

A bill yield is often used as the empirical risk-free rate, but it is only the yield of one issuer in one currency. It still carries inflation and currency risk, and the identification is a convention.
