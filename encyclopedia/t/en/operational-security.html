Operational Security

Simple definition

The everyday habits and rules that keep an attacker away from your keys and accounts: separate devices, verified paths to sites, no secrets typed anywhere, and a plan you have tested.

Professional definition

The human and process layer of security. It starts from a threat model — what you hold, who would want it, what they can reach — and sets rules that hold under pressure: device separation, account hygiene, strong second factors, written custody and rotation procedures, and rehearsed recovery.

Technical definition

Nearly all realised crypto losses are operational rather than cryptographic: approved transactions, phished credentials, reused phone numbers, backups in cloud storage, insiders. Good operational security therefore removes discretion from the moment of attack: fixed navigation instead of links, a dedicated signing device, hardware second factors, written procedures, and the absolute rule that a recovery phrase or private key is never typed, pasted, photographed or dictated to anyone — no legitimate support agent will ever ask for one.

Academic definition

The discipline of designing processes, devices and habits such that a realistic adversary cannot reach the credentials or authorisations that control an asset.

Mechanism

Attackers choose the cheapest path, which is nearly always a person under time pressure rather than a cipher. Judgement degrades exactly when an attack is underway, so controls that depend on noticing something are weak and controls that remove the option are strong. Rules, defaults and separated devices work because they do not require the user to be right in the moment.

In practice

Who uses it
Every holder; and formally the security, treasury and operations functions of firms, funds and DAOs.
Why it matters
It addresses where losses actually happen, and it is the only layer that improves without changing the protocol or the keys.
Limitations
It cannot make a bad custody design safe, decays without review, and is bounded by coercion, insider access and the weakest service in the recovery chain.

Common mistakes

  • Signing transactions on the same device used for browsing and messaging.
  • Storing a recovery phrase, key or 2FA backup in cloud notes or photos.
  • Having no written plan for device loss, incapacity or death.

Examples

numeric

A phone holds the wallet, the authenticator app, the password manager and the email account. On paper there are four controls; one unlocked phone defeats all four, so the number of independent controls is one.

Given
  • Controls listed = wallet keys, authenticator app, password manager, email account
  • All four reside on one device
  • Scenario: the device is unlocked and compromised
Steps
  1. Controls that survive the compromise = 0
  2. Independent controls = number of separate devices or channels involved = 1
Four named controls, one independent control

Illustrative counting. Independence, not quantity, is what makes layers work; splitting the same four across a signing device, a second phone and an offline note changes the count.

everyday

A shop does not rely on staff spotting a thief. It keeps a small till, counts it at fixed times, and puts the rest in a safe. The controls work whether or not anyone is paying attention that day.

Commonly confused with

Custody Risk

Custody risk is the exposure a holding arrangement creates. Operational security is the set of controls applied to it, and it cannot repair a bad arrangement.

The claim that nearly all realised losses are operational rather than cryptographic is stated qualitatively on purpose: the source registry contains no dataset quantifying the split, so no percentage is given. The specific control list is a synthesis of common practice rather than a published standard. The absolute rule — a recovery phrase or private key is never typed, pasted, photographed or dictated to anyone, and no legitimate support agent asks for one — must not be qualified in review.
