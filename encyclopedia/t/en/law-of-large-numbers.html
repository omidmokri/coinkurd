Law of Large Numbers

Simple definition

As you repeat a random experiment more and more times, the average of the results settles towards its true expected value. It says nothing about when that happens.

Professional definition

For independent draws with a finite mean, the sample average converges to the population mean as the sample grows. The weak form converges in probability, the strong form almost surely. Neither form bounds how far the running average can wander on the way.

Technical definition

Let X1 to Xn be independent and identically distributed with finite expectation and mean mu. The weak law gives convergence of the sample mean to mu in probability; Kolmogorov's strong law gives almost-sure convergence. Finite variance is not required for the strong law; independence and a finite first moment are. The result is asymptotic: it fixes no sample size, supplies no rate, and permits arbitrarily long stretches in which the running average sits far from mu.

Academic definition

A convergence theorem stating that the arithmetic mean of independent, identically distributed random variables with finite expectation converges to that expectation — in probability under the weak form, almost surely under Kolmogorov's strong form.

Mechanism

Averaging divides the accumulated noise by n while leaving the signal untouched, so dispersion of the mean shrinks without bound. What shrinks is the dispersion, not any single path. A path may stay on one side of mu for a very long time, because the law constrains the limit and not the journey.

In practice

Who uses it
Strategy developers judging whether a backtest sample is large enough, risk managers sizing against expectancy, and anyone pricing repeated bets.
Why it matters
It is the formal reason an edge measured on many independent observations is more credible than the same edge measured on a few.
Limitations
It promises a limit, not a horizon. It also assumes independent draws with a stable mean — a condition most return series violate.

Common mistakes

  • Treating fifty trades as 'enough' for an edge to show.
  • Invoking it while trades share one regime and are not independent.
  • Confusing it with a promise that losses must even out.

Examples

market

Two traders share the same positive expectancy. One stops after forty trades down on the year and calls the edge fake; the other is still running after four hundred. Only the second sample is large enough for the law to say anything.

numeric

Per-trade edge 0.2% with 2% dispersion. To be 95% sure the measured average is within 0.2% of the truth, Chebyshev's bound needs about 2,000 independent trades — not fifty.

Given
  • sigma = 2% per trade
  • epsilon = 0.2%
  • target failure probability = 5%
  • trades assumed independent with a stable mean
Steps
  1. Chebyshev: P(|mean - mu| > epsilon) <= sigma^2 / (n * epsilon^2)
  2. require sigma^2 / (n * epsilon^2) <= 0.05
  3. n >= 0.0004 / (0.000004 * 0.05) = 2000
n = 2,000 trades

Chebyshev is deliberately loose, so this is an upper bound on the requirement, not a forecast. It also needs finite variance, which the strong law itself does not.

Mathematical foundation

\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \qquad \lim_{n \to \infty} P\big( |\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon \big) = 0 \;\; \forall \varepsilon > 0

Variables

\bar{X}_nSample mean of the first n observationssame as X
\muPopulation mean, the expected value of Xsame as X
\varepsilonAny chosen tolerance around the meansame as X
nNumber of independent observationscount

Procedure

Confirm the draws are independent and drawn from one distribution with a finite mean. Average them. The weak law then says the probability that this average misses mu by more than any fixed tolerance tends to zero as n grows. To turn that into a sample size you need a separate inequality, such as Chebyshev's bound or the central limit theorem.

Worked example

Given
  • sigma = 0.02 (2% per trade)
  • epsilon = 0.002 (0.2%)
  • allowed failure probability 0.05
Steps
  1. Chebyshev bound: sigma^2 / (n * epsilon^2) <= 0.05
  2. 0.0004 / (n * 0.000004) <= 0.05
  3. n >= 0.0004 / 0.0000002 = 2000
n = 2,000 independent trades

Interpretation

The statement is about the limit of a sequence of probabilities. It licenses the claim that a large independent sample estimates a mean well, and forbids any claim about a specific finite sample without a further inequality.

Assumptions

  • Observations are independent and identically distributed; clustered or regime-dependent trades are not.
  • The first moment is finite. For a distribution with no finite mean the law simply does not apply.
  • The Chebyshev step additionally requires finite variance, which the strong law does not.
Limits of the formula
  • No rate and no sample size follow from the theorem itself.
  • Convergence of the average does not bound the path, so an account can be ruined long before the limit is approached.
  • Strictly stated the theorem concerns the sample mean, not the cumulative sum, which does not converge.

Commonly confused with

Central Limit Theorem

The law of large numbers says where the sample mean goes. The central limit theorem says how it is distributed around that destination.

Commonly confused with

Gambler's Fallacy

The law constrains an average over an unbounded sample. It never implies that a run of losses makes a win due.
