Bid-Ask Spread

Foundation

Simple definition

The gap between the best price a buyer will pay and the best price a seller will accept. Cross it and you pay it: that gap is the cost of trading right now.

Professional definition

The difference between the best bid and the best ask on an order book. Quoted absolutely or relative to the mid-price, it is the cost of an immediate round trip and the tightest, most-watched dimension of liquidity.

Technical definition

Spread is the best ask minus the best bid; relative spread divides that by the mid-price and is usually quoted in basis points so instruments at different price levels can be compared. The quoted spread describes the top of book only: an order larger than the best quote pays a wider effective spread, which is why execution analysis uses size-weighted or effective spread instead. Exchange fees sit on top and are not part of it.

Academic definition

The difference between the best available ask and bid quotations, conventionally decomposed into order-processing costs, inventory-holding costs and the adverse-selection cost borne by liquidity suppliers.

Mechanism

The spread is what pays the liquidity supplier. It has to cover the cost of handling the order, the risk of holding inventory the market is moving against, and the losses taken from better-informed counterparties. When any of those rise — a volatility spike, a thin book, pending news — quotes widen or are pulled, which is why spreads are widest exactly when a trader most wants to transact.

In practice

Who uses it
Every taker of liquidity, and above all market makers, execution desks and high-frequency strategies.
Why it matters
It is the floor on round-trip cost, so a strategy's edge must clear it before fees, and a frequently trading strategy lives or dies on it.
Limitations
The quoted spread describes the top of book at one instant and says nothing about the depth behind it. Quotes can also be withdrawn before an order arrives.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing absolute spreads across instruments at different price levels.
  • Assuming the quoted spread applies to any order size.
  • Counting the spread but forgetting exchange fees.

Examples

market

A major pair on a deep venue quotes a spread of a basis point or two all day. The same exchange's thin listing quotes tens of basis points, and both widen into a scheduled announcement as liquidity suppliers step back.

numeric

The best bid is 100.00 and the best ask 100.10. The spread is 0.10, the mid is 100.05, and the relative spread is about 10 basis points — which is what an immediate buy and sell at these quotes would cost, before fees.

Given
  • best bid = 100.00
  • best ask = 100.10
  • fees excluded
Steps
  1. spread = 100.10 - 100.00 = 0.10
  2. mid = (100.10 + 100.00) / 2 = 100.05
  3. relative spread = 0.10 / 100.05 = 0.000999
  4. in basis points: 0.000999 x 10,000 = 10.0
Spread 0.10, about 10 basis points of the mid.

This is the cost for a size that fits inside the best quote. A larger order pays more, because it reaches worse levels.

Procedure

Take the best bid and best ask from the same snapshot of one venue's book. Subtract to get the absolute spread. Divide by the mid-price and multiply by 10,000 for basis points, which is the form that can be compared between instruments.

Worked example

Given
  • P_bid = 100.00
  • P_ask = 100.10
Steps
  1. S = 0.10
  2. mid = 100.05
  3. s = 0.10 / 100.05 = 0.000999
  4. 0.000999 x 10,000
S = 0.10, s = 10.0 basis points

Interpretation

The absolute figure is the immediate round-trip cost for a size that fits at the quote. The relative figure is the same cost as a fraction of price, which is the only form in which two instruments can be compared.

Assumptions

  • Both quotes come from the same instant and the same venue. Quotes taken from different timestamps or different exchanges give a spread that was never tradable.
  • The mid-price is a reference convention, not a price at which anyone traded; some desks divide by the ask or by the last trade instead, which shifts the figure slightly.
  • Exchange, clearing and funding fees are additional costs and are not part of the spread.
Limits of the formula
  • It says nothing about depth: a one-tick spread for one unit and for a thousand units are not the same market.
  • Quotes are cancellable, so the measured spread can be gone by the time an order arrives.
  • In a fragmented market each venue has its own spread, and the consolidated best bid and offer may be unreachable in practice.

Commonly confused with

Slippage

The spread is the standing gap between quotes before you trade. Slippage is the difference between the price you expected and the price you got, which includes crossing the spread and then walking the book.
