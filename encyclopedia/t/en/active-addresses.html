Active Addresses

Simple definition

How many distinct addresses sent or received a transaction on a blockchain in a period. It is often read as a user count, which it is not.

Professional definition

The count of unique addresses appearing as sender or receiver in confirmed transactions over a chosen window. Used as a proxy for network usage, with the understanding that the mapping from addresses to people or businesses is unknown and not one-to-one.

Technical definition

A count of identifiers, not of agents. One entity can control millions of addresses, and best practice generates a fresh address per receipt, so the series rises with wallet hygiene alone. Exchanges, custodians and bridges concentrate many users behind few addresses, pulling the other way. Entity-adjusted series apply clustering heuristics to estimate agents, and those heuristics are estimates with error, not measurements.

Academic definition

The cardinality of the set of distinct addresses participating as inputs or outputs in confirmed transactions over a specified interval, used as an observable proxy for network participation.

Mechanism

A blockchain records addresses, never identities, so any user count built on it is an inference. Two opposite distortions act at once: privacy practice multiplies addresses per person upward, and custodial aggregation collapses many people into one address. Because neither is constant over time, even the direction of a change in the series can be an artefact.

In practice

Who uses it
On-chain analysts, data providers, protocol teams reporting adoption, and researchers studying network growth.
Why it matters
It is directly observable from the ledger, needs no self-reporting, and is one of the few activity series available for every public chain.
Limitations
Addresses are not users. The count reflects wallet software behaviour, custodial concentration and bot activity as much as it reflects adoption.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting active addresses as a user number.
  • Comparing the count between chains with different address models.
  • Ignoring that cheap transactions make the series easy to inflate.

Examples

numeric

A chain reports 1,000,000 active addresses in a day. After clustering heuristics group addresses by probable controller, the estimate falls to about 310,000 entities — roughly three addresses per estimated actor, and the estimate itself has error.

Given
  • Raw unique active addresses in the day = 1,000,000
  • Addresses attributed by heuristic to 12 exchange clusters = 420,000
  • Estimated distinct entities after clustering = 310,000
Steps
  1. Addresses per estimated entity = 1,000,000 / 310,000 = 3.2
  2. Share of raw count inside exchange clusters = 420,000 / 1,000,000 = 42%
1,000,000 addresses correspond to roughly 310,000 estimated entities

Illustrative figures. The clustering step is an estimate produced by heuristics, so the entity count is an inference and not a measurement.

case

A wallet update starts generating a new receiving address for every payment. Active addresses rise sharply over the following months with no new users, and a protocol team reports the rise as adoption growth.

Commonly confused with

Entity-Adjusted Metrics

Raw active addresses count identifiers on the ledger. Entity-adjusted series try to count controllers by clustering those identifiers. The second is an estimate built on heuristics, and the two series can move in opposite directions.

Claim type is contested because the metric is routinely presented as a user count and no source in the registry supports that interpretation. The clustering figures in the numeric example are illustrative, not measurements of any named network, and carry no as-of date. The Kurdish term is a descriptive compound of established words rather than a coinage, so it is marked recommended rather than proposed.
