Support and Resistance

Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Price areas where buying or selling has repeatedly shown up: support sits below, where falls have stopped, and resistance above, where rallies have stalled.

Professional definition

Horizontal or sloping price zones in which resting orders and the memory of earlier trading have previously absorbed order flow. Support is the zone beneath price where demand has met supply; resistance the zone above where supply has met demand. Zones, not lines.

Technical definition

Not a property of price but of the order book and of participant memory: clusters of limit orders, stop placements and heavily traded prices create regions where flow is absorbed. Breaks are therefore ordinary, and a level that gave way often reverses its role. Identification is retrospective — a level exists only after price has reacted to it at least twice — which makes any backtest acutely sensitive to the definition chosen in code.

Academic definition

Price regions at which historical order flow has repeatedly been absorbed, such that continued movement in the prevailing direction has required additional pressure.

Mechanism

Resting limit orders and stop levels cluster around round numbers, prior highs and lows, and prices where a lot of volume changed hands. When price reaches a cluster, the resting side absorbs incoming flow and movement slows. Once that cluster is consumed nothing is left to absorb, so a break often accelerates rather than drifting.

In practice

Who uses it
Discretionary traders placing entries, stops and targets; execution desks choosing passive price levels; risk managers sizing against a defined point of invalidation.
Why it matters
A defined zone turns a vague view into a testable plan: an entry, a place where the idea is proved wrong, and a measurable distance to the next zone.
Limitations
Zones are drawn by hand and two analysts rarely agree on the same one. Evidence that they predict reversals is weak; their dependable use is defining risk, not forecasting.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing a level as a thin line and expecting price to respect it to the tick.
  • Counting only the touches that held and forgetting the ones that broke.
  • Carrying a level found on one timeframe onto every other chart.

Examples

market

A price that capped three rallies over two months is resistance. If price later closes well above it and then returns to the same area, that area often holds as support: sellers who were trapped there are now the buyers.

numeric

Resistance sits in the 98 to 100 band. A trader buying a close at 101 with invalidation at 97 risks 4 points to reach the next zone near 109, a potential 8 points. The ratio is 2 to 1 before costs.

Given
  • resistance zone = 98 to 100
  • entry on close above = 101
  • invalidation below the zone = 97
  • next zone above = 109
Steps
  1. risk = 101 - 97 = 4
  2. reward to next zone = 109 - 101 = 8
  3. ratio = 8 / 4
risk-reward ratio = 2.0 before fees and slippage

The ratio is geometry, not probability. It says nothing about how often price reaches 109 before 97.

Commonly confused with

Order Block

A support zone is inferred from repeated reaction at an area. An order block is a specific candle-derived zone defined by a price-action method, with its own construction rules.
