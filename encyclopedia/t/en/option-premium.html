Option Premium

Simple definition

The price of the option itself: what the buyer pays and the seller receives for the contract. It is not the price of the underlying asset.

Professional definition

The traded price of an option, quoted per unit of underlying and paid per contract once multiplied by contract size. It splits into intrinsic value and time value, and it is the quantity a pricing model is calibrated against, not an output taken on faith.

Technical definition

A market price bounded by no-arbitrage: a European call cannot be worth more than the underlying nor less than the underlying minus the discounted strike, and a European put cannot exceed the discounted strike. Inside those bounds the premium reflects the strike, time to expiry, the volatility input, financing and any carry. On most venues it is paid in full at inception; some margin options futures-style instead, which changes the cash-flow profile without changing the payoff.

Academic definition

The transaction price of a contingent claim, constrained by static no-arbitrage bounds and decomposable into immediate exercise value and an extrinsic component.

Mechanism

The premium is the price of transferring an asymmetric payoff. The buyer pays once and can lose no more; the writer takes the cash and keeps an open-ended obligation, so the premium has to compensate both the expected payout and the risk of carrying it. Supply and demand for protection move it as much as any model does.

In practice

Who uses it
Buyers budgeting a hedge, writers pricing an obligation, and risk teams reporting the cash actually at stake in an options book.
Why it matters
It is the only number in the structure that is paid or received, so it defines the maximum loss of a long position and the compensation of a short one.
Limitations
Quoted premiums in thin option markets can sit inside very wide spreads, so the mid price may be a number no one would trade at.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting the premium per unit and forgetting the contract multiplier.
  • Comparing premiums across strikes or expiries as if they were one product.
  • Treating a received premium as realised income before expiry.

Examples

market

A quoted premium of 2.40 on a contract covering 100 units costs 240 to buy, and that 240 is the entire loss if the option expires worthless. The writer's 240 is received at once but earned only by surviving to expiry.

numeric

A European call with the underlying at 100, strike 95, six months to expiry and a 5% rate cannot trade below 100 minus 92.65, that is 7.35, nor above 100. A quote of 6 would be an arbitrage, not a bargain.

Given
  • S = 100
  • K = 95
  • r = 0.05 per year, continuously compounded
  • T = 0.5 years
  • European exercise, no dividend or carry, no fees
Steps
  1. K e^(-rT) = 95 x e^(-0.025) = 95 x 0.97531 = 92.654
  2. lower bound = max(100 - 92.654, 0) = 7.346
  3. upper bound = S = 100
the premium must lie between about 7.35 and 100

The bounds are arbitrage limits, not a valuation. A premium inside them can still be expensive or cheap relative to a model.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Discount the strike over the remaining life, subtract it from the underlying price and floor at zero: that is the lowest a European call can trade. The underlying price itself is the ceiling. Mirror the arithmetic for the put, whose ceiling is the discounted strike.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 100
  • K = 95
  • r = 0.05
  • T = 0.5
Steps
  1. e^(-0.025) = 0.97531
  2. 95 x 0.97531 = 92.654
  3. 100 - 92.654 = 7.346
C must be at least 7.35 and at most 100

Interpretation

These are the limits any premium must respect for there to be no risk-free profit. They are narrow enough to catch a bad quote and far too wide to tell you what an option is worth.

Assumptions

  • Stated for European-style options on an underlying that pays no dividend and has no carry. A dividend or carry yield q requires replacing S with S e^{-qT}.
  • Frictionless markets: no fees, no bid-ask spread, free short selling and borrowing at the stated risk-free rate. Real quotes deviate inside those costs without an arbitrage existing.
  • American-style options satisfy the same lower bounds and, for calls on a non-dividend underlying, are never worth exercising early, so the bounds coincide.
  • Where a venue margins options futures-style instead of charging the premium upfront, the economics of the payoff are unchanged but the cash flows and the default risk are not.
Limits of the formula
  • Bounds are not a price. Everything between them requires a model, and the model's volatility input is the contested part.
  • A premium is only as informative as the market that produced it: a stale mark or a one-sided quote in an illiquid chain carries no information about value.

Premium and Discount

In derivatives generally, premium often means a price trading above a reference such as spot or net asset value. An option premium is the price of the contract itself.
