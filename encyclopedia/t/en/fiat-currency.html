Fiat currency

Simple definition

Money that is not redeemable for gold or any commodity. Its value rests on law and on the belief that it will still be accepted tomorrow.

Professional definition

A currency whose value comes from legal designation and public confidence rather than convertibility into a commodity. The issuing authority controls the quantity, so purchasing power is an outcome of policy rather than of a physical stock.

Technical definition

An inconvertible liability of a monetary authority, accepted because law assigns it settlement functions and because the issuer manages supply against a stated objective, commonly an inflation target. Fiat status moves the anchoring problem from a commodity stock to institutional credibility: there is no redemption promise, only an expectation of continued discipline in issuance.

Academic definition

A non-convertible monetary instrument whose acceptability rests on statutory designation and institutional credibility rather than on any claim to an underlying commodity.

Mechanism

With no commodity anchor, the price level is set by the quantity of money against the demand to hold it. The issuer can always meet obligations denominated in its own currency, so nominal default risk is near zero while inflation risk is real: the binding constraint is credibility, not solvency.

In practice

Who uses it
Savers, importers and exporters, treasury and payroll teams, and anyone holding a balance in a currency they do not control.
Why it matters
It explains why a government can always pay in its own currency and still impose a loss on holders through inflation.
Limitations
The legal and monetary arrangements behind each fiat currency differ by country, so claims about convertibility, pegs and capital controls are jurisdiction-specific.

Common mistakes

  • Reading 'cannot default in its own currency' as 'cannot lose value'.
  • Assuming all fiat currencies carry comparable inflation risk.
  • Treating a hard peg as permanent.

Examples

market

A saver holding only domestic fiat deposits during a period of high inflation sees the balance grow in name while the trolley of goods it buys shrinks. The nominal statement looks fine; the real position does not.

numeric

A deposit pays 8% for the year while prices rise 20%. The balance is 8% larger in name, yet the exact real return is about minus 10% — the holder financed part of the issuer's spending without a formal tax.

Given
  • nominal rate n = 8% per year
  • inflation i = 20% per year
  • exact Fisher relation (1+n) = (1+r)(1+i)
Steps
  1. 1 + r = 1.08 / 1.20 = 0.90
  2. r = 0.90 - 1
r ≈ -10.0% real for the year

Illustrative rates. The common shortcut n - i would give -12%, which is the linear approximation, not the identity.

Commonly confused with

Legal tender

Fiat describes what backs a currency: nothing redeemable. Legal tender describes a legal power to discharge a debt. A currency can be fiat without every instrument in it being legal tender.
