Simple definition

Anything widely accepted in payment. Money works because other people will take it from you tomorrow, not because the thing itself is useful.

Professional definition

A social technology performing three functions: medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value. Most money in a modern economy is bank deposits — a claim on a bank — rather than notes issued by the central bank.

Technical definition

An instrument whose acceptability rests on the expectation that others will accept it, not on intrinsic use. Monetary aggregates rank instruments by liquidity: currency in circulation, then transaction deposits, then less liquid near-money. Because the three classic functions are separable, an asset can be a serviceable store of value and a poor medium of exchange at the same time.

Academic definition

A generally accepted instrument of settlement that simultaneously serves as medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value, whose acceptability derives from convention and legal framework rather than intrinsic utility.

Mechanism

Acceptance is self-reinforcing: each person takes money because everyone else does, so the equilibrium rests on confidence rather than on backing. That is why a currency can lose its monetary role long before it is formally withdrawn, and why a central bank spends so much effort defending expectations about future purchasing power.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone who prices, saves or settles: households, firms, treasury teams, and traders choosing the unit in which performance is measured.
Why it matters
The unit you measure in decides whether you have actually gained. A portfolio up in a weakening currency can be flat in purchasing power.
Limitations
The three functions can diverge, so no single asset is 'the' money; the useful question is which function you need right now.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a bank deposit as identical to central-bank cash.
  • Measuring returns only in a weakening home currency.
  • Confusing money with wealth.

Examples

everyday

A shopkeeper takes a banknote for bread not because paper is useful, but because the wholesaler will take the same note tomorrow. Remove that expectation and the note stops being money long before any authority withdraws it.

numeric

Hold 1,000 units in cash for a year while prices rise 10%. The unit of account is untouched — still 1,000 — but as a store of value the holding now buys what about 909 bought a year earlier.

Given
  • nominal holding = 1,000 units
  • inflation over the year = 10%
  • cash pays no interest
Steps
  1. real value in last year's prices = 1,000 / 1.10
≈ 909 units of last year's purchasing power

The nominal figure is unchanged; only the store-of-value function degrades. Illustrative inflation rate, not a market observation.

Commonly confused with

Money supply

Money is the concept and its functions. Money supply is a measured stock of specified instruments at a point in time, and the specification is a convention.
