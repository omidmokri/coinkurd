Licensing and Registration

Simple definition

Official permission to run a financial business. Some activities need a full licence that is granted after review; others only need the firm to register and report.

Professional definition

The permission layer of the regulatory perimeter. Licensing involves prior assessment of capital, governance, fitness of management and systems, with continuing supervision. Registration is lighter: notification and recurring obligations without the same prior assessment. Which applies depends on the activity performed, not on how the firm describes itself.

Technical definition

Regimes are activity-based, so one firm can need several permissions and a single business can be licensed in one state, registered in a second and outside scope in a third. Permissions are rarely portable: a licence grants rights within its granting jurisdiction and under any mutual-recognition arrangement, and cross-border service to residents elsewhere usually requires local authorisation. Supervision continues after grant, and a permission can be restricted or withdrawn.

Academic definition

The authorisation mechanism through which a competent authority admits an entity to a regulated activity, conditioned on prudential, governance and conduct requirements and subject to continuing supervision.

Mechanism

Permission exists because supervision needs a counterparty it can bind. An authority cannot impose capital, conduct or reporting duties on an undefined set of firms, so it defines activities, requires permission to perform them, and gains the power to inspect, restrict and withdraw. The licence is less a certificate of quality than the hook by which continuing obligations attach.

In practice

Who uses it
Founders and legal counsel choosing a domicile, compliance and governance teams, supervisors, and users checking whether a venue is authorised.
Why it matters
It determines what a firm may lawfully offer, to whom, and what recourse a customer has if the firm fails.
Limitations
A permission is evidence of admission to supervision, not of solvency, honesty or good execution. Registers differ, and a firm may be authorised for one activity and not another.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a licence as a regulator's endorsement of the product or its returns.
  • Assuming a permission in one jurisdiction allows service to residents of another.
  • Confusing a company registration with a financial-services authorisation.

Examples

regulatory

A platform holds a payment permission and adds margined trading. The new activity sits under a different regime, so the existing permission covers none of it, and the firm is unauthorised for the part that carries most of the risk.

numeric

A firm serving customers in four countries needs permissions in three and is out of scope in one. If each application takes nine months and two run in parallel, market entry is roughly eighteen months, not nine.

Given
  • Permissions required = 3
  • Processing time per application = 9 months (illustrative)
  • Two applications can be progressed in parallel, the third only after the first completes
Steps
  1. Applications 1 and 2 in parallel: 9 months
  2. Application 3 begins at month 9 and ends at month 18
  3. Total elapsed = 18 months
About 18 months to full coverage of three jurisdictions

The nine-month figure is invented for the arithmetic. Real processing times differ greatly by authority and regime and are not quoted from any source here.

JURISDICTION WARNING: rules differ by country and change. This entry describes the general structure of authorisation regimes as at 5 November 2026 and names no capital requirement, application procedure, processing time or authority, because none could be cited from the closed source registry. Whether an activity needs a licence, a registration or nothing at all is a question of each jurisdiction's own law and of how that law classifies the specific activity; the EU regime is cited only as one example of an authorisation framework. The processing times in the numeric example are invented for the arithmetic. Nothing here is legal advice.
