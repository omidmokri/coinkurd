Simple definition

A model of how people actually choose between risky options. Outcomes are judged as gains or losses from a starting point, and small probabilities get more weight than they deserve.

Professional definition

Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 descriptive alternative to expected-utility theory. Outcomes enter as deviations from a reference point rather than as levels of wealth; the value function is concave over gains, convex over losses and steeper on the loss side; and probabilities are replaced by decision weights that overweight small probabilities.

Technical definition

Choice is modelled in two stages. An editing phase frames the prospect and fixes the reference point; an evaluation phase sums value against decision weight. Three properties carry the predictions: reference dependence, so the same final wealth is valued differently depending on the frame; diminishing sensitivity, giving concavity in gains and convexity in losses and hence risk-seeking over losses; and non-linear probability weighting, which overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate ones. Loss aversion is one component of the theory, not a synonym for it.

Academic definition

A descriptive theory of decision under risk in which prospects are evaluated by summing a reference-dependent value function over outcomes weighted by a non-linear transformation of their probabilities.

Mechanism

The theory replaces two assumptions of expected utility. Wealth levels become deviations from a reference point, so framing changes the choice without changing the money; and stated probabilities become decision weights, so a one percent chance carries more than one percent of the weight. Together these produce the four-fold pattern: risk aversion over likely gains and unlikely losses, risk seeking over unlikely gains and likely losses.

In practice

Who uses it
Researchers modelling choice, and practitioners who want a principled account of why framing an exit changes whether it is taken.
Why it matters
It supplies a single structure from which loss aversion, the disposition effect and the appeal of lottery-like payoffs all follow, instead of three separate observations.
Limitations
It is descriptive, not normative: it models how choices are made, not how they should be. It also leaves the reference point unexplained, and that is exactly where most market applications do their work.

Common mistakes

  • Using prospect theory and loss aversion as interchangeable terms.
  • Quoting a single loss-aversion coefficient as though the theory fixed one.
  • Treating it as a prediction about prices rather than about choices.

Examples

numeric

A choice between a certain loss of 90 and a ninety percent chance of losing 100. Expected values are equal, yet most people take the gamble, which is risk seeking in the loss region.

Given
  • option A: lose 90 with certainty
  • option B: lose 100 with probability 0.9, lose nothing with probability 0.1
  • reference point at current wealth, so both options lie in the loss region
Steps
  1. EV(A) = -90
  2. EV(B) = 0.9 x (-100) = -90
  3. under a convex value function over losses, v(-90) < 0.9 x v(-100), so B is preferred
equal expected values, systematic preference for the gamble

Probability weighting is omitted to isolate the curvature. In the full model the weight on 0.9 is also transformed, which shifts the comparison without changing its direction here.

everyday

A lottery ticket and an insurance policy are bought by the same person on the same day. Overweighting a small probability explains both: the tiny chance of winning and the tiny chance of ruin each loom larger than their odds.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Fix the reference point and restate every outcome as a gain or a loss from it. Apply the value function to each deviation, transform each probability into a decision weight, multiply and sum. Compare prospects by V. The two curvature conditions and the steeper loss limb are the theory's content; the specific functional forms are not fixed by the 1979 paper.

Worked example

Given
  • prospect: gain 100 with probability 0.05, otherwise nothing
  • reference point at current wealth
  • illustrative decision weight pi(0.05) = 0.13
  • v treated as locally linear in this small range so that v(100) = 100
Steps
  1. expected value = 0.05 x 100 = 5
  2. prospect value V = 0.13 x 100 = 13
  3. the gap arises entirely from the weighting of the small probability
V = 13 against an expected value of 5

Interpretation

V is not money and not a forecast; it is an index for ranking choices. A V above the expected value shows the small probability has been overweighted, which is the modelled reason lottery-like payoffs attract and cheap insurance sells.

Assumptions

  • The reference point is taken as given. The theory does not derive it, and in markets it may be the entry price, a round number, a peak or an expectation, which changes the prediction.
  • The 1979 formulation weights individual probabilities directly and is stated for simple prospects with few outcomes. Known difficulties with that form were addressed by a later cumulative version, which is outside this project's source registry and is therefore not cited here.
  • The decision weights do not sum to one, so V is not an expectation and must not be read as one.
Limits of the formula
  • No specific functional form or loss-aversion coefficient is fixed by the 1979 paper; parameter values quoted elsewhere come from later estimation and vary by study and population.
  • The theory describes choices in framed decision problems with stated probabilities. Market decisions have neither stated probabilities nor a single frame, so application to a trade is an extrapolation rather than a result.

Commonly confused with

Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is one component of prospect theory: the steeper slope on the loss side. The theory also contains reference dependence, diminishing sensitivity and probability weighting, each of which predicts behaviour loss aversion alone does not.
