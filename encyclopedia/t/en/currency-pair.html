Foundation

Simple definition

Two currencies quoted against each other, like EUR/USD. The price says how much of the second currency one unit of the first costs.

Professional definition

A quotation convention in which the first-named currency is the base and the second is the quote: the price is the number of quote units per one base unit. Every price is a ratio, so a move can come from either side, and a pair without a direct market is derived as a cross through a common currency.

Technical definition

An exchange rate is a relative price with no absolute level, so the identity price(A/B) = 1 / price(B/A) always holds up to the bid-ask spread. Market convention, not logic, fixes which currency is quoted as the base, and the convention differs by pair. Cross rates multiply two quotes through a shared leg, and in liquid markets arbitrage keeps the triangle consistent within transaction costs.

Academic definition

An ordered pair of currencies defining an exchange rate as the quantity of the quote currency exchangeable for one unit of the base currency.

Mechanism

Because the quote is a ratio, there is no such thing as a currency rising on its own: it can only rise against something. A pair can move because the base strengthened, because the quote weakened, or both, which is why single-pair analysis often misattributes the cause.

In practice

Who uses it
Importers and exporters, FX traders, treasury hedging teams, and anyone holding assets priced in a currency other than their own.
Why it matters
It is the unit of all cross-border pricing, and the direction convention decides whether a rising number is good or bad for you.
Limitations
A pair quote says nothing about which currency moved; judging that needs a basket or index view across several pairs.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a rising pair as the base strengthening without checking the other side.
  • Inverting a quote without accounting for bid and ask.
  • Assuming the base currency is the one you hold.

Examples

market

An exporter invoicing in a foreign currency and paying costs at home is exposed to the pair, not to one currency. If the home currency strengthens, revenue converts into less at home even with unchanged sales.

numeric

If A/B trades at 1.25, then B/A is 0.80. Given B/C at 150, the cross A/C is 187.5. In a real market each leg has a spread, so the cross is a range, not a point.

Given
  • A/B = 1.25 (units of B per A)
  • B/C = 150 (units of C per B)
  • spreads ignored
Steps
  1. B/A = 1 / 1.25 = 0.80
  2. A/C = 1.25 x 150
B/A = 0.80; A/C = 187.5

Illustrative, non-market figures using placeholder currency letters. With spreads, inversion and crossing both cost money.

Mathematical foundation

P_{A/B} = \frac{1}{P_{B/A}}, \qquad P_{A/C} = P_{A/B} \times P_{B/C}

Variables

Procedure

Identify which currency is the base in each quote before doing anything. To invert, take the reciprocal; to cross, chain two quotes so that the shared currency cancels. In live markets use bid for one direction and ask for the other.

Worked example

Given
  • P_{A/B} = 1.25
  • P_{B/C} = 150
Steps
  1. 1 / 1.25 = 0.80
  2. 1.25 x 150
P_{B/A} = 0.80; P_{A/C} = 187.5

Interpretation

The number is always a price of one currency in units of another. Its level means nothing in isolation: only the change, and which side caused it, carries information.

Assumptions

  • Quoting direction follows market convention, which differs by pair; the identity only holds once the base and quote are correctly identified.
  • The reciprocal and cross identities are exact only at a single mid price. With bid-ask spreads, both operations cost money and the triangle holds only within transaction costs.
  • Cross rates assume both legs are quoted at the same moment; stale quotes break the arithmetic.
Limits of the formula
  • A pair cannot say which of its two currencies moved, so attribution requires looking at several pairs or an index.
  • In pegged or capital-controlled regimes an official quote may not be the rate at which transactions actually clear.

Commonly confused with

Dollar Index

A pair is one currency against one other. A currency index is one currency against a weighted basket, which is what you need to say whether that currency itself moved.
