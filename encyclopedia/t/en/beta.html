Beta

Simple definition

How strongly an asset tends to move when the market moves. A beta of one means it moves with the market, two means twice as hard, zero means no systematic link.

Professional definition

The slope coefficient when an asset's excess return is regressed on the market's excess return. Equivalently the covariance of asset and market returns divided by the market's variance. It prices only the risk that cannot be diversified away.

Technical definition

In the market-model regression of asset excess return on market excess return, beta is the slope. It equals the correlation between the two series times the ratio of their standard deviations, so a high-volatility asset with weak correlation can have a modest beta. Beta is an estimate conditional on the index chosen as the market proxy, the return frequency and the estimation window; changing any one of these changes the number, and none of them is dictated by theory.

Academic definition

The sensitivity of an asset's excess return to the excess return of the market portfolio, equal to the ratio of their covariance to the market's variance, and the sole priced risk measure in the single-factor capital asset pricing model.

Mechanism

Diversification removes the part of an asset's variance that is unique to it, but cannot remove the part shared with every other asset. Beta measures exactly that shared part, as the slope of the line that best fits asset returns against market returns. Because only non-diversifiable risk requires compensation in equilibrium, beta is what a single-factor model prices.

In practice

Who uses it
Equity analysts computing a cost of capital, portfolio managers controlling market exposure, and anyone sizing a hedge against an index.
Why it matters
It converts a position into units of market exposure, which is what a hedge ratio, a required return and a risk budget all need.
Limitations
It is unstable: estimates drift with the window, the frequency and the choice of index, and a beta fitted in calm markets understates exposure in a crisis.

Common mistakes

  • Treating beta as total risk rather than the market-linked part.
  • Comparing betas estimated against different indices.
  • Using a long-window beta for a position held days.

Examples

numeric

Covariance of asset and market returns is 0.0018 and the market's variance is 0.0012, so beta is 1.5. The asset has carried half again the market's systematic exposure over that sample.

Given
  • Cov(r_i, r_m) = 0.0018
  • Var(r_m) = 0.0012
  • returns measured at one frequency on one index
Steps
  1. beta = 0.0018 / 0.0012 = 1.5
beta = 1.5

Beta can also be built from correlation and volatilities: correlation 0.8 with asset volatility 30% and market volatility 20% gives beta 1.2 and regression R-squared 0.64. Both routes are exact; they simply use different inputs.

pitfall

A volatile small-cap shows annual volatility of 60% against the index's 20% but correlation of only 0.2. Beta is 0.6, below one, even though the position is three times as volatile as the market.

Mathematical foundation

R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i\left(R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}\right) + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \qquad \beta_i = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\mathrm{Var}(R_m)} = \rho_{i,m}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}

Variables

\beta_iSensitivity of asset i to the market factorratio (dimensionless)
R_{m,t}Market proxy return in period t% per period
R_{f,t}Risk-free rate for the same period% per period
\rho_{i,m}Correlation between asset and market returnsratio
\sigma_i, \sigma_mStandard deviations of asset and market returns% per period
\varepsilon_{i,t}Residual: the part of the asset's excess return the market does not explain% per period

Procedure

Choose the market proxy, the return frequency and the estimation window, and record all three. Subtract the matching risk-free rate from both return series. Regress the asset's excess return on the market's excess return by least squares; the slope is beta. Equivalently divide the covariance by the market's variance — the two routes give the same number.

Worked example

Given
  • Cov(r_i, r_m) = 0.0018 and Var(r_m) = 0.0012
  • second case: rho = 0.8, sigma_i = 30%, sigma_m = 20%
Steps
  1. case A: beta = 0.0018 / 0.0012 = 1.5
  2. case B: beta = 0.8 * (0.30 / 0.20) = 0.8 * 1.5 = 1.2
  3. case B R-squared = rho^2 = 0.64, so 36% of the asset's variance is unexplained by the market
beta = 1.5 in case A; beta = 1.2 with R-squared 0.64 in case B

Interpretation

Beta one means market-like systematic exposure; above one means amplified, below one damped, negative means it tends to move against the market. It describes the market-linked component only, with the residual variance left over entirely.

Assumptions

  • Beta is defined relative to a chosen market proxy; no index is 'the market', so the number is model-relative.
  • The regression assumes a linear relation and a stable coefficient over the window; both are approximations.
  • Excess returns over a matching risk-free rate are the textbook inputs, but many data vendors regress raw returns, which shifts the intercept and slightly the slope.
Limits of the formula
  • It captures only linear comovement and ignores dependence that appears only in extreme moves.
  • Estimates are noisy and mean-revert, which is why practitioners often shrink a raw beta towards one.
  • A low beta is not low risk: it can coexist with very high total volatility, as the worked example shows.

Commonly confused with

Correlation

Correlation is scaled to lie between minus one and one and says nothing about magnitude. Beta is correlation multiplied by the ratio of volatilities, so it carries scale as well as direction.

Commonly confused with

Volatility

Volatility is total variability. Beta is only the slice of it explained by the market, so the two can point in opposite directions for the same asset.
