Drawdown

Simple definition

How far an account or a price has fallen from its highest point, as a percentage of that high. A fall from 100 to 60 is a drawdown of 40 percent.

Professional definition

The percentage decline from a running peak to a subsequent trough in an equity or price series. Maximum drawdown is the largest such decline over a period, and it is quoted alongside the time spent below the old peak, because depth and duration are separate pains.

Technical definition

A path-dependent statistic, not a distributional one: two series with identical mean and standard deviation can have very different maximum drawdowns because order of returns matters. Its recovery requirement is asymmetric, since the gain is measured on the reduced capital base, and the asymmetry grows without bound as the drawdown approaches total loss.

Academic definition

The relative decline of a cumulative value process from its running maximum, whose supremum over an interval defines maximum drawdown and whose recovery requirement is a convex function of depth.

Mechanism

The asymmetry is pure arithmetic. A loss is taken on the old, larger capital base; the recovery must be earned on the new, smaller one. Lose half and the remaining half must double, which is why drawdown tolerance, not return, usually decides whether a strategy survives long enough to show its edge.

In practice

Who uses it
Fund allocators, risk committees, strategy developers reading a backtest, and any trader with a monthly loss limit.
Why it matters
It describes the experience of holding the strategy, which is what determines whether capital stays in place long enough for the average to arrive.
Limitations
It is one realised path. A backtest's maximum drawdown is a sample minimum, and the live worst case is normally deeper than anything in the sample.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a backtest maximum drawdown as a ceiling.
  • Comparing drawdowns measured on different sampling frequencies.
  • Quoting depth without the time spent below the peak.

Examples

market

Two strategies return the same amount over five years. One does it in a smooth line, the other after a fall of 60 percent in the second year. Most capital leaves the second one before the fifth year arrives.

numeric

Equity peaks at 100,000 and troughs at 50,000. The drawdown is 50 percent. Recovering to the old peak needs 50,000 of gain on a 50,000 base, which is 100 percent. At a 20 percent drawdown the requirement is only 25 percent.

Given
  • running peak P = 100,000
  • trough V = 50,000
  • no deposits or withdrawals in the period
Steps
  1. DD = (100,000 - 50,000) / 100,000 = 50,000 / 100,000 = 0.50
  2. recovery gain g = DD / (1 - DD) = 0.50 / 0.50 = 1.00
  3. direct check: needed gain = (100,000 - 50,000) / 50,000 = 1.00
  4. for DD = 0.20: g = 0.20 / 0.80 = 0.25
DD = 50 percent, requiring a 100 percent gain to recover; a 20 percent drawdown requires 25 percent

Deposits during the drawdown break the comparison: they restore equity without the strategy earning anything, so a drawdown series must be computed on returns, not on balances.

Mathematical foundation

DD_t = \frac{P_t - V_t}{P_t}, \qquad P_t = \max_{s \le t} W_s, \qquad g = \frac{DD}{1 - DD}

Variables

DD_tDrawdown at time t, as a fraction of the running peakfraction
W_sEquity or price level at time saccount currency or price
P_tRunning peak: the highest level reached up to time taccount currency or price
V_tCurrent level, or the trough of the episode being measuredaccount currency or price
gGain on the reduced base required to return to the old peakfraction

Procedure

Track the running maximum of the equity curve. At each point, subtract the current level from that maximum and divide by the maximum. The largest value over the window is the maximum drawdown. To get the recovery requirement, divide the drawdown by one minus the drawdown.

Worked example

Given
  • peak P = 100,000
  • trough V = 50,000
Steps
  1. P - V = 100,000 - 50,000 = 50,000
  2. DD = 50,000 / 100,000 = 0.50, i.e. 50 percent
  3. g = 0.50 / (1 - 0.50) = 0.50 / 0.50 = 1.00, i.e. 100 percent
  4. independent check: 50,000 x (1 + 1.00) = 100,000, back at the peak
DD = 50 percent and the required recovery gain is 100 percent

Interpretation

Drawdown measures depth from a high-water mark and the recovery term translates that depth into the work needed to undo it. The two are not symmetric, and the gap widens fast: 20 percent needs 25, 50 percent needs 100, 80 percent needs 400.

Assumptions

  • The series is a clean equity or price path with no deposits, withdrawals or fee rebates; cash flows must be removed by working in returns.
  • Measured on a fixed sampling frequency. Daily closes understate intraday drawdowns, and an end-of-month series understates both.
  • The recovery term assumes the capital base is simply the troughed equity, with no further contributions.
Limits of the formula
  • Maximum drawdown is the worst case of a single observed path, so it has no upper-bound interpretation and grows with sample length.
  • It says nothing about the shape of the loss: a slow grind and a one-day crash of the same depth report the same number.

Commonly confused with

Value at Risk

A drawdown is a realised fall from a peak along one path. Value at risk is a forward-looking quantile of loss over a fixed horizon. One is history, the other a modelled forecast.
