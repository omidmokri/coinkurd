Simple definition

The rules that decide how many units of a token exist, who gets them, when they arrive, and what makes anyone want to hold them. The design, not the chart.

Professional definition

The economic design of a token: initial allocation, emission schedule, lock and vesting terms, fee and burn mechanics, staking or escrow incentives, and governance over all of the above. The set of rules together determines future float, who bears dilution, and whether holding is rewarded by anything other than price.

Technical definition

A mechanism-design problem with two separable halves: supply, which is largely deterministic and readable from contracts and schedules, and demand, which is not. Most published analysis is really supply analysis dressed as valuation, because allocations, unlock cliffs and emission curves can be computed exactly while sources of recurring demand usually cannot. Honest work states which claims are read from a schedule and which are assumptions about behaviour.

Academic definition

The study of incentive and supply mechanisms encoded in a crypto-asset's issuance, distribution and governance rules, and of their implications for distribution of claims, dilution, and participant behaviour.

Mechanism

Supply rules are credible because code executes them; demand assumptions are not, because nothing enforces them. That asymmetry is why a design can guarantee that tokens arrive on a date and guarantee nothing about who will buy them. Well-built designs therefore try to tie issuance to something that generates its own demand — fees, security, access — rather than paying out of the token itself and hoping new buyers appear.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol designers, venture and treasury allocators, listing committees, and anyone reading a project's distribution table before taking a position.
Why it matters
It is where future selling pressure, holder incentives and the distribution of control are actually decided, long before any of it shows up in price.
Limitations
The supply side is computable and the demand side is not, so a tokenomics review can rule designs out far more reliably than it can rule them in.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a long vesting schedule as alignment without checking what unlocks first.
  • Counting staking yield paid in the same token as income.
  • Taking an allocation chart as evidence of actual holder distribution.

Examples

numeric

A design allocates 20 percent to the team: a 12-month cliff, then linear release over 36 more months. At the cliff, a quarter of that tranche unlocks at once — 5 percent of total supply in one day.

Given
  • team allocation = 20 percent of total supply
  • cliff = 12 months with no release before it
  • after the cliff, linear release over 36 further months
  • total vesting horizon = 48 months
Steps
  1. fraction of the tranche earned by the cliff date = 12 / 48 = 0.25
  2. released at the cliff = 0.25 x 20 percent of supply = 5 percent
  3. remaining 15 percent of supply releases evenly over 36 months, about 0.42 percent per month
5 percent of total supply becomes transferable on a single date, then roughly 0.42 percent per month

Hypothetical terms. The arithmetic depends entirely on how the cliff is worded in the contract: a cliff that does not count towards the linear period gives a different first-day figure, which is why the contract text matters more than the headline percentage.

crypto

A protocol paying liquidity providers in its own newly issued token funds those rewards by diluting every holder. Whether that is investment or transfer depends on whether the liquidity stays once the rewards stop.

Commonly confused with

Relative Valuation

Tokenomics describes the rules that generate supply and incentives, and most of it can be read from documents. Relative valuation compares an asset with others through multiples, which needs assumptions about demand that no token document supplies.

Editorial status

Draftindustry-conventionv1.02026-11-05

Written, not yet reviewed.

The term itself is industry coinage with no settled academic definition; the entry describes the practice rather than asserting a standard. The numeric example deliberately shows two readings of the same cliff wording to make the ambiguity visible, and all its terms are hypothetical. The claim that supply is computable while demand is not is an editorial framing of the asymmetry, grounded in the cited disclosure material rather than in any empirical study. Both Persian and Kurdish retain the transliterated English word, as practitioners do.
