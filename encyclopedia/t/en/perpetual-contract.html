Perpetual Contract

Simple definition

A derivative that tracks an asset's price but never expires. Instead of a settlement date, longs and shorts pay each other a periodic funding payment.

Professional definition

A cash-settled, exchange-traded contract with no delivery date, held open indefinitely on margin. A periodic funding payment between longs and shorts, set from the gap between the contract's price and a spot index, is the mechanism that keeps the two tethered.

Technical definition

A leveraged, cash-settled swap on a spot index with no expiry, so the convergence that delivery imposes on a futures contract is replaced by a recurring cash flow. When the contract trades above the index, longs pay shorts, which makes a persistent premium expensive to hold and pulls the price back. On most venues positions are marked against an index rather than the last trade, to limit liquidation by wick.

Academic definition

A perpetual derivative on a reference index that substitutes a periodic bilateral transfer between long and short holders for a fixed settlement date, maintaining approximate convergence to the index without delivery.

Mechanism

Without an expiry there is no delivery to force the contract back to spot, so the venue builds the pull in as a cost: whichever side is crowded pays the other. Holding a premium position therefore carries a cost that rises with the premium, and arbitrageurs who are long spot and short the contract collect it.

In practice

Who uses it
Crypto traders wanting continuous leveraged exposure, basis desks harvesting funding, and market makers hedging inventory.
Why it matters
No expiry means no roll, liquidity concentrates in one instrument, and short exposure is available without borrowing the asset.
Limitations
Access is jurisdiction-dependent and restricted for retail clients in several places, and venue rules on funding, mark price, margin and liquidation differ substantially.

Common mistakes

  • Holding a funding-negative position for weeks and ignoring the cumulative cost.
  • Confusing the contract's mark price with the last traded price.
  • Assuming the funding formula and interval are the same on every venue.

Examples

market

Leveraged longs crowd in and the contract trades above its index. Funding turns positive, so longs pay shorts every interval. The cost pressures weak holders out, and if price slips the same crowding becomes a liquidation cascade.

numeric

Funding is 0.01% per 8-hour interval, three intervals a day. On a notional of 10,000 held 30 days that is 0.9% of notional, or 90. Against 1,000 of margin at ten times leverage, it is 9% of the margin posted.

Given
  • funding rate = +0.01% per interval
  • three intervals per day
  • holding period = 30 days
  • notional = 10,000, initial margin = 1,000 (ten times leverage)
Steps
  1. daily cost = 0.01% x 3 = 0.03% of notional
  2. over 30 days = 0.03% x 30 = 0.9% of notional = 90
  3. as a share of margin = 90 / 1,000 = 9%
  4. simple annualised = 0.03% x 365 = 10.95% of notional
90 paid over 30 days, which is 9% of the margin posted

The 8-hour interval and three payments a day are a common convention, not a standard. Interval length, caps and the formula differ by venue, and a constant rate for 30 days is an assumption made for the arithmetic.

Mathematical foundation

\text{Funding}_i = N_i \times f, \qquad f > 0 \Rightarrow \text{longs pay shorts}

Variables

\text{Funding}_iAmount paid or received by position i at one funding timestampquote currency
N_iNotional value of position i at that timestamp, usually measured at the mark or index pricequote currency
fThe venue's funding rate for that interval, signedfraction per interval

Procedure

At each funding timestamp the venue publishes a signed rate for the interval, computed from the premium of the contract over its index plus an interest-rate component. Every position open at the stamp is debited or credited its notional times that rate, longs paying shorts when the rate is positive and the reverse when it is negative.

Worked example

  • position is long

  2. f is positive, so the long pays
the long pays 1.00 and the matched short receives 1.00 at that timestamp

Interpretation

Funding is a transfer between traders rather than a fee to the venue on most exchanges. Because it is charged on notional and not on margin, leverage multiplies its effect on the capital actually posted.

Assumptions

  • Only positions open at the funding timestamp pay or receive. On most venues a position opened and closed between stamps pays nothing, so the published rate overstates the cost borne by short-horizon traders.
  • Notional is normally measured at the mark or index price at the timestamp, not at the entry price. Which price a venue uses changes the amount.
  • The payment is between traders on most venues, but whether the exchange takes a share, and whether funding accrues continuously rather than at discrete stamps, is venue-specific.
  • There is no universal funding formula or interval. The rate f is defined by each exchange's own specification; see the funding-rate entry for the general structure and the ways venues differ.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity above is only the payment arithmetic. It says nothing about how f is set, which is where almost all the variation between venues lives.
  • Funding tethers the contract to its index on average over time; it does not prevent large short-lived dislocations, and during one the mark price can move far from both the index and the last trade.

Flagged by the editor as uncertain on a linguistic, mathematical or regulatory point.

Every quantitative detail of a perpetual contract -- funding interval and formula, mark price construction, margin tiers and liquidation logic -- is set by the individual exchange and is not standardised. The 8-hour interval used in the numeric example is a common convention only. Availability to retail clients is jurisdiction-dependent and changes; no jurisdiction's rules are stated here.
