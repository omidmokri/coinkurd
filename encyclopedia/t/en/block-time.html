Simple definition

How long a new block usually takes to appear. Bitcoin aims at roughly ten minutes; Ethereum schedules one block per twelve-second slot.

Professional definition

The average interval between consecutive blocks. Under proof of work it is an emergent statistic that the difficulty rule steers toward a target. Under slot-based proof of stake it is a scheduled slot length, so blocks arrive on a clock rather than from a random search.

Technical definition

Under proof of work, block discovery is a memoryless search, so intervals are approximately exponential around the target mean: gaps of one minute or of an hour are both ordinary, and only long averages approach the target. Difficulty retargeting corrects the mean, not the variance. Slot-based proof of stake replaces the search with a schedule, so the interval is fixed and residual variance comes from missed proposals.

Academic definition

The expected inter-arrival time of blocks in a chain, targeted by a difficulty rule under proof-of-work consensus and fixed by slot scheduling under slot-based proof-of-stake consensus.

Mechanism

A target interval trades latency against fork rate. Shorter intervals give faster inclusion but raise the chance that two valid blocks appear before either has propagated, so more work is orphaned and each block carries less weight. The target is therefore a propagation margin, not a performance figure.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol designers, exchange and custody teams setting confirmation policies, bot developers timing on-chain actions, and on-chain analysts.
Why it matters
It fixes how long inclusion takes, how deep a given wait is in blocks, and how quickly an arbitrage or liquidation window closes.
Limitations
It is an average over a random process on proof-of-work chains, so no individual wait can be promised from it.

Common mistakes

  • Promising a customer a deposit time from the target interval.
  • Treating an unusually long gap as evidence of an attack.
  • Comparing confirmation counts across chains with different intervals.

Examples

crypto

A proof-of-work chain can go forty minutes with no block and then produce three in two minutes. Nothing broke: the search has no memory, so the gaps are exponentially distributed around the target.

numeric

A retarget window of 2,016 blocks closes after 12 days instead of the intended 14. The realised average is 8.57 minutes per block, not 10, which is exactly the signal the difficulty rule acts on.

Given
  • N = 2,016 blocks
  • elapsed time = 12 days = 17,280 minutes
Steps
  1. average interval = 17,280 / 2,016
  2. = 8.571 minutes
8.57 minutes per block against a 10-minute target

Hypothetical window. The same arithmetic is the input to Bitcoin's difficulty retarget.

Mathematical foundation

\bar{T} = \frac{t_N - t_0}{N}

Variables

\bar{T}Realised average block intervalseconds or minutes
t_0, t_NTimestamps of the first and last block of the windowtime
NNumber of block intervals in the windowcount

Procedure

Take the timestamps of the first and last block of a window, subtract, and divide by the number of intervals between them, which is one less than the number of blocks.

Worked example

Given
  • N = 2,016 intervals
  • t_N - t_0 = 17,280 minutes
Steps
  1. 17,280 / 2,016 = 8.571
average interval = 8.57 minutes

Interpretation

A realised average below the target means blocks arrived too fast over that window, so the protocol must make the search harder; above target means the opposite. The average says nothing about the next single interval.

Assumptions

  • Block timestamps are taken as given; protocols allow producers limited freedom in setting them, so short-window averages can be slightly distorted.
  • Intervals are measured on one canonical chain, excluding orphaned blocks.
Limits of the formula
  • The mean is not the distribution: on proof-of-work chains the spread of individual intervals is wide and does not shrink as the mean is corrected.
  • On slot-based chains the formula measures missed slots rather than search luck.

Commonly confused with

Finality

Block time says how often blocks appear. Finality says when what is in them stops being reversible. A fast chain can still take longer to be final.

Editorial status

Draftconsensusv1.02026-11-05

Written, not yet reviewed.

The ten-minute and twelve-second figures are protocol parameters of Bitcoin and Ethereum respectively and can be changed by their communities; they are cited to the protocol documents, not presented as laws. The propagation-margin rationale for a ten-minute target is the standard engineering explanation rather than a claim made in the Bitcoin paper itself.
