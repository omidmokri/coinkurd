Bitcoin Power Law

Simple definition

A model that draws bitcoin's fair value as a rising curve based only on how long the network has existed. With both chart axes stretched logarithmically, that curve looks like a straight line.

Professional definition

A fitted model in which price is a power function of time since the genesis block: price proportional to network age raised to an exponent. Parameters come from a least-squares fit on log-log axes, where a power law appears as a straight line, and bands drawn around the fit are presented as a valuation corridor.

Technical definition

Taking logs turns a power law into a linear relation between log price and log age, so the fit is an ordinary regression. Any monotonically rising series gives a high coefficient of determination in these coordinates, because logging compresses several orders of magnitude on both axes; goodness of fit therefore carries almost no information about the specification. No mechanism links elapsed time to value, and both parameters are re-estimated as new data arrives.

Academic definition

A time-parameterised power-law specification for bitcoin price, estimated by linear regression in double-logarithmic coordinates, whose apparent explanatory power derives largely from the monotone scaling of both variables rather than from an identified economic mechanism.

Mechanism

There is no mechanism. The model relates price to the passage of time, so it cannot say why value should follow age; the usual narratives — adoption curves, diffusion, rising difficulty — are attached after the fit rather than derived from it. What the regression captures is that price and network age both grew over the same sample, which is enough to make a straight line appear in log-log coordinates.

In practice

Who uses it
Long-horizon bitcoin analysts, chart publishers, and anyone studying how valuation models are fitted and then misread.
Why it matters
It supplies one long-run reference line that is easy to plot and easy to communicate, and it is a clean teaching case for the hazards of log-log fitting.
Limitations
No mechanism, parameters that drift with every refit, and no dated short-horizon claim that could fail — so the model cannot be falsified on the timescales traders act over.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a high coefficient of determination on log-log axes as evidence.
  • Treating a refitted band as the same model as before.
  • Using a decade-scale curve to time an entry.

Examples

market

entekhab.net recomputes this model daily on live price data and publishes the resulting corridor. Because each refit moves the parameters, a level quoted from the model last year is not the level the same model gives today.

numeric

With an exponent of 5, doubling the network's age multiplies the modelled value by 32. But the age doubles only after another seventeen years, so the claim cannot be checked until the 2040s.

Given
  • model form: price proportional to t to the power n, with t the age in days since the genesis block
  • illustrative exponent n = 5 (parameters are refitted, not constants)
  • network age at the start of 2026: about 17 years
Steps
  1. doubling t multiplies the modelled value by 2 to the power n
  2. 2 to the power 5 = 32
  3. t doubles only after another 17 years, in about 2043
A 32-fold modelled rise, testable only in the 2040s.

Nothing about the coming year follows from the fit. That is why short-horizon falsification is impossible, and why the exponent used here is illustrative rather than quoted as a measured value.

Procedure

For each day take the price and the network's age in days since the genesis block. Take logarithms of both. Fit a straight line by least squares; its slope is the exponent and its intercept the log of the scale constant. Exponentiate to draw the curve on price axes. Bands are then added by a separate, non-standard choice.

Worked example

Given
  • illustrative n = 5
  • network age about 17 years at the start of 2026
Steps
  1. ratio of modelled values when t doubles = 2 to the power n
  2. = 2 to the power 5 = 32
  3. t doubles in about 2043
A doubling of network age implies a 32-fold modelled value under n = 5.

Interpretation

The output is a modelled reference value for a given date, not a forecast with an error bar. Its level depends entirely on the sample used for the fit, so two publishers using different start dates or different price sources report different corridors for the same day.

Assumptions

  • t is measured from the genesis block in most implementations; some use the first traded price instead, which changes both parameters and therefore every published level.
  • Parameters are estimated by least squares on logged variables, which minimises relative rather than absolute error and weights the earliest, lowest-priced data heavily.
  • Band construction is not standardised: fixed multiples of the fit, residual quantiles and visually drawn corridors all circulate under the same name.
  • The specification is a bare power law; there is no standard version with a stochastic term, so no confidence interval follows from the model itself.
Limits of the formula
  • A log-log fit to any monotonically rising series looks good, so the fit statistic is not evidence for this specification over others.
  • No mechanism connects elapsed time to value, so there is no parameter that can be wrong for an identifiable reason.
  • Parameters drift with every refit and the model makes no dated short-horizon claim, so it cannot be falsified over horizons traders care about.
  • Survivorship: the curve exists because this asset survived. The same procedure applied to assets that failed is not plotted alongside it.

Commonly confused with

MVRV

MVRV compares market value with an on-chain cost basis, so it is anchored to observable holder data. The power law is anchored only to elapsed time and contains no market information at all.
