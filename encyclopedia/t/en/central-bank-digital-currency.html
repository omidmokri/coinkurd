Simple definition

Money issued directly by a central bank in digital form, as a liability of that bank rather than of a commercial bank. It is public money in a new container, not a cryptoasset.

Professional definition

A digital liability of a central bank, denominated in the national unit of account and available either to the public for payments, a retail design, or restricted to financial institutions for settlement, a wholesale design. Unlike a stablecoin, there is no private issuer and no reserve question: the claim is on the central bank itself.

Technical definition

Design choices are interdependent and each involves a trade-off. Access can be account-based, requiring identification, or token-based, which complicates compliance. Intermediation can be direct, putting the central bank in the customer relationship, or two-tier, leaving distribution with banks. Holding limits and remuneration determine how far deposits migrate out of commercial banks. Privacy and traceability pull against each other, offline capability conflicts with settlement finality, and cross-border use raises questions about currency substitution. None of these has a settled answer.

Academic definition

A digital form of sovereign money constituting a direct claim on the issuing central bank, whose design parameters — access model, intermediation structure, remuneration, holding limits and privacy architecture — jointly determine its implications for payment systems, bank intermediation and monetary policy transmission.

Mechanism

Because the claim is on the central bank, the credit and liquidity risk behind a commercial deposit disappears — and that is precisely the design problem. Money safer than a deposit invites migration out of banks, and banks fund lending with deposits, so holding limits and whether the instrument pays interest decide how far the effect goes. Every other choice has the same shape: improving one property worsens another.

In practice

Who uses it
Central banks and finance ministries, payment-system operators and banks, privacy and consumer advocates, and analysts assessing effects on deposits and cross-border flows.
Why it matters
It is the main public-sector response to digital payment change, and its design decides who bears settlement risk and who sees transaction data.
Limitations
This entry describes design trade-offs, not any project's status. Programmes differ by jurisdiction, most are in research or pilot stages, and nothing here endorses or opposes issuance.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a CBDC a cryptocurrency or a stablecoin.
  • Assuming any single design — on privacy, limits or interest — is settled practice.
  • Reading a pilot as a decision to issue.

Examples

comparison

A wholesale design serves only banks settling with each other, so it changes plumbing without touching households. A retail design reaches the public directly and therefore raises questions about deposits, privacy and offline use that the wholesale case does not.

numeric

Suppose a retail design caps individual holdings at 3,000 units. For a population of 10 million adults, the theoretical ceiling on migration out of deposits is 30 billion units — a bound set by policy, not by demand.

Given
  • per-person holding limit = 3,000 units
  • adult population = 10,000,000
  • assumes every holder uses the full limit
Steps
  1. 3,000 x 10,000,000 = 30,000,000,000 units
a theoretical maximum of 30 billion units could sit in CBDC rather than in deposits

Hypothetical illustration of why holding limits exist. Actual take-up would be far below any such ceiling, and the figure is not a projection for any country or project.

Commonly confused with

Stablecoin

A stablecoin is a private liability that references official money and depends on its issuer's reserves and redemption. A CBDC is official money itself, issued by the central bank, with no private issuer and no reserve adequacy question.
