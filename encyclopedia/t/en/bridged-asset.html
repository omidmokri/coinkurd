Simple definition

An asset that appears on a second chain because a bridge locked the original somewhere else and issued a stand-in. You hold the stand-in, and the bridge holds the original.

Professional definition

A representation issued by a cross-chain bridge, typically by locking or burning units on a source chain and minting an equivalent on a destination chain. The holder's claim depends on the bridge's validation set and its locked reserves, so a bridged unit of an asset is not the same instrument as the native unit even when the ticker matches.

Technical definition

A bridge must convince the destination chain that something happened on the source chain, and the destination chain cannot verify this itself without a light client or proof system. Most designs therefore delegate that attestation to a validator set, oracle committee or multisig, which concentrates the entire value of locked reserves behind a signing quorum — the reason bridges have been among the most heavily attacked components of this market. Multiple bridges for the same asset also produce multiple non-fungible representations, which fragments liquidity.

Academic definition

A ledger entry on a destination chain representing units immobilised on a source chain, whose fidelity to the original depends on the security of the cross-chain message-attestation mechanism and on the integrity of the escrow holding the locked units.

Mechanism

The locked reserve is a single pot and the attestation mechanism is the lock on it, so the economics are those of a vault: the cost of attacking the bridge is bounded by the cost of corrupting the quorum, while the prize is everything deposited. That asymmetry grows as deposits accumulate while the signing threshold stays the same. Replacing committees with on-chain verification shifts security back to the chains.

In practice

Who uses it
Users moving assets between chains, protocol teams deciding which bridged representation to accept, and risk teams sizing exposure to a bridge's validator set.
Why it matters
Chains cannot read each other, so without bridges an asset is confined to the ledger that issued it.
Limitations
Risk is additive: you hold the asset's own risk plus the bridge's. Representations from different bridges are not interchangeable, and documented bridge failures have resulted in total loss for holders of the affected wrapper.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a matching ticker means a fungible asset.
  • Judging a bridge by its user interface rather than its validation model.
  • Holding a bridged representation long-term when the native asset was available.

Examples

crypto

The same asset arrives on a chain through two bridges. The two representations trade separately, cannot be redeemed against each other, and split the available depth, so quoted liquidity for the asset overstates what any one holder can exit into.

numeric

A bridge holds reserves worth 500 million behind a 5-of-9 signing set. An attacker needs five keys; the prize is the whole reserve. Security is the cost of five compromises, not of the reserve.

Given
  • locked reserves = 500,000,000 units of account
  • signing threshold = 5 of 9 keys
  • no additional on-chain verification
Steps
  1. keys required to move reserves = 5
  2. value at risk per successful quorum compromise = 500,000,000
  3. security margin = cost of compromising 5 keys versus 500,000,000 of prize
the whole reserve is recoverable by whoever controls five keys

Hypothetical figures. The point is structural: threshold schemes bound attack cost at the quorum, while the prize grows with deposits.

Commonly confused with

Layer 2

A layer 2 inherits security from the chain it settles to, with proofs or a dispute window. A third-party bridge does not: its guarantees come from its own validator set, so the two are not comparable trust models even when both move assets between ledgers.
