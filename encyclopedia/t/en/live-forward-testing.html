Live Forward Testing

Simple definition

Running the rule with real money, in deliberately small size, and recording what actually happens. It is the only test that includes your fills and your own behaviour.

Professional definition

Deployment at reduced size as an evaluation phase: the signals and the operational path are the live ones, the capital is real but small, and the purpose is to measure execution cost, fill behaviour and operator discipline rather than to earn.

Technical definition

It converts the remaining unmeasurable terms into data: realised slippage against the decision price, achieved fill ratio on passive orders, funding actually paid, and the frequency of manual intervention. Because the record is prospective it cannot be curve-fitted. But it is slow and small: a statistically useful number of trades can take months, and several costs scale non-linearly with size, so a clean record at one tenth of target size does not extrapolate to target size.

Academic definition

A prospective evaluation phase in which a strategy is executed with real but reduced capital, generating an out-of-sample record inclusive of realised execution costs and operational behaviour.

Mechanism

Nothing simulated can produce a queue position or a counterparty's decision, so some quantities become observable only by participating. Small live size buys those observations at a bounded cost. The trade-off is statistical: reducing size reduces the loss and also reduces the information, because the cost terms that matter most at full size are precisely the ones smallest at test size.

In practice

Who uses it
Funds promoting a strategy from research into production, and individual traders moving from backtest to deployment.
Why it matters
It is the only record that is out-of-sample by construction and also inclusive of real execution and real behaviour.
Limitations
Slow, small and not scale-invariant: impact, borrow and funding grow with size, so a good small-size record is necessary and never sufficient.

Common mistakes

  • Scaling up after the first profitable month.
  • Changing the rule mid-test, which turns the record into in-sample data.
  • Assuming test-size costs extrapolate to target size.

Examples

numeric

Sixty live trades return four basis points each against a backtest expectancy of twelve. With a per-trade standard deviation of fifty basis points the standard error is about six and a half, so the live result is not yet distinguishable from either figure.

Given
  • n = 60 live trades
  • mean realised result = +4 bps per trade
  • backtest expectancy = +12 bps per trade
  • per-trade standard deviation = 50 bps
Steps
  1. standard error = 50 / sqrt(60) = 50 / 7.746 = 6.45 bps
  2. approximate 95% interval = 4 +/- 1.96 x 6.45, about -8.6 to +16.6 bps
the interval contains both 0 and 12, so sixty trades decide nothing

This is the arithmetic most forward tests skip. The honest conclusion after sixty trades is usually that the sample is too small, not that the edge is confirmed or gone.

practical

A desk runs a new rule at one tenth of target size for a quarter. The signals match the research exactly, but realised slippage is three times the modelled figure, and the rule is rejected on cost rather than on signal.

Commonly confused with

Walk-Forward Testing

Walk-forward testing is still a historical simulation, however carefully arranged. Live forward testing happens in real time with real orders, so it contains costs, fills and behaviour that no simulation produces.
