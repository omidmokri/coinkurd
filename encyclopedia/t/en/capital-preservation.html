Capital Preservation

Category
Professional Investment
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

An objective of not losing the money rather than growing it. In nominal terms that means avoiding losses; in real terms it also means keeping pace with rising prices.

Professional definition

An investment objective in which the binding constraint is the acceptable loss of principal rather than the return sought. It forces two separate decisions: what loss is tolerable over what horizon, and whether preservation is measured in nominal currency or in purchasing power, because those are different objectives.

Technical definition

Preservation is asymmetric. Recovering a decline requires a larger percentage gain than the decline itself, because the gain is earned on a smaller base, so bounding depth matters more here than raising the average return. Measured in real terms, a holding that returns nothing while prices rise is losing capital even though its nominal value is unchanged, so a nominal target can be met while the objective fails. Instruments with no price volatility still carry issuer, currency and inflation risk.

Academic definition

An objective function in which the primary constraint is the admissible loss of principal over a stated horizon, measured either nominally or in real purchasing power, rather than the level of expected return.

Mechanism

Gains and losses are not symmetric in percentage terms, because each is measured against a different base, and a decline removes capital that would otherwise have compounded. A preservation objective is therefore mostly a constraint on depth and on the chance of permanent impairment, not a view about expected return. Inflation imposes the same arithmetic quietly, with no visible loss at all.

In practice

Who uses it
Investors with near-term obligations, treasury functions, and anyone whose objective is funding a known commitment rather than maximising growth.
Why it matters
It turns a vague preference for safety into a stated maximum tolerable loss and a stated measurement basis, both of which can be checked afterwards.
Limitations
Nothing preserves capital unconditionally. Cash carries inflation and currency risk, bonds carry issuer and rate risk, and preservation in one currency is not preservation in another.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a stable nominal value as preserved capital while inflation reduces what it buys.
  • Assuming a low-volatility instrument carries no issuer or currency risk.
  • Stating the objective without naming the horizon or the currency.

Examples

numeric

A portfolio that falls 30% needs a 42.9% gain to return to its starting value, because the gain is earned on the smaller remaining base. The deeper the decline, the more disproportionate the recovery it demands.

Given
  • starting value: 100
  • decline of 30% leaves 70
Steps
  1. required multiple = 100 / 70 = 1.429
  2. required gain = 1.429 - 1 = 0.429
a 42.9% gain is needed to recover a 30% loss

At a 50% decline the required gain is 100%, and at 70% it is about 233%. The general expression is given in the drawdown entry.

everyday

A balance left untouched for ten years, while prices rise at an assumed 3% a year, buys roughly a quarter less at the end even though the number on the statement never moved. Nominal and real preservation are different objectives.

Commonly confused with

Drawdown

A drawdown is the measurement of a decline from a peak. Capital preservation is an objective that constrains how large a drawdown is acceptable, and in what currency or purchasing power it is measured.
