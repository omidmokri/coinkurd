Simple definition

The single interest rate that makes a bond's price today equal to all the money it will pay later. It is the return if you hold to the end and nothing goes wrong.

Professional definition

The discount rate that equates a bond's market price to the present value of its remaining coupons and principal. It is the bond's internal rate of return, found by solving the pricing equation for the rate rather than by a formula, and it assumes every coupon is reinvested at that same rate.

Technical definition

Defined implicitly: y is the root of the price equation, with price given and y unknown. For a positive-coupon bond the equation has one economically meaningful root because price is monotonically decreasing in y. No general closed form exists, so y is obtained numerically; textbook 'approximate YTM' expressions are approximations and should be labelled as such. The measure embeds a flat-curve and full-reinvestment assumption.

Academic definition

The internal rate of return implied by a bond's market price and its contractual cash-flow schedule, obtained as the unique positive root of the price-yield equation.

Mechanism

Price and yield are two readings of one quantity: fix the cash flows and you can state either, since each implies the other. Solving for y answers 'what rate am I being offered', which is why the market quotes bonds in yield even though it trades them in price.

In practice

Who uses it
Fixed-income desks, portfolio managers comparing issues of different coupons, and credit analysts reading spreads over a benchmark.
Why it matters
It makes bonds with different coupons, prices and maturities comparable on a single axis.
Limitations
A realised return equal to YTM requires holding to maturity, no default, and reinvesting every coupon at that rate; all three frequently fail.

Common mistakes

  • Reading YTM as a guaranteed return.
  • Comparing yields quoted under different compounding conventions.
  • Ignoring that a callable bond may never reach maturity.

Examples

market

A bond quoted at 92 with a 3% coupon and four years left offers a yield above its coupon: the buyer collects small coupons plus the 8-point climb back to face. Quoting it as 'a 3% bond' hides most of the return.

numeric

Price 946.54, face 1,000, coupon 40 a year, three years left. Trying 6% makes the discounted cash flows equal the price exactly, so the yield to maturity is 6% — found by search, not by rearranging a formula.

Given
  • P = 946.54
  • F = 1,000
  • C = 40 per year
  • n = 3 years
  • annual compounding
Steps
  1. try y = 5%: 40/1.05 + 40/1.05^2 + 1,040/1.05^3 = 972.77 (too high)
  2. try y = 6%: 37.74 + 35.60 + 873.20 = 946.54 (match)
YTM = 6.0% per year

Solved by iteration because the price equation cannot be rearranged for y in general.

Procedure

Take the price as given and search for the rate that reproduces it: bracket a low and a high guess, then bisect or use a solver until the discounted cash flows match the price to the required tolerance. Report the compounding convention used.

Worked example

Given
  • P = 946.54
  • C = 40
  • F = 1,000
  • n = 3
Steps
  1. y = 5% gives 972.77
  2. y = 6% gives 946.54
  3. price matches at y = 6%
y = 6.0%

Interpretation

A yield of 6% says: at this price, the contract is equivalent to lending at 6% per year under the stated assumptions. It is a price restated as a rate, not a promise about what you will earn.

Assumptions

  • No closed-form solution exists in general; y is obtained numerically. Any algebraic 'approximate YTM' formula is an approximation and is not reported here as an identity.
  • Every coupon is assumed reinvested at y, which is the single largest reason realised returns differ from YTM.
  • A flat yield is applied to all maturities, so YTM is not the same as a set of zero-coupon spot rates.
  • Call, put and sinking-fund features invalidate the fixed-schedule premise; yield-to-call is then the relevant measure.
Limits of the formula
  • Comparisons require identical compounding and day-count conventions; otherwise two quoted yields are not on the same scale.
  • For a bond trading at a distressed price, YTM becomes an arithmetic artefact rather than an expected return, because default is not in the cash flows.

Commonly confused with

Internal Rate of Return

Yield to maturity is the internal rate of return of a bond's contractual cash flows. IRR is the same idea applied to any project, where the cash flows are forecasts rather than contractual promises.
