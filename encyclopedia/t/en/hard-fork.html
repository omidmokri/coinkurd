Hard Fork

Simple definition

A rule change that old software will not accept. Everyone has to upgrade, or the network ends up running as two separate chains.

Professional definition

A backward-incompatible change: blocks valid under the new rules are rejected by non-upgraded nodes, so those nodes cannot follow the upgraded chain. If a meaningful share of users and producers declines to upgrade, two chains persist with one shared history and separate futures.

Technical definition

A hard fork widens or otherwise alters the validity predicate, so old and new rule sets are not nested. Coordination is therefore all-or-nothing at a stated activation height or epoch. A persistent split duplicates every balance across both chains and needs replay protection, so that a transaction signed for one chain is not also valid on the other; without it, users can be induced to move funds twice.

Academic definition

A change to a protocol's validity predicate that is not backward compatible, requiring near-universal adoption to avoid a permanent partition of chain history and state.

Mechanism

The incompatibility is the point: because old nodes cannot even parse the new chain as valid, there is no quiet path. Either the economic weight moves together, and the old chain is abandoned, or both chains find users and the split becomes permanent. The protocol cannot decide this; holders, producers and venues do.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol developers, node operators, miners and validators, exchange listing and custody teams, and holders deciding which chain to follow.
Why it matters
It is the only way to relax or restructure a rule, and it is the mechanism by which a contested community actually separates.
Limitations
A split duplicates balances but not liquidity, support or security; the smaller chain usually ends up cheaper to attack.

Common mistakes

  • Trading a forked asset before replay protection is confirmed.
  • Assuming duplicated balances mean duplicated value.
  • Treating a scheduled upgrade as a split when nobody intends to keep the old rules.

Examples

crypto

Replacing a chain's consensus mechanism is necessarily backward-incompatible: blocks produced the new way cannot satisfy the old rules, so every node must run the upgraded software or stop following the chain.

everyday

Two committees keep the same minute book up to today, then adopt different standing orders. Yesterday's minutes are shared; from tomorrow there are two records, and both claim to be the real one.

Commonly confused with

Fork

Fork is the general word, including accidental one-block splits. A hard fork specifically means a backward-incompatible rule change that old nodes cannot follow.
