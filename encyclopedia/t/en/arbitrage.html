Arbitrage

Simple definition

Buying something where it is cheap and selling the same thing where it is dear, to capture the difference. In practice the difference is small and the execution is hard.

Professional definition

Simultaneously taking offsetting positions in the same or economically equivalent exposure at different prices, in order to capture the spread. Textbook arbitrage is riskless; every implementable version carries execution, funding, counterparty and transfer risk.

Technical definition

A trade built to be insensitive to the direction of the underlying, earning the convergence of two prices rather than a move in either. The risks shift from market risk to operational and financing risk: one leg fills and the other does not, a transfer between venues confirms too slowly, a withdrawal limit traps capital on the wrong side, a borrow is recalled, or a venue itself fails.

Academic definition

The simultaneous purchase and sale of equivalent exposures at divergent prices to realise the spread; a riskless profit under frictionless assumptions, and a capital- and execution-constrained limited-risk strategy under realistic frictions.

Mechanism

Arbitrage is what makes the law of one price hold approximately, because acting on a gap narrows it. The gap persists only to the extent that closing it is expensive: capital must be committed on two venues at once, assets must move between them, and the position must survive a widening before convergence. Those costs set the width of the band the price can sit in.

In practice

Who uses it
Proprietary desks, market makers hedging inventory, and funds running cash-and-carry or funding-capture books.
Why it matters
It earns a spread that does not depend on predicting direction, so its returns are less correlated with market moves than a directional strategy's.
Limitations
No implementable version is risk-free. Capital is locked on both venues, the spread can widen before it converges, and the failure modes are operational: a stuck transfer, a withdrawal limit, a halted venue.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a cross-venue trade risk-free and sizing it with leverage.
  • Ignoring withdrawal limits, confirmation times and transfer fees.
  • Forgetting the short leg can be liquidated while the trade is still correct.

Examples

market

A desk buys spot and sells the same quantity of a dated future trading above it, locking the basis until expiry. The position is flat on direction, but the short future needs margin, and if the basis widens first that margin must be funded or the leg is liquidated.

numeric

Venue A asks 100.00 and venue B bids 100.40, a gross spread of 0.40%. On 100,000 that is 400 gross. Taker fees on both sides cost 80 and returning the asset costs 50, leaving 270, or 0.27% of notional.

Given
  • venue A ask = 100.00, venue B bid = 100.40
  • notional = 100,000
  • taker fee = 0.04% per side
  • cost of transferring the asset back = 0.05% of notional
Steps
  1. gross spread = 0.40% of 100,000 = 400
  2. fees = 0.04% x 2 = 0.08% = 80
  3. transfer = 0.05% = 50
  4. net = 400 - 80 - 50 = 270, that is 0.27% of notional
270 net on 100,000 of notional, before slippage or any failed fill

The arithmetic assumes both legs fill at the quoted prices at the same instant. If one leg slips 0.3% the entire edge is gone, and a delayed transfer leaves capital exposed in the meantime.

Commonly confused with

Market Making

A market maker quotes both sides and waits to be traded with, earning the spread on flow. An arbitrageur acts on a price difference that already exists, usually as a taker on at least one leg.

The entry makes no claim that arbitrage is risk-free: the riskless version exists only under frictionless assumptions, and the real failure modes (partial fills, transfer latency, withdrawal limits, margin on the short leg, venue failure) are stated in the definition and in practice.limits. No math block is given because the real content is the no-arbitrage boundary already stated in the futures-contract entry; the net-edge arithmetic in the numeric example is not a standard formula and is deliberately not presented as one. Fees and transfer costs in the example are illustrative.
