Recency Bias

Category

Level
Foundation

Simple definition

Weighting what just happened more heavily than what happened before. After a sharp fall, every chart looks fragile; after a long rise, risk feels theoretical.

Professional definition

The tendency to let the most recent observations dominate an estimate of what is normal. It is closely related to the availability heuristic: vivid, easily recalled events are judged more frequent, and the recent past is the most available sample of all.

Technical definition

Formally this is a sampling-window error. An estimate drawn from the last few observations has a large standard error and inherits whatever regime those observations fell in, so volatility measured across a quiet month understates the distribution the position will actually meet. The bias is not only psychological: a short lookback in an indicator or a risk model encodes the same error in code, where it is easier to see and easier to fix.

Academic definition

The systematic overweighting of recent observations in judgements of frequency, probability or central tendency, related to the availability heuristic and equivalent in effect to estimation from an unrepresentatively short sample.

Mechanism

Memory is not a uniform sample. Recent and vivid episodes are easier to retrieve, and ease of retrieval is read as evidence of frequency. Markets then amplify the error, because volatility arrives in clusters: a calm stretch really is followed by more calm, until it is not, and the quiet sample gives no warning of the break.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone sizing a position from recent volatility, choosing an indicator lookback, or judging a strategy by its last few trades.
Why it matters
It explains why risk estimates drift down in calm markets and up after a shock, exactly out of phase with what the position needs.
Limitations
Recent data is not always the wrong data. In a genuine regime change it is the only relevant sample; the error is treating a short window as representative without asking.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a strategy on its last five trades.
  • Setting stop distance from the quietest month of the year.
  • Calling a long calm stretch low risk rather than low realised volatility.

Examples

crypto

After months of range trading, position sizes across a market tend to grow and stops tighten. The first large gap then liquidates far more leverage than the same move would have reached a year earlier.

numeric

Daily volatility over a quiet 30-day window is 1.0 percent; over four years it is 3.0 percent. A stop placed at two quiet-window deviations is inside normal daily range for the longer sample.

Given
  • sigma over the last 30 days = 1.0 percent per day
  • sigma over four years of history = 3.0 percent per day
  • stop distance chosen as 2 sigma from the recent window
Steps
  1. stop distance = 2 x 1.0 = 2.0 percent
  2. expressed in long-sample units = 2.0 / 3.0 = 0.67 sigma
  3. a 0.67 sigma move is common, not exceptional
a stop intended as a two-sigma buffer is a two-thirds-sigma buffer against the longer sample

Illustrative volatility figures, not measured market values. Which window is the right one is a modelling choice; the point is that the choice is being made either way.

Commonly confused with

Volatility Clustering

Volatility clustering is a measured property of a return series: calm follows calm. Recency bias is a property of the person reading it. One can be tested out of sample; the other cannot be tested from the chart at all.
