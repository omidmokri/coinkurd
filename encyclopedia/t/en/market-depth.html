Simple definition

How much you could buy or sell near the current price before moving it. A deep market absorbs a large order quietly; a thin one jumps out of the way.

Professional definition

The quantity resting within a chosen distance of the quote, read off the order book. Usually reported as the size available within so many ticks or basis points of the mid, or as the notional needed to move the mid by a set amount.

Technical definition

Depth is a function rather than a single number: cumulative resting quantity against distance from the mid, one curve per side. Common summaries are size within ten basis points, the notional required to move the mid by one percent, and the slope of the cumulative curve. None of these is standardised, so two depth figures are comparable only when the band, the side, the venue and the snapshot timing all match. Hidden and iceberg size is excluded by construction.

Academic definition

The cumulative quantity of resting liquidity available as a function of price distance from the prevailing quote, representing the depth dimension of liquidity alongside tightness and resiliency.

Mechanism

Depth exists because liquidity suppliers are willing to hold inventory at a price, and the further from the quote the more compensation they demand, which is why the cumulative curve steepens. Depth also drains asymmetrically: when one side is being hit, suppliers withdraw there first, so the book thins in the direction price is already moving and impact per unit rises exactly as volume does.

In practice

Who uses it
Execution desks, market makers, risk teams setting size limits, and traders deciding whether to work an order or take the quote.
Why it matters
It converts position size into an expected cost and answers the prior question: whether an exit of that size exists at all right now.
Limitations
Displayed depth excludes hidden liquidity and includes cancellable quotes, and in a fragmented market no single venue's depth describes the asset.

Common mistakes

  • Reading 24-hour volume as a proxy for depth.
  • Comparing depth figures measured over different price bands.
  • Assuming weekend depth matches weekday depth.

Examples

market

Two venues quote the same one-tick spread on the same asset. One holds a thousand units within ten basis points of the mid, the other forty. The spread says they are alike; depth says only one of them can take a large order.

numeric

With a mid of 100.05, a band of one tenth of a percent runs from 99.95 to 100.15. Bids hold 170 units inside it and asks 240, so a 300-unit buy cannot be filled without leaving the band.

Given
  • mid = 100.05
  • band = plus or minus 0.1 percent of the mid, i.e. 99.95 to 100.15
  • bids: 50 at 100.00, 120 at 99.95
  • asks: 40 at 100.10, 200 at 100.15
Steps
  1. bid depth in band = 50 + 120 = 170 units
  2. ask depth in band = 40 + 200 = 240 units
  3. a 300-unit buy exceeds the 240 units offered inside the band
Depth within ten basis points: 170 bid, 240 ask.

Widen the band to half a percent and the same book reports a larger depth. The band is part of the measurement, not a detail of it.

Commonly confused with

Trading Volume

Volume counts what has already traded over a period. Depth counts what is resting right now, within a chosen distance of the quote, waiting to trade. High volume on thin depth is common.
