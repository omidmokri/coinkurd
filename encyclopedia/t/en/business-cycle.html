Simple definition

The repeated pattern of an economy growing, overheating, slowing and recovering. The sequence repeats, but the length and depth of each round do not.

Professional definition

Fluctuation of aggregate activity around its trend, conventionally described in phases from trough to expansion to peak to contraction. Phases are dated from several series rather than one, and the same calendar quarter can look like different phases on different indicators.

Technical definition

A cycle in the deviation of activity from an unobserved trend or potential, which means every measurement depends on how that trend is estimated. Filters, production-function estimates and survey-based measures give different output gaps for the same data, and all are revised. The regularity is in the sequence of phases, not in their duration; cycles are not periodic and cannot be extrapolated by counting quarters.

Academic definition

Recurrent but aperiodic fluctuations of aggregate output, employment and income around an estimated trend, characterised by the co-movement of many series across phases.

Mechanism

Expansions build their own reversal: tight capacity raises costs, credit standards loosen while collateral is rising, and inventories and capital spending overshoot demand. When demand slows, those same positions unwind together. Policy then leans the other way, with lags long enough that the response often lands in the following phase.

In practice

Who uses it
Strategic and tactical allocators, credit and equity analysts, and macro managers mapping phases to factor and sector exposures.
Why it matters
Phase conditions earnings growth, default risk, policy direction and cross-asset correlation together, so it organises expectations better than any single indicator.
Limitations
The current phase is only estimated, the trend it is measured against is unobservable, and phase-based allocation rules are fitted to a small number of historical cycles.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming cycles have a fixed length you can count forward.
  • Fitting a rotation rule to a handful of past cycles and trusting it.
  • Confusing the market cycle with the economic cycle.

Examples

numeric

Actual output is 9,800 and estimated potential output 10,000. The output gap is minus 2 percent: spare capacity exists on this estimate. A different trend estimate of 9,900 would make the same quarter look nearly balanced.

Given
  • actual output = 9,800
  • estimated potential output = 10,000
  • alternative potential estimate = 9,900
Steps
  1. gap = (9,800 - 10,000) / 10,000 = -0.02
  2. alternative gap = (9,800 - 9,900) / 9,900 = -0.0101
output gap = -2.0% on one trend estimate, -1.0% on another

Figures are invented. The point is that potential output is estimated, not observed, so the sign and size of a gap depend on the estimate chosen.

market

Two expansions can last very different lengths, so an allocator who shortens risk simply because the current one is old is using duration as a signal that history does not support.

Commonly confused with

Market Cycle

The market cycle is a pattern in asset prices and positioning; the business cycle is a pattern in production, employment and income. Prices usually turn first, so the two are offset rather than identical.
