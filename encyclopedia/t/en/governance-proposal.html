Simple definition

A formal motion to change a protocol, decided by token holders voting. If it passes and the code allows it, the change happens automatically.

Professional definition

An executable or signalling motion submitted to a protocol's governance system, voted on by token weight and enacted after any delay period. Typical subjects are risk parameters, fee splits, treasury spending, contract upgrades and the permissions held by administrative keys.

Technical definition

Token-weighted voting makes influence proportional to holdings, so the distribution of the token is the distribution of control. Where a small number of addresses or delegates can reach quorum, the outcome is effectively decided before the vote opens, and the public process documents rather than produces the decision. Timelocks give users a window to exit, but only for changes that actually route through governance: emergency powers and multisignature keys usually do not.

Academic definition

A formal mechanism for collective decision-making over protocol parameters and code, in which voting rights are allocated by transferable token holdings and outcomes are executed by smart contract subject to procedural delays.

Mechanism

Making votes transferable makes control purchasable. That is efficient in one sense — whoever values a decision most can acquire the weight to make it — and it is exactly why concentration is the standing problem: a protocol described as community-run can be parameter-controlled by a handful of large holders, delegates or an insider allocation that never had to seek anyone's agreement.

In practice

Who uses it
Token holders and delegates, protocol teams drafting changes, large depositors whose risk parameters are at stake, and researchers measuring voting concentration.
Why it matters
It is the only visible route by which the rules a position depends on can be changed, so reading proposals is part of monitoring risk.
Limitations
Weight is bought, not earned, turnout is usually low, and many consequential powers sit outside governance entirely in admin keys.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a passed vote as broad agreement rather than as a quorum reached.
  • Assuming collateral parameters cannot change under an open position.
  • Ignoring the timelock window that is the only chance to exit.

Examples

numeric

A vote needs a quorum of four percent of supply and a simple majority. With 1,000,000,000 tokens issued, 40,000,000 votes suffice, and three addresses holding 15,000,000 each can carry it alone.

Given
  • token supply = 1,000,000,000
  • quorum = 4% of supply
  • passage rule = simple majority of votes cast
  • three addresses hold 15,000,000 each
Steps
  1. quorum in votes = 0.04 x 1,000,000,000 = 40,000,000
  2. three addresses together = 3 x 15,000,000 = 45,000,000
  3. 45,000,000 > 40,000,000, so quorum is met and the majority is theirs
4.5% of supply, held by three addresses, decides the outcome.

Quorum rules and thresholds differ by protocol; the figures are illustrative of the arithmetic, not of any particular vote.

market

A proposal lowers the liquidation threshold on one collateral asset. Positions that were comfortable become eligible for liquidation the moment it executes, so the timelock between approval and execution is the only window to add collateral or repay.

Commonly confused with

Governance Token

The token is the voting weight. The proposal is the decision that weight is applied to; holding one does not imply participating in the other.
