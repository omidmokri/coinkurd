Index Investing

Simple definition

Holding a portfolio built to track a published index instead of choosing holdings, so the result is the index's return minus costs.

Professional definition

A rules-based approach in which the portfolio replicates a named index's constituents and weights, by full replication or by sampling. The intended outcome is the index return less fees and tracking error. There is no attempt to outperform and no manager judgement about individual holdings.

Technical definition

Implementation is mechanical: constituents and weights come from the index rules, and the portfolio's deviation from them is measured as tracking error. Costs are low because turnover is limited to index changes and cash flows. The construction inherits whatever the index embeds: a capitalisation-weighted index automatically raises weight in whatever has risen, concentrates when a few constituents dominate, and offers no protection in a market-wide decline. Index changes are published in advance, which creates predictable trading around them.

Academic definition

A passive construction method in which portfolio weights follow the published rules of a reference index, with performance evaluated as tracking error against that index rather than as excess return.

Mechanism

The cost argument is arithmetic: all investors together hold the market, so before costs the average invested unit of capital earns the market return and after costs earns less in proportion to what it pays. An index fund's edge is therefore a smaller deduction, not skill. The counterargument is that the index is itself a set of choices about market, weighting and inclusion, and those choices are not neutral.

In practice

Who uses it
Individuals, pension plans and institutions implementing a policy allocation, and anyone who wants a defined exposure at a known cost.
Why it matters
It delivers a stated exposure cheaply and predictably, with a result that can be checked against a public reference instead of a narrative.
Limitations
It delivers the index's losses as faithfully as its gains, carries whatever concentration the weighting rule produces, and leaves the choice of index as the decision that actually matters.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a narrow sector or single-country index 'the market'.
  • Assuming an index fund reduces risk rather than defining it.
  • Comparing funds on headline fee alone, ignoring tracking error and trading costs.

Examples

numeric

An index returns 8.00% over a year; the fund tracking it returns 7.85%. The 0.15 point gap breaks down into the stated fee and the residual cost of trading and holding cash. Nothing in the gap is a manager decision.

Given
  • index total return: 8.00% for the year
  • fund total return: 7.85% for the year
  • stated annual fee: 0.08%
Steps
  1. gap = 8.00% - 7.85% = 0.15 percentage points
  2. fee accounts for 0.08 points
  3. remaining 0.07 points attributable to trading costs, cash drag and sampling error
a 0.15 point shortfall, of which about half is the stated fee

A tracking shortfall can also be negative in a given year, for example when securities-lending income exceeds costs. The figures are illustrative.

market

When a handful of constituents grow to a large share of an index's capitalisation, a fund tracking it holds that concentration by construction. The method imposes no limit; the weighting rule decides, and the holder has accepted it in advance.

Commonly confused with

Exchange-Traded Fund

An exchange-traded fund is a wrapper; index investing is a strategy. Many ETFs track indices, but ETFs can be actively managed and index funds can be ordinary mutual funds.
