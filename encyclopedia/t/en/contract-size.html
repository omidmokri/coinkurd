Contract Size

Category
Derivatives
Level
Foundation

Simple definition

How much of the underlying one contract represents. Buying one contract is never buying one unit; it is buying whatever quantity the contract specification says.

Professional definition

The exchange-defined quantity of the underlying covered by a single contract, or the multiplier applied to an index level. It converts a quoted price into money: notional equals contract size times the number of contracts times the price, and it also fixes what one tick is worth.

Technical definition

A specification field, not a market variable. It determines notional exposure, the cash value of the minimum price increment, and therefore the granularity of position sizing: a trader cannot size below one contract, so the contract size sets the smallest expressible risk. Venues list the same underlying in standard, mini and micro sizes precisely to lower that floor, and sizes differ between venues for the same underlying.

Academic definition

The contractually specified quantity of the underlying asset, or the monetary multiplier applied to a reference index level, by which a quoted derivative price is translated into notional value.

Mechanism

Standardising quantity is what makes contracts fungible: if every contract covers the same amount, any seller can close any buyer. The side effect is a sizing floor. Where the floor is high relative to an account, the trader's only way to control risk is the stop distance, because the quantity cannot be reduced — which is the real reason mini and micro listings exist.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone trading listed futures or options, and risk teams converting contract counts into exposure for reporting and limits.
Why it matters
It is the bridge between a chart in price terms and an account in money terms; without it a stop distance cannot be turned into a loss figure.
Limitations
Specifications are venue-specific and can be revised, and the same ticker on two venues may differ in size, tick and settlement, so a size must be read from the current contract specification.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a quote as if one contract were one unit of the underlying.
  • Copying a position size between venues with different multipliers.
  • Forgetting that tick value, not tick count, decides the loss.

Examples

market

Two venues list a contract on the same index, one with a multiplier of 50 and one with 5. The chart is identical and the tick is identical, but a ten-tick move costs ten times as much on the first. The specification, not the chart, decides the risk.

numeric

Suppose a contract covers 5 units of an index quoted at 3,000, with a minimum increment of 0.25. Notional is 15,000 per contract and one tick is worth 1.25, so three contracts moving eight ticks against you cost 30.

Given
  • Contract size Q_c = 5 units per contract
  • Price P = 3,000 index points
  • Minimum price increment = 0.25 points
  • n = 3 contracts
  • Adverse move = 8 ticks
Steps
  1. Notional per contract = 5 x 3,000 = 15,000
  2. Tick value = 5 x 0.25 = 1.25
  3. Loss = 1.25 x 8 x 3 = 30
Notional 45,000 across three contracts; the eight-tick adverse move costs 30

Figures are illustrative. Contract size, tick size and tick value are published per product by each venue and must be read there.

Mathematical foundation

N = Q_c \times n \times P, \qquad V_{\text{tick}} = Q_c \times \Delta_{\text{tick}}

Procedure

Read the contract size and minimum increment from the product specification. Multiply size by the number of contracts and by the current price to get notional. Multiply size by the increment to get what one tick is worth, then multiply by ticks and contracts to turn any price distance into money.

Worked example

Given
  • Q_c = 5
  • n = 3
  • P = 3,000
  • tick = 0.25
Steps
  1. N = 5 x 3 x 3,000 = 45,000
  2. V_tick = 5 x 0.25 = 1.25
  3. 8 ticks x 1.25 x 3 contracts = 30
N = 45,000 and an eight-tick adverse move costs 30

Interpretation

These are unit conversions, not a model: they say what a position is worth and what a price distance costs. Their value is that they make the two numbers a trader actually controls, contracts and stop distance, commensurable with account equity.

Assumptions

  • A linear, quote-currency-settled contract. An inverse or coin-margined contract converts price into money differently, so this notional identity does not hold there.
  • Contract size and tick size are taken from the current published specification of that product on that venue.
Limits of the formula
  • Notional is a gross exposure figure. It says nothing about margin required, which is set separately by the venue.
  • Tick value is fixed only where the multiplier is fixed; products quoted in yield or in percentage terms can have a tick value that depends on price.

Commonly confused with

Lot Size

Lot size is the tradable increment of quantity, how much you may add per step. Contract size is how much underlying each of those units represents. One governs granularity, the other exposure.
