Simple definition

How fast an option's delta changes as the underlying moves. High gamma means the position's directional exposure is changing quickly under your feet.

Professional definition

The second partial derivative of option value with respect to the underlying price, ∂²V/∂S², equivalently the rate of change of delta. It is positive for every long option, call or put, and negative for every written one, and it measures the curvature that a delta hedge cannot capture.

Technical definition

Under Black-Scholes, gamma is the standard normal density at d1 divided by the underlying price times volatility times the square root of time, which is identical for a call and a put on the same strike and expiry. It peaks near the money and grows without bound for an at-the-money option as expiry approaches, because the denominator shrinks while the density stays near its maximum. The same limit sends gamma to zero away from the strike.

Academic definition

The second-order sensitivity of a contingent claim's value to the underlying price, governing the convexity term in a Taylor expansion of the value function.

Mechanism

Gamma is curvature, and curvature is concentrated where the outcome is still undecided. Far from the strike the option is almost certainly worthless or almost certainly behaves like the asset, so delta barely moves. Near the strike a small move flips the answer, and the closer expiry gets the smaller the move needed to flip it — which is why gamma concentrates in both senses at once.

In practice

Who uses it
Option market makers, volatility desks running hedged books, and risk managers who need to know how fast a hedge will go stale.
Why it matters
It tells you how often a delta hedge must be rebalanced and how badly the first-order estimate will fail on a large move.
Limitations
Gamma is itself a model output and changes with the volatility input. Near expiry the at-the-money figure is so large that it stops being a usable planning number.

Common mistakes

  • Selling near-dated at-the-money options for premium without sizing for gamma.
  • Rebalancing a short-gamma hedge on a fixed schedule through a fast move.
  • Assuming calls and puts on one strike have different gamma.

Examples

market

A writer of at-the-money options days from expiry is short large gamma. Each move forces a hedge adjustment in the direction the market already went: buying higher and selling lower, repeatedly, which is how a small premium becomes a large loss.

numeric

Delta 0.45, gamma 0.04 per unit. The underlying rises 2: delta becomes about 0.53 and the premium gains about 0.98, against the 0.90 a delta-only estimate would predict. The 0.08 difference is the gamma term.

Given
  • delta = 0.45
  • gamma = 0.04 per unit of underlying
  • change in underlying = +2
  • volatility and time held fixed
Steps
  1. new delta = 0.45 + 0.04 x 2 = 0.53
  2. delta term = 0.45 x 2 = 0.90
  3. gamma term = 0.5 x 0.04 x 2^2 = 0.08
  4. total = 0.90 + 0.08
approximately +0.98 of premium

A second-order estimate. It is still an approximation, and it ignores the change in value from time passing and from any change in the volatility input.

Procedure

Gamma is defined as the derivative of delta with respect to the underlying. In the Black-Scholes framework, evaluate the standard normal density at d1 and divide by the underlying price times volatility times the square root of time remaining. Multiply gamma by the move and add it to delta to update the hedge.

Worked example

Given
  • delta = 0.45
  • gamma = 0.04
  • move = +2
Steps
  1. delta term = 0.45 x 2 = 0.90
  2. gamma term = 0.5 x 0.04 x 4 = 0.08
value change about 0.98; delta updates to 0.53

Interpretation

Gamma prices the fact that a hedge is only correct at one price. A long-gamma book is paid for moves and bleeds time value; a short-gamma book collects time value and pays for moves, with the payment growing as the square of the move.

Assumptions

  • The closed form is Black-Scholes and inherits its assumptions: continuous trading, no frictions, constant volatility, lognormal dynamics. The partial-derivative definition is model-free; the number is not.
  • Stated for European-style options on an underlying with no dividend or carry.
  • Gamma is identical for a call and a put of the same strike and expiry under this model, which follows directly from put-call parity.
  • The second-order expansion ignores the theta and vega terms, so it describes a move at frozen time and frozen volatility, which never happens.
Limits of the formula
  • As time to expiry goes to zero the at-the-money gamma diverges, so the model stops producing a usable number exactly where traders need one. Pin risk at expiry is this divergence in practical form.
  • Gamma is measured per unit of underlying, so comparing it across instruments requires normalising by notional and by price level.
  • A short-gamma position can look profitable for long stretches and lose all of it in one move; the distribution of outcomes is skewed, not the symmetric picture an average suggests.

Commonly confused with

Volatility

Volatility is a property of the underlying's returns. Gamma is a property of the option position's curvature. A high-gamma position is sensitive to movement; it does not mean movement is happening.
