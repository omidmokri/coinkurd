Byzantine Fault Tolerance

Simple definition

A system's ability to keep agreeing correctly even when some participants lie, go silent, or behave in any arbitrary way.

Professional definition

The property of a consensus protocol that it still reaches agreement when up to f participants behave arbitrarily, including maliciously. Classical results need more than three times as many participants as faults — n greater than or equal to 3f plus 1 — with decisions taken by quorums of 2f plus 1.

Technical definition

The threshold counts voting members or their stake weight, not computational resources. With n participants of whom at most f are Byzantine, safety and liveness hold when n is at least 3f plus 1, because any two quorums of 2f plus 1 must overlap in at least one honest member, so two contradictory decisions cannot both be certified. Nakamoto consensus does not fit this model: it has no fixed membership and no quorum, and its threshold is a majority of the measured resource.

Academic definition

The capacity of a distributed protocol to preserve agreement and validity despite up to f arbitrarily faulty participants, attainable in the classical partially synchronous setting when total membership n satisfies n greater than or equal to 3f plus 1.

Mechanism

The one-third bound comes from overlap. Any two decision quorums must share an honest member, or two conflicting decisions could each look legitimate. With membership of at least 3f plus 1 and quorums of 2f plus 1, any two quorums intersect in at least f plus 1 members, so at least one of them is honest and contradiction is impossible to certify.

In practice

Who uses it
Consensus protocol designers, validator-set operators, auditors of permissioned ledgers, and anyone comparing security claims across chains.
Why it matters
It states precisely how much misbehaviour a voting-based protocol survives, which is what makes validator-set size and distribution a security parameter rather than a statistic.
Limitations
The bound assumes known membership and resistance to identity forgery. Open networks must first make identities costly, which is what mining or staking provides.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting the one-third threshold as if it described a proof-of-work chain.
  • Counting machines instead of independent operators or stake.
  • Ignoring the synchrony assumptions under which the bound is proved.

Examples

numeric

With a hundred voting members, the classical bound tolerates thirty-three faults and needs a quorum of sixty-seven. Add a thirty-fourth faulty member and the protocol can no longer guarantee that two conflicting decisions are impossible.

Given
  • n = 100 voting members
  • classical requirement n >= 3f + 1
Steps
  1. f <= (n - 1) / 3 = 99 / 3 = 33
  2. quorum = 2f + 1 = 67
f = 33 tolerated faults, quorum of 67

Counts members or stake weight. It is not a statement about hashrate, and it does not transfer to chain-selection consensus.

crypto

Ethereum's proof of stake inherits this shape: finality needs a two-thirds supermajority of stake, so an adversary holding more than a third can stall finalisation, while contradicting a finalised checkpoint exposes at least a third of the stake to slashing.

Mathematical foundation

n \ge 3f + 1, \qquad Q = 2f + 1

Variables

nTotal number of voting participantscount
fMaximum number tolerated to behave arbitrarilycount
QQuorum size required to certify a decisioncount

Procedure

Fix the membership n. The tolerated fault count is the largest f with 3f plus 1 no greater than n, that is the integer part of n minus one over three. A decision is certified only by a quorum of 2f plus 1 members.

Worked example

Given
  • n = 100
Steps
  1. f = floor((100 - 1) / 3) = 33
  2. Q = 2(33) + 1 = 67
tolerates 33 faults with a quorum of 67

Interpretation

The ratio is about identity, not power: it says what fraction of a known membership may defect. A protocol whose security is measured in hashrate or stake share is making a different claim and must be read against its own threshold.

Assumptions

  • Membership is known and bounded, and identities cannot be forged freely.
  • Channels are authenticated and the network is at worst partially synchronous.
  • Each member carries one vote, or stake-weighted votes are treated as the equivalent weight.
Limits of the formula
  • The bound is about voting-based agreement. It does not describe open-membership chain-selection consensus, where the relevant threshold is a majority of the measured resource.
  • Tolerating f faults says nothing about whether the f faulty members are independent: correlated failure, shared hosting or one dominant client can break the independence the bound assumes.

Commonly confused with

Fifty-One Percent Attack

BFT's one-third bound counts voting members or stake in a known set. The fifty-one percent threshold counts a majority of production power in an open set. Different models, different thresholds, and neither number applies to the other.
