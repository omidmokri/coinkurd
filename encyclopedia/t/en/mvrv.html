Simple definition

Market capitalisation divided by realised capitalisation: what the coin supply is worth now against what it was worth when each coin last moved.

Professional definition

An on-chain ratio of market capitalisation to realised capitalisation, where realised capitalisation values every unit of supply at the price at which it last changed hands on chain. Above one, holders are in aggregate paper profit; below one, in aggregate paper loss.

Technical definition

A cost-basis ratio built from UTXO or address-level transfer histories. Realised capitalisation substitutes the price of each unit's last on-chain movement for the current price, so the ratio is the aggregate unrealised profit multiple of the supply. Its denominator inherits every defect of that attribution: coins lost forever keep an ancient price and never leave the base, and exchange internal transfers restamp coins that never changed owner.

Academic definition

The quotient of an asset's market capitalisation and its realised capitalisation, the latter valuing each unit of supply at the price prevailing at its most recent on-chain transfer.

Mechanism

Realised capitalisation is an approximation of what the market actually paid. Dividing current value by it gives the aggregate multiple on that cost basis, which tends to be high after a long advance, when most of the supply was acquired far lower, and near or below one after a capitulation, when heavy movement reset much of the base near current prices.

In practice

Who uses it
On-chain analysts, long-horizon allocators, and researchers studying where a market sits within a cycle.
Why it matters
It reduces the whole supply's cost basis to one ratio, which gives a cycle-relative sense of aggregate paper profit that price alone does not.
Limitations
Realised capitalisation is an estimate. Lost coins, exchange internal transfers and custodial restructuring all restamp or freeze cost bases, and the thresholds people quote come from only a handful of cycles.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a historical extreme as a threshold rather than a sample of a few cycles.
  • Comparing MVRV across chains with different attribution methods.
  • Reading a ratio above one as a sell signal.

Examples

market

After a long advance most of the supply was last moved far below the current price, so the ratio sits well above one. After a capitulation, heavy on-chain movement restamps cost bases near current prices and it approaches or falls below one.

numeric

Supply is 20 million units at a market price of 40,000, so market capitalisation is 800 billion. Realised capitalisation is 400 billion, which makes the realised price 20,000 and MVRV exactly 2.0.

Given
  • circulating supply = 20,000,000 units
  • market price = 40,000 per unit
  • realised capitalisation = 400 billion
Steps
  1. market capitalisation = 20,000,000 x 40,000 = 800 billion
  2. realised price = 400 billion / 20,000,000 = 20,000
  3. MVRV = 800 / 400 = 2.0
  4. equivalently, market price / realised price = 40,000 / 20,000 = 2.0
MVRV = 2.0, realised price = 20,000

Both routes give the same ratio when the same supply definition is used in numerator and denominator. Illustrative figures, not a reading for any date.

Procedure

For every unit of supply, record the price at its most recent on-chain movement and multiply by its size, then sum across all units to get realised capitalisation. Divide the current market capitalisation by that sum. Dividing realised capitalisation by supply instead gives the realised price, and market price over realised price is the same ratio.

Worked example

Given
  • S = 20,000,000 units
  • P = 40,000
  • realised cap = 400 billion
Steps
  1. market cap = 20,000,000 x 40,000 = 800 billion
  2. MVRV = 800 / 400 = 2.0
  3. realised price = 400 billion / 20,000,000 = 20,000
  4. check: 40,000 / 20,000 = 2.0
MVRV = 2.0, realised price = 20,000

Interpretation

A ratio of 2 says the supply is worth twice its aggregate on-chain cost basis. Above one is aggregate paper profit and below one aggregate paper loss. It is a position relative to a cost basis, not a valuation and not a signal.

Assumptions

  • Realised capitalisation assumes a coin's last on-chain movement was a change of economic ownership at the market price of that moment. Exchange internal transfers, wallet consolidations and custodial restructuring all break that assumption by restamping coins that did not change owner.
  • Coins that are lost or permanently immobile keep an ancient cost basis and never leave the denominator, which biases realised capitalisation downward and the ratio upward.
  • Data providers attribute, cluster and filter transfers differently, so two published MVRV series for the same asset will not match exactly.
  • The supply definition matters: circulating, issued and liquid supply give different numerators and different realised prices, and the same definition must be used on both sides of the ratio.
  • The metric is defined on assets with a public transfer history. Supply held off chain, wrapped, or on a second layer is attributed by convention rather than observed directly.
Limits of the formula
  • The historical ranges people quote as overheated or undervalued come from a small number of completed cycles, so they describe a short sample rather than an estimated distribution.
  • It is a slow, aggregate measure. It says nothing about any individual holder, nothing about timing, and little about assets whose on-chain history is short.
  • A ratio below one means the aggregate cost basis is above the price; it does not mean the price cannot fall further, and historical analogies are not a forecast.

Commonly confused with

Net Unrealised Profit/Loss

Both are built from realised capitalisation. MVRV is a ratio of market value to cost basis; NUPL expresses the same gap as a share of market value, so the two move together but are read on different scales.

MVRV is a data-provider convention, not a standardised measure: realised capitalisation depends entirely on how a provider attributes on-chain movement, and lost coins and exchange internal transfers are known, unresolved distortions of the denominator. No numerical threshold is given for 'overheated' or 'undervalued' because the commonly quoted bands come from a handful of completed cycles and the registry holds no source that establishes them statistically.
