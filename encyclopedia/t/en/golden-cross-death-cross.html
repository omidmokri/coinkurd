Golden Cross and Death Cross

Foundation

Simple definition

A golden cross is when a shorter moving average rises above a longer one. A death cross is the reverse. Dramatic names for a change of direction that has already happened.

Professional definition

A crossover between two moving averages of different lengths, most often 50 and 200 periods on a daily chart. Both lines are averages of past prices, so the cross confirms a shift that began weeks or months earlier; the names carry no analytical content beyond the arithmetic.

Technical definition

A crossover is a sign change in the difference of two filters, which is itself a filter with a longer effective delay than either input. With 50 and 200 daily periods that delay is measured in months, so the event classifies the past rather than forecasting. Reported performance depends on the length pair, the averaging method, the asset and the sample, and the 50/200 pairing is inherited convention, not an optimised or theoretically derived choice.

Academic definition

A sign change in the difference between two unequal-length moving averages, conventionally read as a transition between trend states.

Mechanism

The faster average sits nearer recent prices and the slower one nearer older prices, so their gap measures how far the recent average has moved away from the older one. A cross is the moment that gap changes sign, which cannot happen until enough new prices have entered the short window to outweigh the long one. The delay is built into the arithmetic, not added by implementation.

In practice

Who uses it
Long-horizon trend followers, allocation committees wanting a simple regime label, and the financial press, which reports crosses as if they were news.
Why it matters
It produces one unambiguous, auditable regime label that needs no discretion and that two analysts will compute identically from the same data.
Limitations
Lagging by construction and badly whipsawed in ranges. The 50/200 pair has no special standing; other lengths change the results materially, and the name's popularity may itself affect behaviour.

Common mistakes

  • Entering at the cross as if it were news rather than a delayed summary.
  • Quoting back-tested cross results without stating the lengths used.
  • Applying it where 200 bars span only a few days.

Examples

market

On a daily chart the long average needs about two hundred sessions of history before it exists at all, and the cross typically prints long after the low. The signal is a label for a move already underway.

numeric

Yesterday the fast average was four tenths below the slow one; today it is three tenths above. The difference changed sign, so a golden cross is recorded today — using closes only, and after today's close is final.

Given
  • SMA(50) yesterday = 99.6, SMA(200) yesterday = 100.0
  • SMA(50) today = 100.3, SMA(200) today = 100.0
  • both averages fully seeded with 200 closes
Steps
  1. delta yesterday = 99.6 - 100.0 = -0.4
  2. delta today = 100.3 - 100.0 = +0.3
  3. sign changed from negative to positive
golden cross recorded on today's close

Evaluating the same rule intrabar would signal and unsignal repeatedly. A cross must be judged on confirmed closes or it repaints.

Mathematical foundation

\Delta(t) = MA_{f}(t) - MA_{s}(t), \qquad \text{golden cross at } t \iff \Delta(t) > 0 \ \wedge \ \Delta(t-1) \le 0

Variables

MA_f, MA_sFast and slow moving averages, conventionally 50 and 200 periodsprice units
\Delta(t)Spread between the two averages at period tprice units
tIndex of the confirmed bar being evaluatedbars

Procedure

Compute both averages with the same method on the same price series. Evaluate the spread only on confirmed closes. Record a golden cross on the first bar where the spread turns non-negative after being negative, and a death cross on the mirror condition.

Worked example

Given
  • delta(t-1) = -0.4
  • delta(t) = +0.3
Steps
  1. delta(t-1) <= 0 holds
  2. delta(t) > 0 holds
  3. both conditions met on bar t
golden cross at bar t

Interpretation

The spread is a slow measure of how far the recent average has pulled away from the older one. Its sign change says the two windows have swapped order — a classification of history, with no statement about what follows.

Assumptions

  • Both averages use the same averaging method. Mixing an SMA with an EMA changes the cross dates.
  • Both windows are fully seeded; the first 200 bars of any series produce an incomplete slow average and spurious early crosses.
  • Evaluation on confirmed closes. Intrabar evaluation makes the signal repaint and inflates any backtest.
Limits of the formula
  • The 50/200 pairing is convention. Results are sensitive to the pair chosen, so a single back-tested figure without the lengths is uninterpretable.
  • The rule is lagging by construction: it cannot signal before the averages have absorbed the move, and that is a property of averaging, not a tuning problem.
  • Evidence that crosses add value beyond simple trend exposure is mixed and sample-dependent.

Commonly confused with

Breakout

A breakout is price passing a level. A cross is one average passing another: no level is involved, and it can occur while price sits far from anything notable.
