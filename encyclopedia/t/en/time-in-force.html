Time in Force

Simple definition

The instruction saying how long your order stays alive: until you cancel it, for this instant only, all-or-nothing, or passive only.

Professional definition

A flag that bounds an order's lifetime and its matching behaviour. Good-till-cancelled rests until filled or pulled; immediate-or-cancel fills what it can at once and cancels the rest; fill-or-kill demands the whole quantity immediately or nothing; post-only rejects the order if it would take liquidity.

Technical definition

A set of venue-specific qualifiers rather than a standard. Common ones are good-till-cancelled, good-till-date, immediate-or-cancel, fill-or-kill, day, at-the-open and at-the-close, alongside behavioural flags such as post-only and reduce-only that are usually grouped with them. On most venues the only difference between immediate-or-cancel and fill-or-kill is that the first permits partial fills, which matters precisely when depth is thin. Names and exact semantics vary between venues.

Academic definition

An order qualifier specifying the interval over which an instruction remains executable and whether partial execution of the requested quantity is admissible.

Mechanism

A resting order is exposed: to adverse selection, to news, and to the trader simply forgetting it. Time in force is the control that limits that exposure in time rather than in price, which is why short-lived qualifiers are the natural tool around scheduled events and for sweeping depth without leaving a footprint.

In practice

Who uses it
Execution algorithms, event traders, API users, and anyone whose order must not survive the next minute.
Why it matters
It stops an order from becoming a standing liability. An immediate-or-cancel takes what is there and leaves no resting quantity for others to read.
Limitations
Labels are not standardised. Some venues cancel good-till-cancelled orders after a fixed number of days, and some expire everything at the session end.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming good-till-cancelled means forever.
  • Using fill-or-kill where immediate-or-cancel was meant and getting no fill.
  • Leaving resting orders across a contract roll or a session break.

Examples

market

An algorithm sweeps three venues with immediate-or-cancel orders. Each takes whatever is resting and disappears, so no venue is left displaying an order that would reveal the algorithm's remaining size.

numeric

An order for 500 meets 320 available at the limit. Immediate-or-cancel fills 320 and cancels 180. Fill-or-kill fills nothing: the same book, the same limit, a different outcome from one flag.

Given
  • order quantity 500 at a stated limit price
  • available quantity at or better than the limit: 320
  • two cases: immediate-or-cancel and fill-or-kill
Steps
  1. immediate-or-cancel: filled = min(500, 320) = 320; cancelled = 180
  2. fill-or-kill: 320 < 500, so the all-or-nothing condition fails; filled = 0
320 filled under immediate-or-cancel, 0 under fill-or-kill

On a few venues these labels are implemented differently, so confirm the behaviour in the venue's own documentation before relying on it.

Commonly confused with

Partial Fill

A partial fill is an outcome: less traded than requested. Time in force is the instruction that decides what happens to the remainder — rest, cancel, or never allow the partial fill at all.
