Correlation Risk

Simple definition

The risk that holdings you thought were different all fall together. Diversification only reduces risk while the things you hold behave differently, and in a crisis they often stop.

Professional definition

The exposure created when the dependence between positions is higher than the portfolio was built to assume, or rises when it matters most. It has two distinct forms: positions that were always driven by one common factor, and positions whose historically low correlation increases in stressed markets.

Technical definition

In the two-asset case, portfolio variance carries a cross term of twice the product of the weights, the two volatilities and the correlation coefficient, so the correlation enters the risk figure directly. Because the coefficient is estimated from a finite window, it is both uncertain and non-stationary; measured dependence in the tails is typically stronger than the full-sample coefficient suggests, so a model calibrated on average conditions understates joint losses.

Academic definition

The sensitivity of portfolio risk to the dependence structure among its constituents, including parameter uncertainty in estimated correlations and the empirical tendency of dependence to increase in the lower tail of the joint return distribution.

Mechanism

Diversification works through the cross term in portfolio variance: when correlation is low, the individual volatilities partly cancel. Raise the correlation and that cancellation disappears, leaving the weighted sum of the volatilities. Nothing about the positions changed; only the parameter did. In stressed markets the same selling pressure hits many assets at once, which is exactly when the parameter rises.

In practice

Who uses it
Portfolio managers checking whether allocations are genuinely distinct, risk teams running stress scenarios, and traders holding several positions in one sector.
Why it matters
It is the reason a book of many positions can behave like one large position, and the reason position counts are a poor measure of diversification.
Limitations
Correlation is estimated, window-dependent and unstable. A single coefficient also misses non-linear dependence, where assets move together only in the tails.

Common mistakes

  • Counting positions instead of measuring factor exposure.
  • Using a long-sample correlation to size risk for a crisis.
  • Treating a hedge as complete when the hedge ratio was derived from a calm period.

Examples

numeric

Two equally weighted assets with volatilities of 40 and 60 percent. At zero correlation the portfolio volatility is 36.1 percent. At a correlation of 0.9 it is 48.8 percent — a 35 percent increase in risk from one parameter.

Given
  • w1 = w2 = 0.5
  • sigma1 = 0.40, sigma2 = 0.60
  • rho = 0 in the first case, 0.9 in the second
Steps
  1. w1^2 sigma1^2 = 0.25 x 0.16 = 0.040
  2. w2^2 sigma2^2 = 0.25 x 0.36 = 0.090
  3. rho = 0: variance = 0.130, volatility = 0.361
  4. cross term at rho = 0.9: 2 x 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.9 x 0.40 x 0.60 = 0.108
  5. rho = 0.9: variance = 0.238, volatility = 0.488
Portfolio volatility rises from 36.1 to 48.8 percent.

Neither asset changed. The same two positions are a materially different portfolio under the two correlation assumptions.

crypto

A book of ten different tokens can behave as one position: when the dominant asset falls sharply, broad token selling tends to follow, and the diversification the ten names appeared to provide is not present in that move.

Mathematical foundation

\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho_{12} \sigma_1 \sigma_2

Variables

\sigma_pPortfolio volatilityfraction per period
w_1, w_2Portfolio weights of the two assetsfraction of portfolio
\sigma_1, \sigma_2Volatilities of the two assetsfraction per period
\rho_{12}Correlation coefficient between the two return seriesbetween -1 and 1

Procedure

Estimate each asset's volatility and the pairwise correlation over the same window and the same return frequency. Square and weight the individual variances, add the cross term, then take the square root for portfolio volatility. Repeat under a stressed correlation assumption, for example 0.9 or 1.0, to see how much of the diversification benefit is conditional on the estimate holding.

Worked example

Given
  • w1 = w2 = 0.5
  • sigma1 = 0.40, sigma2 = 0.60
  • case A: rho = 0; case B: rho = 0.9
Steps
  1. own terms = 0.25 x 0.16 + 0.25 x 0.36 = 0.040 + 0.090 = 0.130
  2. case A variance = 0.130, so sigma_p = 0.361
  3. cross term B = 2 x 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.9 x 0.40 x 0.60 = 0.108
  4. case B variance = 0.130 + 0.108 = 0.238, so sigma_p = 0.488
36.1 percent volatility at zero correlation, 48.8 percent at 0.9.

Interpretation

The gap between the two figures is the diversification benefit, and it is entirely conditional on the correlation estimate. Correlation risk is the size of that gap: the more a portfolio's risk figure depends on low correlation, the more it will surprise when correlation rises.

Assumptions

  • Volatilities and the correlation are estimated from the same window at the same return frequency. Mixing daily and weekly estimates produces an inconsistent figure.
  • The correlation coefficient captures the dependence. It measures linear dependence only, and says little about joint behaviour in the tails.
  • Weights are fixed over the horizon. Rebalancing or drift changes the computed risk.
  • The two-asset formula generalises to many assets only through the full covariance matrix, where the number of estimated parameters grows roughly with the square of the asset count.
Limits of the formula
  • Estimated correlations are unstable and tend to rise during stress, which is when the diversification assumption is being relied on.
  • A low full-sample coefficient is compatible with strong joint losses in the worst periods, so the formula can understate crisis losses even when correctly computed.
  • Correlation says nothing about causation or about the common factor driving both series; identifying that factor usually matters more than the coefficient.

Commonly confused with

Concentration Risk

Concentration risk is about how much weight sits in one place. Correlation risk is about whether different places move together. A portfolio can be perfectly spread by weight and still carry one bet, which is correlation risk, not concentration.
