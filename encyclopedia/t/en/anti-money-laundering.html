Simple definition

The whole set of rules a regulated firm must follow to notice money that may come from crime, keep records of it, and report it to the authorities.

Professional definition

A supervisory programme rather than a single check: enterprise risk assessment, customer due diligence, ongoing transaction monitoring, record keeping, sanctions screening, suspicious-activity reporting, staff training and an accountable compliance officer. Identification is one input; detection and reporting are the output.

Technical definition

Obligations fall on obliged entities defined by activity, so the perimeter, not the asset, decides who is caught. Monitoring is risk-based and behavioural: typologies, thresholds and deviation from an expected profile generate alerts, which humans triage before any report is filed. On-chain analytics extend monitoring to counterparty exposure, but attribution is probabilistic and false positives dominate alert volume.

Academic definition

The body of supervisory requirements obliging financial intermediaries to assess and monitor financial-crime risk, maintain records, and report suspicious activity to a competent authority.

Mechanism

Supervisors cannot watch every transfer, so they conscript the firms that can. The duty is designed as a process obligation: a firm is penalised for an inadequate programme even where no criminal money is ever found. That shifts the incentive from catching crime to documenting diligence, which explains both the volume of low-value alerts and the practice of de-risking whole customer categories.

In practice

Who uses it
Compliance and monitoring teams, supervisors and auditors, exchange and bank operations, and customers whose transfers are queried.
Why it matters
It sets what a firm must watch for, what it must keep, and when it must tell a public authority without telling the customer.
Limitations
The programme detects patterns, not guilt. Alert quality is poor, attribution on-chain is probabilistic, and the obligations differ by jurisdiction and change.

Common mistakes

  • Reading an alert or a frozen transfer as a finding that a crime occurred.
  • Assuming a decentralised interface puts an operator outside the supervisory perimeter.
  • Treating blockchain analytics labels as proven attribution rather than probabilistic inference.

Examples

regulatory

Two firms run the same business from different countries. One must file reports to a financial intelligence unit and keep records for years; the other sits outside the local perimeter entirely. The activity is identical; the duty is not.

numeric

A monitoring system raises 10,000 alerts a month. If 97 per cent are cleared as false positives, 300 reach deeper review and perhaps 30 become reports. Most compliance effort is spent clearing noise.

Given
  • 10,000 alerts per month
  • False-positive clearance rate = 97%
  • Of the remainder, 10% are escalated to a filed report
Steps
  1. Alerts surviving triage = 10,000 x 0.03 = 300
  2. Reports filed = 300 x 0.10 = 30
  3. Share of alerts becoming a report = 30 / 10,000 = 0.3%
300 deeper reviews and about 30 reports from 10,000 alerts

These rates are illustrative, not measured. Published false-positive rates vary widely by firm and system, and no figure from the source registry is being quoted.

Commonly confused with

Know Your Customer

Know Your Customer answers who the customer is. Anti-money laundering asks what their activity looks like over time and what must be reported.

Commonly confused with

Sanctions Screening

Sanctions screening is a list-matching duty with strict liability in many states. Anti-money laundering is a risk-based judgement about behaviour.
