Average True Range

Simple definition

A measure of how far a market typically travels in one bar, gaps included. Bigger numbers mean wider swings. It says nothing about which way price is going.

Professional definition

Wilder's volatility measure: the smoothed average of true range, where true range is the largest of the bar's high-to-low distance, the distance from the previous close up to this high, and the distance from the previous close down to this low. Default lookback 14.

Technical definition

True range is the maximum of three quantities: high minus low, the absolute distance from the previous close to the high, and the absolute distance from the previous close to the low — so a gap through the previous close is counted rather than ignored. ATR applies Wilder's smoothing to that series: the first value is a simple mean of n true ranges, then each later value is (previous x (n-1) + current) / n. Output is in price units, never negative, and direction-free.

Academic definition

A recursively smoothed estimator of per-period price range that incorporates overnight discontinuity by taking the maximum of the intraperiod range and the two gap-adjusted ranges measured from the prior close.

Mechanism

Range counts the whole distance travelled inside the bar, which close-to-close change does not, and the two previous-close terms stop a gap from being invisible. Wilder's smoothing gives every past bar a declining weight that never reaches zero, so ATR jumps quickly on a volatility shock and then decays slowly, instead of stepping back the moment one wide bar leaves a fixed window.

In practice

Who uses it
Risk managers and systematic traders setting stop distance, position size and volatility filters, and anyone comparing two instruments on one scale.
Why it matters
It turns the question how far is far into a number in price units that updates with the market, which makes stops and sizing coherent across instruments and regimes.
Limitations
A magnitude, not a direction or a probability. It is in price units, so raw ATR cannot be compared between instruments until it is divided by price.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a rising ATR as a bullish or bearish signal.
  • Comparing ATR across instruments without dividing by price.
  • Smoothing true range with an SMA and calling it Wilder's ATR.

Examples

market

After a gap-down open, the close-to-close change looks modest while true range is large, because the gap enters the calculation. A stop placed from the session high and low alone would have sat inside the day's noise.

numeric

Today's high is 105 and low 99, but the previous close was 108. True range is 9, set by the gap rather than by the intraday range, and a 14-period ATR of 7.00 updates to 7.14.

Given
  • n = 14
  • previous ATR = 7.00
  • today: high = 105, low = 99
  • previous close = 108
Steps
  1. high - low = 6
  2. |high - previous close| = |105 - 108| = 3
  3. |low - previous close| = |99 - 108| = 9
  4. TR = max(6, 3, 9) = 9
  5. ATR = (7.00 x 13 + 9) / 14 = 100 / 14
ATR = 7.14 price units

The gap, not the intraday range, set today's true range. ATR rose because the bar was wider than average, not because price fell.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

For each bar compute the three distances and take the largest as true range. Seed the average with a simple mean of the first n true ranges, then update recursively: multiply the previous ATR by n-1, add today's true range, divide by n.

Worked example

Given
  • n = 14
  • previous ATR = 7.00
  • high = 105, low = 99, previous close = 108
Steps
  1. candidates: 6, 3, 9
  2. TR = 9
  3. (7.00 x 13) + 9 = 100
  4. 100 / 14
ATR = 7.14

Interpretation

The output is the typical distance one bar covers, in the instrument's own price units. Divide it by price for a percentage figure that can be compared between instruments.

Assumptions

  • Wilder's recursive smoothing, seeded with a simple mean of the first n true ranges. An exponential or simple average of true range is a different series, although platforms often still label it ATR.
  • Absolute values are used for the two gap terms, so true range is at least the high-low distance and never negative.
  • Wilder used n = 14; other lookbacks are configurations, not corrections, and ATR values from different lookbacks are not interchangeable.
Limits of the formula
  • Backward-looking: it records volatility that has already happened and lags a regime change by roughly the lookback.
  • Scale-dependent, because the output carries price units; one very wide bar can hold it elevated for many periods.
  • It measures range only. Two instruments with the same ATR can have completely different trend behaviour.

Related terminology

Commonly confused with

Volatility

ATR is a range-based estimate in price units. Volatility in the statistical sense is the standard deviation of returns, a dimensionless rate that can be annualised. They rank calm and wild periods similarly but are not the same quantity.
