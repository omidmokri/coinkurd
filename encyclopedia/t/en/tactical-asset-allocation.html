Tactical Asset Allocation

Simple definition

Temporary, deliberate shifts away from a portfolio's long-term target weights, taken to express a view on the next few months or quarters and reversed afterwards.

Professional definition

Short- to medium-horizon deviations from strategic weights, taken inside pre-agreed ranges and charged against an explicit risk budget. Its result is measured as the return of the tilted portfolio minus the return of the policy portfolio, so the activity carries its own separable track record.

Technical definition

An active overlay on policy weights. Its contribution is the sum over classes of active weight times class return, and its risk is the tracking error of the active weight vector against the policy benchmark. Because deviations are capped by the permitted ranges, tactical allocation cannot change a portfolio's dominant exposures, only tilt them. Evidence that allocation timing adds return net of costs is mixed, which is one reason mandates bound it rather than leave it open.

Academic definition

An active deviation from policy weights, evaluated as the realised covariance of active weights with subsequent class returns and charged against a tracking-error budget.

Mechanism

A tactical position pays only if the forecast behind it is better than the market's and the deviation is large enough to matter after costs. Those two requirements pull against each other: small tilts are cheap but cannot move the result, while large tilts can, and reintroduce exactly the concentrated risk the policy was built to bound.

In practice

Who uses it
Multi-asset managers, allocation committees, and discretionary managers whose mandate permits bounded deviations from policy.
Why it matters
It separates a view about the coming quarters from the long-run plan, so the view can be sized, limited, logged and later judged on its own record.
Limitations
Allocation timing needs both the right direction and the right timing. Transaction costs, taxes and the permitted ranges all cut the achievable payoff.

Common mistakes

  • Letting a tilt become the new policy because it happened to work.
  • Not recording the policy portfolio, so the tilt's contribution cannot be measured.
  • Sizing a tilt until it dominates total portfolio risk.

Examples

market

A committee running a 60/40 policy with plus or minus 10 point ranges cuts equities to 52% on a deteriorating growth view. The decision is logged with a review date, so three months later its contribution can be separated from the policy portfolio's own return.

numeric

Against a 60/40 policy, a manager holds 52/48. Over the quarter equities fall 6% and bonds gain 1%. The tilt contributed about 0.56% gross, 0.46% after a 0.10% round-trip cost, measured purely against the policy portfolio.

Given
  • policy: equities 60%, bonds 40%
  • tactical: equities 52%, bonds 48%
  • quarter returns: equities -6%, bonds +1%
  • round-trip cost of implementing and unwinding the tilt: 0.10% of portfolio
Steps
  1. active weights: equities -8 pp, bonds +8 pp
  2. equity contribution = -0.08 x (-6%) = +0.48%
  3. bond contribution = +0.08 x (+1%) = +0.08%
  4. gross active return = +0.56%
  5. net of cost = 0.56% - 0.10% = +0.46%
+0.46% relative to the policy portfolio

The arithmetic is symmetric: had equities risen 6% instead, the same tilt would have cost about 0.40% gross and 0.50% net. A tilt must be judged over many such decisions, not one.

Mathematical foundation

R_p - R_b = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (w_i - b_i)\,R_{b,i} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (w_i - b_i)\,\left(R_{b,i} - R_b\right)

Variables

w_iPortfolio weight in class i at the start of the periodfraction of portfolio
b_iPolicy (benchmark) weight in class ifraction of portfolio
R_{b,i}Return of class i over the period% per period
R_bReturn of the policy portfolio, the weighted sum of class returns at policy weights% per period
R_pReturn of the actual, tilted portfolio% per period

Procedure

Record the policy weights before the period. At the end, take each class's active weight as portfolio weight minus policy weight, multiply by that class's return, and sum. Because the active weights sum to zero, subtracting the policy return from every class return gives the same total, which is why a tilt can add value in a falling market.

Worked example

Given
  • policy weights b = (0.60, 0.40)
  • portfolio weights w = (0.52, 0.48)
  • class returns R_b = (-6%, +1%)
Steps
  1. active weights w - b = (-0.08, +0.08)
  2. direct form: (-0.08)(-6%) + (0.08)(1%) = 0.48% + 0.08%
  3. policy return R_b = 0.60(-6%) + 0.40(1%) = -3.2%
  4. centred form: (-0.08)(-6% + 3.2%) + (0.08)(1% + 3.2%) = 0.224% + 0.336%
both forms give +0.56% active return before costs

Interpretation

The figure is the portfolio's return minus what the policy would have returned, attributable to weights alone. It is not profit: a positive active return in a quarter when the policy lost 3.2% still means money was lost.

Assumptions

  • Single period, weights measured at period start, no intra-period cash flows or trading.
  • Each class is held at the benchmark class return, so the expression isolates the allocation effect and contains no selection effect; a full Brinson-type attribution separates the two.
  • Active weights sum to zero, which requires the portfolio and the policy to cover the same opportunity set.
Limits of the formula
  • One period's active return says nothing about skill; the quantity of interest is its mean and standard deviation over many decisions.
  • Transaction costs, taxes and financing of the tilt sit outside the identity and must be subtracted separately.

Commonly confused with

Rebalancing

Rebalancing moves weights back toward the policy. Tactical allocation deliberately moves them away from it. They run in opposite directions, and conflating them hides an active bet inside a maintenance trade.
