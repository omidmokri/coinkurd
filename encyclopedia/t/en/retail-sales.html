Simple definition

How much money households spent at shops and online over a month. It is counted in currency, so a rise can mean more goods bought or the same goods at higher prices.

Professional definition

A monthly survey estimate of the value of sales by retail establishments, published in nominal terms and usually by category. Analysts strip out volatile components such as motor vehicles and fuel to get a core measure closer to the trend in discretionary household spending.

Technical definition

A nominal value series, not a volume series: it must be deflated by a matching price index before it says anything about real consumption. It covers goods retailing plus food services in some designs, excludes most services, and is revised as late returns arrive. Seasonal and holiday-timing effects are large, which is why month-on-month changes are quoted on a seasonally adjusted basis.

Academic definition

A monthly establishment-survey estimate of nominal turnover in the retail trade sector, used as a high-frequency proxy for goods consumption pending national accounts data.

Mechanism

Household spending is the largest component of output in most economies, and retail sales arrive weeks before the national accounts do, which is why markets watch them. The cost of that speed is that the series is nominal, narrow and noisy: price change, a single holiday shift or one large category can dominate a month.

In practice

Who uses it
Macro analysts nowcasting consumption, consumer-sector equity analysts, and rates traders positioned around monthly data.
Why it matters
It is the earliest broad read on household demand, and the core or control measure feeds directly into consumption nowcasts used before official output data.
Limitations
It is nominal, covers goods far better than services, and a single month's change is often smaller than its own revision and sampling error.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a nominal rise real growth when prices rose as much.
  • Reading the headline while the core measure moved the other way.
  • Comparing a non-adjusted month with an adjusted one.

Examples

numeric

Nominal retail sales rise 4 percent over a year while the matching goods price index rises 4 percent. Real sales are roughly flat: households spent more money on the same quantity of goods.

Given
  • nominal sales growth = +4.0% year over year
  • matching goods price index growth = +4.0%
  • exact deflation, not subtraction
Steps
  1. real growth = (1.04 / 1.04) - 1
  2. = 0
real retail sales growth = 0%

Growth rates are invented for the arithmetic. Subtracting the two rates gives the same answer here only because they are equal; in general the exact ratio differs from the difference.

everyday

A household's grocery bill grows every month while the trolley holds the same items. The shop's sales figures rise, the family's consumption does not, and only a deflated series can tell the two apart.

Commonly confused with

Gross Domestic Product

Retail sales are nominal turnover in one sector of one month. GDP is total production over a quarter, measured in both nominal and real terms. One is a fast proxy, the other the accounting aggregate.
