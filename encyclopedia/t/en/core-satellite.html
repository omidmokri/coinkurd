Simple definition

A structure that keeps most of a portfolio in one broad, low-cost core holding and a smaller part in separate, deliberately different positions called satellites.

Professional definition

A portfolio architecture that separates implementation from active risk. The core supplies the policy exposure cheaply, usually by index tracking, while the satellites carry the intended deviations. Because active risk is confined to a known fraction of capital, total tracking error against policy can be budgeted directly.

Technical definition

If the core tracks the policy benchmark closely, nearly all active risk originates in the satellites, and total tracking error scales with the satellite share and the satellites' own tracking errors, reduced further where the satellites are mutually uncorrelated. The structure makes fees and active risk legible, because both are attributable to a stated share of capital. It does not reduce active risk by itself: a small satellite carrying leverage can dominate, and a satellite that duplicates the core destroys the separation the structure is built on.

Academic definition

A two-tier architecture in which a benchmark-replicating core carries policy exposure and a bounded satellite allocation carries active risk, permitting explicit budgeting of tracking error and cost.

Mechanism

Both fees and active risk are proportional to the capital exposed to them. Putting the policy exposure into a cheap, closely tracking core means the rest of the portfolio is the only place either can arise, so both become one explicit decision about how much capital goes there rather than a diffuse property of the whole.

In practice

Who uses it
Advisers and institutions that want policy exposure cheaply while keeping a defined, limited space for active positions.
Why it matters
It makes a portfolio's cost and its active risk attributable to a named fraction of capital, which can then be capped, reported and reviewed.
Limitations
The split is only as honest as the core's tracking. A core that drifts, or satellites that duplicate it, remove the separation the structure depends on.

Common mistakes

  • Letting satellites grow until they drive total portfolio risk.
  • Choosing satellites that hold much of what the core already holds.
  • Quoting the core's low fee as the whole portfolio's cost.

Examples

market

A fund keeps 85% in a broad index tracker and 15% split between two specialist mandates. Both the fee schedule and the portfolio's deviation from policy are then attributable to that 15%, so a cap on the satellite share caps both at once.

numeric

A core of 80% tracks policy almost exactly. One satellite of 20% with 8% tracking error gives the portfolio 1.60% tracking error; two uncorrelated satellites of 10% each with the same 8% give 1.13%.

Given
  • core 80% of capital, tracking error against policy taken as zero
  • satellite budget 20% of capital
  • case A: one satellite with 8% tracking error
  • case B: two satellites of 10% each with 8% tracking error, uncorrelated with each other
Steps
  1. case A: 0.20 x 8% = 1.60%
  2. case B: each contributes 0.10 x 8% = 0.80%
  3. case B total = sqrt(0.80%^2 + 0.80%^2) = 0.80% x 1.414 = 1.13%
1.60% portfolio tracking error in case A against 1.13% in case B

Splitting the same satellite budget across uncorrelated positions lowers total active risk for the same capital at risk. Correlated satellites give that reduction back, and the zero-correlation assumption here is an idealisation.

Commonly confused with

Portfolio of Strategies

A portfolio of strategies combines several active programmes with no passive anchor. Core-satellite deliberately keeps one benchmark-tracking tier, so that active risk has a fixed, bounded home.
