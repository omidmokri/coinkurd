Simple definition

An attacker sends you a transaction from an address that looks almost identical to one you use, hoping you later copy theirs out of your history and send funds to them.

Professional definition

A misdirection attack on the wallet's transaction history. The attacker generates an address whose first and last characters match a counterparty you transact with, then creates a tiny or zero-value transfer so it appears in your list; a later payment copied from that list goes to the attacker, irreversibly.

Technical definition

It exploits two habits: verifying an address by its first and last characters, and treating wallet history as an address book. Vanity generation makes matching those visible ends cheap, while matching the whole string is infeasible. The defences are procedural: send only to an entry in your own saved address book or a freshly confirmed source, verify the full string or a checksum rather than the ends, confirm the destination on a hardware device screen, and send a small test amount first to any new counterparty.

Academic definition

A deception technique that seeds a visually similar address into a victim's transaction history, exploiting partial-string verification habits to misdirect a subsequent transfer.

Mechanism

Transfers are final and addresses are unreadable, so people substitute a cheap check for a hard one: compare the ends. An attacker who can mint addresses freely only has to satisfy that cheap check once, and the wallet's own history does the rest of the work by placing their address beside a legitimate one.

In practice

Who uses it
Anyone who sends funds by copying an address, payroll and operations teams, and the designers of wallet interfaces.
Why it matters
The loss is total for the amount sent and cannot be reversed, so the only effective controls are habits applied before signing.
Limitations
Nothing on-chain stops an attacker from sending you a transaction. Filtering and warnings help, but the last check is always the user's.

Common mistakes

  • Copying an address out of transaction history instead of a saved contact.
  • Checking only the first and last few characters.
  • Sending a large first payment to a new counterparty with no test transfer.

Examples

numeric

A 40-character hex address has 16 possibilities per character. Matching the first four and last four means searching about 16 to the power of 8, roughly 4.3 billion candidates — cheap. Matching all 40 is not.

Given
  • Address body = 40 hexadecimal characters
  • Characters compared by eye = first 4 and last 4
  • Each hex character has 16 values
Steps
  1. Candidates for an 8-character match = 16^8 = 4,294,967,296
  2. Candidates for a full match = 16^40, which is beyond any search budget
An 8-character lookalike is cheap to generate; a full-string match is not

The arithmetic is for an account-style hex address. Other formats differ in length and encoding, and some carry a checksum, which is why checking the checksum beats checking the ends.

everyday

A bank transfer typed from a near-identical account number in an old email thread, instead of from the supplier's confirmed details. Same failure, older plumbing, and the same fix: confirm the destination out of band.

Commonly confused with

Dusting Attack

Both arrive as unsolicited transfers. Dusting wants to link your addresses to each other; poisoning wants your next payment to go to the wrong one.
