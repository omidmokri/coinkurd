Simple definition

A public network that runs programs, not just payments. Its native asset, ether, pays for the computation, and most tokens you meet in crypto are contracts deployed on it or on a network like it.

Professional definition

A programmable blockchain with a shared execution environment, the EVM, in which smart contracts hold state and are called by transactions. Ether pays gas fees and, since the move to proof-of-stake, is staked by validators who propose and attest blocks. Fees are split into a protocol-burned base fee and a priority tip.

Technical definition

An account-model ledger rather than a UTXO one: contracts and externally owned accounts both carry persistent state, which is what makes composability possible and also makes one contract's bug another's exposure. Consensus is proof-of-stake with attestation-based finality, so finality is explicit rather than purely probabilistic. Net issuance is the difference between validator rewards and the burned base fee, so supply change depends on network demand rather than on a fixed schedule.

Academic definition

A replicated state machine with a quasi-Turing-complete execution environment, in which computation is metered by gas, consensus is secured by bonded stake, and the native asset serves simultaneously as fee currency and as security collateral.

Mechanism

Gas prices computation so that a shared machine cannot be monopolised by an expensive program: every operation has a cost, and the sender bids for inclusion. Staking replaces hash power with bonded capital, so attacking the chain means risking the asset rather than buying machines. Burning the base fee links supply directly to usage, which is why issuance can be positive in quiet periods and negative in busy ones.

In practice

Who uses it
Contract developers, validators and staking services, DeFi users paying gas, token issuers choosing a deployment chain, and analysts tracking fee revenue.
Why it matters
It is the main settlement layer for tokens, stablecoins and DeFi, so its fee market and finality rules shape how most of that activity behaves.
Limitations
Base-layer throughput is limited and fees are demand-driven, pushing activity to layer 2; contract risk is borne by users, not by the protocol.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the asset ether with tokens issued on Ethereum.
  • Reading a gas price in gwei as a total transaction cost.
  • Assuming a contract is safe because the chain is.

Examples

numeric

A transfer using 21,000 gas at a 30 gwei base fee costs 630,000 gwei, which is 0.00063 ether. The base-fee portion is burned rather than paid to a validator.

Given
  • gas used = 21,000
  • base fee = 30 gwei per gas
  • 1 ether = 10^9 gwei
  • priority tip excluded for clarity
Steps
  1. 21,000 x 30 = 630,000 gwei
  2. 630,000 / 1,000,000,000 = 0.00063 ether
0.00063 ether in base fee, burned

Gas prices move with demand, so the gwei figure here is illustrative only. A real cost also includes the priority tip paid to the block proposer.

crypto

A stablecoin, a lending market and a DEX can call each other inside one transaction because they share the same state machine. That composability is the platform's main advantage and the reason failures propagate across protocols.

Commonly confused with

Token versus Coin

Ether is the chain's own asset and pays for computation. Tokens on Ethereum are contract balances that cannot pay gas themselves; they depend on ether for every transfer.

Describes protocol design only. The 30 gwei base fee in the numeric example is explicitly illustrative, not an observed figure, and no price, supply or market-size number is given. 'Quasi-Turing-complete' is used deliberately: execution is bounded by the gas limit. Net issuance being demand-dependent follows from EIP-1559 burning combined with staking rewards and is stated as a mechanism, not as a forecast about whether supply rises or falls.
