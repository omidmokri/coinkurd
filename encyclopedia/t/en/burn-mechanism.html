Simple definition

A rule that permanently removes units from supply, by sending them where nobody can spend them or by deleting them outright. Burning reduces how many units exist; it does not add value from nowhere.

Professional definition

A protocol or contract function that destroys tokens irreversibly, either by transferring them to an address with no known key or by reducing total supply in the contract's accounting. Burns may be discretionary, scheduled, or automatic and usage-linked, as with a protocol fee that is destroyed rather than paid to a validator.

Technical definition

Economically, a burn is a distribution to remaining holders financed by whoever paid the burned units: it is closer to a share buyback than to a dividend, and it transfers nothing unless the units burned were genuinely paid for. Usage-linked burns couple net supply change to demand, so supply can fall in busy periods and rise in quiet ones. Verification matters: burning to an unspendable address leaves the units visible on-chain forever, whereas a supply reduction in contract accounting depends on the contract being what it claims.

Academic definition

An irreversible supply-reduction mechanism in which units are rendered permanently unspendable, redistributing proportional claims among remaining holders at the expense of whoever bore the cost of the destroyed units.

Mechanism

Removing units raises each remaining holder's proportional share, and that is the whole effect: the mechanism redistributes claims, it does not create cash flow. So a burn financed by real revenue is materially different from a burn of units the issuer never sold, which changes a supply statistic and nothing else. Usage-linked burns are the strongest version because the units destroyed were paid for by users seeking the network's service.

In practice

Who uses it
Protocol designers setting fee policy, analysts computing net issuance, treasury committees deciding buy-and-burn, and holders assessing whether a burn is funded by revenue.
Why it matters
It is the only lever that reduces supply, so it is the counterweight to emission when a design wants net issuance near or below zero.
Limitations
A burn redistributes; it does not create value. Burning unsold inventory changes a statistic only, and burns can be announced, delayed or reversed in policy if governance allows.

Common mistakes

  • Treating any burn as inherently bullish regardless of what funded it.
  • Counting burned units as circulating supply in a market-value figure.
  • Confusing a burn with a lock, which can be undone.

Examples

numeric

Validator rewards issue 900,000 units in a period while fees burn 1,100,000. Net supply change is minus 200,000: supply fell because usage was high, not because anyone bought the token.

Given
  • gross issuance in the period = 900,000 units
  • units burned in the period = 1,100,000 units
  • no other supply changes
Steps
  1. net change = 900,000 - 1,100,000
  2. = -200,000 units
net supply change of minus 200,000 units for the period

Hypothetical figures. Net issuance of this kind is demand-dependent, so the sign can flip between periods and a single period proves nothing about a trend.

comparison

A protocol burns fees users actually paid. Another burns tokens from its own unsold allocation. The first is financed by revenue; the second only lowers a number the issuer controls.

Mathematical foundation

\Delta S = I - B

Procedure

Measure gross issuance and burns over the same window from the same source, then subtract. Report the window: net issuance over an hour, a day and a year can carry different signs when burning is usage-linked.

Worked example

Given
  • I = 900,000 units issued in the period
  • B = 1,100,000 units burned in the period
Steps
  1. 900,000 - 1,100,000 = -200,000
net supply change = -200,000 units

Interpretation

A negative result means supply shrank over the window measured; it is not a statement about price, demand or the following period. It is an accounting identity, not a model.

Assumptions

  • Issuance and burns are measured over the same window and from a consistent source.
  • Burned units are genuinely unrecoverable. Sending to an address with an unknown key is treated as a burn by convention, not by proof that no key exists.
  • Lost or inaccessible coins are not burns: they reduce usable supply without appearing in B.
Limits of the formula
  • The identity describes supply only. It implies nothing about value, and a negative net issuance does not make an asset deflationary in purchasing-power terms.
  • Where burning is usage-linked, the sign of the result depends on the measurement window, so any single figure needs its window stated.

Commonly confused with

Token Unlock

A burn removes units from supply permanently. An unlock moves units from locked into tradable supply. One is irreversible and reduces the count; the other is scheduled and raises the float.
