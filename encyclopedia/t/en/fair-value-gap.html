Fair Value Gap

Simple definition

A band of price skipped in the middle of a three-candle burst, where the market moved so fast that one range was left behind. Traders expect price to come back and trade through it.

Professional definition

A three-candle imbalance: in the bullish case the low of the third candle sits above the high of the first, so the middle candle's range was covered with one-sided trade. The band between those two levels is marked as a zone and watched for a later return, usually called a fill or a rebalance.

Technical definition

Bullish condition: the low of candle t+1 exceeds the high of candle t-1, and the gap is the band between those two levels; bearish is the mirror image. Variants add a minimum width — absolute, a percentage of price, or a multiple of ATR — and disagree on whether a fill needs a touch of the near edge, the midpoint, or full closure. On a continuously traded venue this measures candle non-overlap, not a true gap in the tape.

Academic definition

A three-bar non-overlap condition identifying a price band traversed without two-sided trading, employed in practitioner frameworks as a mean-reversion target and lacking an established evidence base.

Mechanism

A fast directional move prints bars whose ranges do not overlap, and the skipped band is simply where little two-sided trade occurred. Whether price returns there is the open question: on a continuous venue every level inside the band did trade at least once in passing, so unfilled means an absence of two-sided activity, not a hole in the tape. The displacement-and-rebalance story is an interpretation of that shape.

In practice

Who uses it
Price-action and smart-money-concepts traders, and developers coding imbalance detection for entries or for chart annotation.
Why it matters
The condition is purely arithmetic on three bars, so it is unambiguous to compute and gives an objective zone with defined edges.
Limitations
Width filters and fill criteria vary between teachers, so counts of gaps and of fills are not comparable. No peer-reviewed evidence shows that unfilled bands attract price.

Common mistakes

  • Marking every tiny non-overlap as a tradable zone.
  • Calling a gap filled on a wick touch of the far edge.
  • Assuming all gaps eventually fill, so no stop is needed.

Examples

market

A funding-rate flush drives three candles up in a straight line. The middle one leaves a band that the next session trades back into, and traders who had marked it treat the return as an entry area rather than as weakness.

numeric

Candle one has a high of 100.0, candle three a low of 101.5. The bullish band runs from 100.0 to 101.5 — 1.5 wide, about 1.5 percent of price. With a filter of half an ATR of 2.0, the threshold is 1.0, so this gap qualifies.

Given
  • candle t-1 high = 100.0
  • candle t+1 low = 101.5
  • ATR = 2.0
  • width filter = 0.5 x ATR
Steps
  1. condition: 101.5 > 100.0, so a bullish gap exists
  2. band = 100.0 to 101.5, width = 1.5
  3. relative width = 1.5 / 100.75 = 1.49%
  4. threshold = 0.5 x 2.0 = 1.0, and 1.5 > 1.0
A qualifying bullish band from 100.0 to 101.5, midpoint 100.75.

Change the filter to one full ATR and the same gap is discarded. The filter is part of the definition, not a detail.

Commonly confused with

Candlestick

A single candle shows where price traded in one period. A fair value gap is inferred from three candles at once: it is the band between two of their extremes, and no single candle displays it.

No source in this registry documents the smart-money-concepts literature; the cited works cover classical gap analysis and the general standard of evidence only. Definitions differ on zone edges, minimum width and what counts as a fill, and the claim that institutional flow returns to rebalance such bands is an interpretation of candles, not an observation of order flow.
