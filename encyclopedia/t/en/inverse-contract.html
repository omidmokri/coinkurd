Inverse Contract

Simple definition

A contract priced in dollars but paid for and settled in the coin itself. Because the coin's own value moves, the profit and loss in coin terms is not a straight line.

Professional definition

A derivative quoted in the quote currency, usually USD, while margin, profit and loss and settlement are all denominated in the base asset. Notional is a fixed amount of quote currency per contract, so coin-denominated profit and loss depends on the reciprocal of price, not on price itself.

Technical definition

A contract whose payoff is linear in 1/P rather than in P. For a long of Q units of quote-currency notional, coin profit is Q(1/P_entry - 1/P_exit). The function is convex in price, so in coin terms a given percentage fall costs more than the same percentage rise earns, and collateral is itself the depreciating asset when the price falls. At equal nominal leverage an inverse long therefore reaches its maintenance threshold earlier than a linear long, and an inverse short later.

Academic definition

A derivative contract with notional denominated in the quote currency but margined and settled in the base asset, yielding a payoff function linear in the reciprocal of the price and hence non-linear in the price itself.

Mechanism

Fixing notional in dollars while collateral is the coin makes the two move against each other. A falling price means both a losing position and shrinking collateral in dollar terms, so the loss and the cushion compound. The reciprocal payoff is not an exchange's design choice so much as the arithmetic of denominating a dollar-sized promise in an asset whose dollar price is what you are betting on.

In practice

Who uses it
Traders holding the base asset as treasury, coin-denominated miners and funds, and desks hedging a coin balance without converting it to cash.
Why it matters
It lets someone who thinks and reports in the coin margin and settle in it too, which removes the need to hold quote-currency collateral.
Limitations
The non-linearity works against a long twice over, in the payoff and in the collateral, and the same stated leverage is more dangerous here than on a linear contract.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the stated leverage means the same thing as on a linear contract.
  • Reading coin-denominated profit as if the coin's own price were fixed.
  • Hedging a dollar exposure with a coin-margined contract and ignoring the mismatch.

Examples

crypto

A holder who keeps everything in the coin shorts an inverse contract against part of the balance. No dollars are needed at any stage: margin, funding and settlement all move in the coin, and the hedge is reported in the same unit as the treasury.

numeric

At the same stated ten times leverage, an inverse long entered at 50,000 fails its maintenance test near 45,682, a fall of 8.6%, while a linear long fails near 45,226, a fall of 9.5%. Identical leverage, a shorter rope.

Given
  • Q = 10,000 of quote-currency notional
  • P_entry = 50,000
  • Leverage 10x, so inverse initial margin = Q / (10 x P_entry) = 0.02 coin
  • Maintenance rate = 0.5% of notional
  • Fees and funding ignored
Steps
  1. Inverse coin equity at price P: 0.02 + 10,000(1/50,000 - 1/P) = 0.22 - 10,000/P
  2. Maintenance in coin = 0.005 x 10,000 / P = 50/P
  3. Set equal: 0.22 = 10,050/P, so P = 45,681.8 (a 8.64% fall)
  4. Linear equivalent: P* = 50,000 x (1 - 0.10) / (1 - 0.005) = 45,226.1 (a 9.55% fall)
The inverse long fails at about 45,682 against 45,226 for the linear long: roughly one percentage point less room

The asymmetry reverses for a short, which gets more room on an inverse contract than on a linear one. Both figures assume a flat maintenance rate.

Mathematical foundation

Procedure

Take the reciprocal of the entry price and of the exit price, subtract the second from the first, and multiply by the quote-currency notional. The result is in units of the base asset. Reverse the sign for a short. To express it in quote currency, multiply by the exit price.

Worked example

Given
  • Q = 10,000 of quote-currency notional, long
  • P_entry = 50,000
  • Case A: P_exit = 55,000 (+10%)
  • Case B: P_exit = 45,000 (-10%)
Steps
  1. A: 10,000 x (1/50,000 - 1/55,000) = 10,000 x 0.0000018182 = +0.018182 coin
  2. B: 10,000 x (1/50,000 - 1/45,000) = 10,000 x -0.0000022222 = -0.022222 coin
  3. Ratio of coin loss to coin gain = 0.022222 / 0.018182 = 1.222
A 10% rise earns 0.018182 coin; a 10% fall costs 0.022222 coin, about 22% more in coin terms. A linear contract of the same notional would gain and lose 1,000 of quote currency symmetrically.

Interpretation

The asymmetry is in the unit of account, not in the market. Measured in quote currency at the exit price the two contracts agree, but the inverse holder's wealth, margin and reporting are all in the base asset, and in that unit the downside is larger and arrives sooner.

Assumptions

  • Notional Q is fixed in quote currency per contract, and margin, profit, loss and settlement are all in the base asset.
  • Sign convention stated for a long; a short reverses it. Fees, funding payments and any venue rounding are excluded.
  • The maintenance comparison in the example assumes a flat maintenance rate and a single position; tiered rates and cross margin change the thresholds.
Limits of the formula
  • The expression gives realised coin profit between two prices. It is not a valuation of the position between those points, which also depends on the venue's mark.
  • Contract specifications, notional units and margin treatment are venue conventions in crypto markets rather than standards, so the arithmetic must be checked against the specific product.
  • A coin-margined short has a coin loss bounded by its notional divided by the entry price, but the base asset in which that bound is expressed is itself appreciating in the scenario that produces the loss.

Commonly confused with

Contract Size

On a linear contract, notional equals contract size times price, so money and exposure scale together. On an inverse contract notional is a fixed amount of quote currency and the coin amount at risk moves with the reciprocal of price, so the same sizing arithmetic does not apply.
